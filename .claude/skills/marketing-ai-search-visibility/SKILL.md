# AI Search Visibility

## Read AI visibility as a four-rung ladder — retrieved, cited, mentioned, recommended — and report the rung, never a single "AI visibility" number, because each rung is earned by a different system.

**Why it works:** Retrieved means a model read your page while composing an answer. Cited means your page is listed as a source. Mentioned means your brand name appears in the answer text. Recommended means your product is on the shortlist the buyer actually considers. The first two are governed by your own content — crawlability, structure, clarity, freshness — and are the rungs you can move by editing pages. Recommendation is governed by aggregate web consensus: reviews, forums, analysts, press, video. Collapsing them into one score hides the only diagnosis that matters, because a rising citation count against a flat recommendation rate is a specific, nameable gap — the web does not yet corroborate what your content says — and it calls for completely different work than a low citation count does. There is also a shadow rung: on requirements-heavy prompts models increasingly name products to *avoid*, with sources, so weak third-party consensus can produce an explicit rule-out rather than mere absence. That makes the framing around a mention — favourable, neutral, hedged, recommended-against — part of the measurement rather than a nicety.

**Evidence offered:** A synthesis rather than a study: the ladder itself is a framework, supported by Lily Ray's (Amsive) 2026 analysis of 100 B2B "best [category] software" queries and behavioural work from Scrunch and SimilarWeb. The rungs are not independently validated as discrete states and the B2B software setting is far from a $50 body-care order. Treat the ladder as a diagnostic vocabulary that earns its place by separating two causes, not as a measured model.

**Fit here (9/10):** This is the single most clarifying idea in the source material for RSC, because it explains a result already sitting in the data. On-site GEO here is genuinely good — answer-first rewriting, `citation-finder`, a curated `/llms.txt` served through `templates/llms.txt.liquid`, breadcrumb schema — and the measured mention rate is still ~2% (4 of 180 prompt×engine cells), almost entirely from Google AI Overviews, with roughly 0% on standalone ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Claude. Under one number that reads as failure of the on-site work and invites more of it. Under the ladder it reads correctly: the citation rungs are being earned and the recommendation rung is not, because RSC has thin third-party corroboration in a category dominated by national incumbents. `agents/pr-target-finder` already exists and targets exactly that gap — the ladder is what makes it obvious that the PR agent, not another on-site pass, is the instrument. Costs nothing to adopt: it is a change to how `data/reports/ai-citations/` is read and reported. Held at 9 rather than 10 only because the framework's supporting studies are all B2B software.

## Before commissioning any AI-visibility work, split the complaint into one of three causes — technical (we cannot be crawled or parsed), comprehension (we are read but described inaccurately or vaguely), or trust (we are understood and simply not selected) — because each has a different fix and only one of them is cheap.

**Why it works:** "We do not show up in AI answers" is a symptom with three unrelated causes, and the industry default is to assume the technical one because it is the only one with a checklist. Technical is a robots.txt, rendering and structure problem, fixable in an afternoon. Comprehension is a content problem — the model can read you but cannot state what you are. Trust is a consensus problem and is the expensive one, because it is bought with reviews, communities and earned media over quarters. Diagnosing in that order stops the common failure: spending repeatedly on structural fixes for a problem that was never structural, which feels productive because each fix is verifiable and none of them moves the number.

**Evidence offered:** Framing, not measurement — offered as audit doctrine with no study behind the three-way split itself. Its value is that it is falsifiable per branch: each cause can be independently ruled in or out with a cheap check, which is more than most AEO advice offers.

**Fit here (9/10):** Immediately actionable and it *resolves* on this site rather than staying theoretical. The technical branch was ruled out by live verification on 2026-09-05: `robots.txt` allows the search-time crawlers (`OAI-SearchBot`, `ClaudeBot`, `PerplexityBot`, `Google-Extended`, `ChatGPT-User`) and blocks only the training-only ones (`GPTBot`, `CCBot`), which OpenAI's own bot documentation confirms is the correct split — "Disallowing GPTBot indicates a site's content should not be used in training generative AI foundation models", while search opt-out is governed by `OAI-SearchBot`, which is allowed. `/llms.txt` returns 200 with 52 KB of curated content. So the ~0% standalone-ChatGPT result is **not** a crawler-access problem, and any future proposal to fix it with more markup should be met with this paragraph. That leaves comprehension and trust: the comprehension branch has a cheap, bounded fix (the self-contained answer blocks below), and the Catalog evidence recorded in the Falsified section points hard at trust as the binding one. Held at 9 because the split is asserted rather than demonstrated; it earns the score by having already changed what work is worth doing here.

*Source: Corey Haines — "marketingskills / ai-seo" (github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills, references/format-volatility.md)*

## Apply one test before funding another on-site AI-visibility push: if a model ignored everything on our own domain, would the rest of the web still put us on the shortlist? If not, that gap is the priority and no amount of on-site work closes it.

**Why it works:** Recommendation is a consensus signal assembled from sources you do not control — review platforms, forums, press, video, analyst-equivalent roundups. Your own domain contributes to citation and to how the category gets described, but a model deciding what to *recommend* is largely reading everyone else. So the on-site work has a ceiling, and a brand that keeps pushing against that ceiling gets better and better at being quoted while never appearing on the list. The test is useful precisely because it is uncomfortable to answer honestly, and the answer routes the budget.

**Evidence offered:** Asserted as a decision heuristic, resting on the same Amsive/Scrunch/SimilarWeb material as the ladder. No controlled comparison of on-site versus off-site spend exists; the claim that recommendation is "largely independent" of your own content is directionally supported by the listicle finding below but not quantified.

**Fit here (9/10):** Answers itself here, and the answer is no. RSC has 389 Judge.me reviews at 4.68★ on its own storefront — real proof, but on a surface a model weighs as first-party — against national incumbents with third-party review corpora, and the coconut soap and toothpaste carry 58 and 33 reviews respectively in categories where the competition is measured in thousands. That is the gap, stated plainly, and it is the same conclusion `agents/pr-target-finder` was built on independently. The practical consequence is a budget rule that fits the Prime Directive: when a proposal is "more AI-optimised blog content", it is answering a question the site has already answered well; when it is "get corroborated somewhere we do not own", it is attacking the binding constraint. Held at 9 because off-site consensus is genuinely slow and expensive for a one-person operation at ~$2,700/mo — the test tells you what to do, not that you can afford to do much of it this quarter.

*Source: Corey Haines — "marketingskills / ai-seo" (github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills, references/citations-vs-recommendations.md)*

## When the off-site test comes back "no", the only lever is placement on domains you do not own — review sites, external comparison pages, category roundups — and at this size the affordable version is seeding product to 15-25 relevant publishers for honest inclusion in roundups they already publish.

**Why it works:** Answer engines look for corroboration from sources the brand does not control before they will recommend it, so the same sentence carries different weight depending on whose domain it sits on. "Best natural deodorant for sensitive skin" written by you is a claim; written into an existing roundup by a reviewer with their own audience it is evidence. That is why the trust rung is the one on-site work cannot touch, and why the fix is an outreach motion rather than a content motion. The scaled-down version is not press: it is identifying the publishers who already rank for the category questions, offering free product, and accepting an honest verdict — which is the same seeding mechanic used for pre-launch validation, pointed at publishers instead of buyers.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, plus a screenshot claim that Reddit and third-party sources are the top sources Google pulls from for category and comparison questions. No numbers, no named accounts, no before/after. It agrees with the Amsive/Scrunch/SimilarWeb material already cited above, which is corroboration by agreement rather than independent measurement — treat the direction as well supported and the magnitude as unknown.

**Fit here (6/10):** The only tactic in this file that attacks the gap the off-site test names rather than describing it. Runnable today by the solo operator with no media budget: 15-25 natural-deodorant and clean-beauty bloggers and reviewers, free product, honest inclusion in their existing roundups. `agents/pr-target-finder` already ranks the targets, so this is the fulfilment motion for a list that exists — the missing half was always "and then what". It also earns its keep even if AI citation never arrives, because a third-party verdict is quotable proof on the PDP, and the 389 Judge.me reviews currently sitting on our own storefront are weighed as first-party by exactly the systems we are trying to influence. Not scored higher because retention rather than discovery is the binding constraint on revenue at ~$2,700/mo, and because a placement cycle runs in months against a baseline mention rate measured at n=1 per cell — so fix the sampling before claiming the seeding moved anything.

*Source: Jackson Blackledge (@blvckledge) — "How to get AI Overview & AI Mode to recommend your products | A Complete Guide" (social post)*

## Treat a single AI answer as an anecdote, never a measurement: run each tracked query 3-5 times per platform in a fresh session, record the mention RATE with its sample size ("cited 3/5, n=5"), and compare rates across months rather than runs.

**Why it works:** AI answers are non-deterministic — the same prompt returns different sources run to run, so a yes/no from one run carries a large and unknown error bar. That has two consequences. A drop from 4/5 to 3/5 is noise and must not trigger work; a drop from 4/5 to 0/5 sustained across a month is signal and must. And a headline visibility figure built from single runs is not a low number, it is a number of unknown precision, which is worse: it cannot be trended, cannot be used to judge whether an intervention worked, and quietly invites action either way.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as measurement discipline rather than demonstrated — no published run-to-run variance figures accompany it. But the underlying non-determinism of LLM sampling is not in dispute, and the practice is the standard statistical response to it. The specific 3-5 range is a convention, not a derived sample size.

**Fit here (8/10):** Directly actionable against an agent that already exists, and it lands on a real defect. `agents/ai-citation-tracker` iterates its prompt list exactly once — `for (let i = 0; i < prompts.length; i++)` with no repeat pass — so the ~2% figure this project quotes as its AI-visibility baseline is **n=1 per prompt×engine cell**. It is not necessarily wrong, but it is not yet a measurement, and it is the number `agents/pr-target-finder` ranks its targets from. The fix is bounded and cheap in engineering terms but not free in spend: repeating 75 prompts across the engine set 3-5× multiplies an already paid API cost by the same factor, and the ChatGPT engine in that tracker is currently 429/quota-blocked, so the repeat pass should land on the engines that answer before the one that does not. A defensible middle path is to repeat a stable core subset rather than all 75, and to record `n` on every figure the report emits so a future reader knows what they are holding. Held at 8 rather than 9 because the honest version costs real API budget and the finding it would sharpen — that RSC is barely visible on standalone assistants — is not in serious doubt.

## Do not publish a self-ranked "best [category]" guide expecting it to win the recommendation — for a brand that is not already the consensus pick, your own buyer's guide reliably earns the citation while the answer recommends someone else.

**Why it works:** The model treats your guide as a source about the *category*, not about you. It extracts the competitor names, the comparison criteria and the evaluation framework you assembled, then makes its recommendation from web-wide consensus, where established players dominate. So the research you paid for improves the model's description of your competitors. The category-framing effect is real and is worth something — you influenced how the model defines a good product in your space — but it is positioning work, not shortlist placement, and confusing the two funds the wrong content indefinitely.

**Evidence offered:** The strongest number in the source material. Lily Ray (Amsive) analysed 100 B2B "best [category] software" queries across three dates in spring 2026: self-promotional listicles earned 323 citations in AI Overviews, and in 224 of them — **69%** — the answer left the publishing brand out of the recommendations and pointed at competitors. Caveats worth carrying: B2B software, AI Overviews only, one analyst, three sampling dates, and no control arm of non-self-promotional guides.

**Fit here (9/10):** Corroborated here independently and from a different direction, which is why it scores high despite the B2B setting. RSC's own attribution measured AI **blog** referrals at 336 sessions → 1 transaction (0.30% CVR, worth roughly $2.50/mo) against AI **product** referrals at 144 sessions → 9 transactions (6.25% CVR). AI cites this site's listicles heavily and that traffic converts at approximately nothing. Two unrelated measurements — a B2B citation study and a DTC revenue split — say the same thing: the listicle earns the citation and not the sale. It also converges with the Prime Directive from outside, which is the useful part: "collections and PDPs convert, informational posts mostly do not" and "an emerging brand's own buyer's guide is a vote for its competitors" are the same instruction reached by different roads. Practical rule for the content calendar: a "best natural deodorant" post is defensible as a human-conversion asset if it earns its buy path, and is not defensible on the grounds that it wins AI citations. Held at 9 only because the primary study is B2B software.

*Source: Corey Haines — "marketingskills / ai-seo" (github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills, references/citations-vs-recommendations.md)*

## Choose content formats per engine and re-check them on a schedule — after ChatGPT's August 2026 retrieval change, listicle and comparison citations fell by half and a third while owned "official" pages rose, so a format's AI-citation value is a dated snapshot rather than a property.

**Why it works:** Retrieval behaviour is a product decision at each vendor and changes without warning, usually against whatever format the market has most aggressively exploited. Formats scaled for GEO become the formats a retrieval update demotes, which means a content plan justified by "this format wins AI citations" carries an expiry date nobody wrote down. Building the plan per engine and re-verifying against your own monitoring converts a standing assumption into a checkable one.

**Evidence offered:** Peec AI data on ChatGPT retrieval before and after the 5.6 launch, shared by Tomek Rudzki via Lily Ray, August 2026: listicle citations 15.77% → 7.80% of total (−50.5%), comparison pages 9.08% → 6.17% (−32.1%), with a simultaneous surge in `site:` and "official" fan-out modifiers and a decline in "vs", "best", "top", "reviews". Measured on ChatGPT only — the reference is explicit that "OK" for Gemini and Perplexity means no demotion has been *reported*, not that stability was measured. One vendor's panel, one release, one month.

**Fit here (8/10):** The direction of the shift — toward primary sources and owned domains, away from aggregator formats — points at exactly the pages the Prime Directive already prioritises, so acting on it costs nothing that is not already planned: product pages, collections and the store's own descriptions are the rising citable class, and they are also the pages that convert. It also supplies the standing caution this project needs more than the tactic: every number in this whole skill is a dated snapshot of a system that reprices formats overnight, so none of it may be quoted as a durable fact. Held at 8 because it is one vendor's measurement of one release, and because RSC's AI position is currently governed by the trust rung rather than by format — a format shift matters most to a brand already being retrieved often, which this one is not.

*Source: Corey Haines — "marketingskills / ai-seo" (github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills, references/format-volatility.md)*

## Expect standard analytics to under-report the AI touch — most visits that follow an AI recommendation arrive as branded search or direct, not as a visible AI referral — so read the referral channel as a floor and cross-check it against a self-reported "how did you hear about us" answer and Search Console's generative-AI performance section.

**Why it works:** A buyer who is told about you in a chat does not usually click the citation. They finish the conversation, then search your name or type the URL, and land as branded organic or direct — indistinguishable from an ordinary visitor. So the AI referral channel measures only the fraction that clicked through in-session, and treating that fraction as the channel's size systematically undervalues it. The correction is not a better referrer parser; it is a second, independent instrument that asks the buyer, plus branded-search volume watched as a proxy for influence that leaves no referrer at all. A third instrument exists now: Search Console's generative-AI performance section, which measures the Google half of the channel first-party instead of inferring it — free, minutes to check, no traffic volume required. **It reports IMPRESSIONS ONLY — there is no click data in it** (pages, countries, devices and dates, but no clicks and no prompt text). So it answers "is Google surfacing us in AI answers at all", which is a real and previously unanswerable question, and it does NOT answer "did anyone arrive" — the very gap this whole tactic exists to work around. Read it as a presence detector, never as the traffic number.

**Evidence offered:** SimilarWeb, across thousands of real user journeys over seven days post-answer: a brand ChatGPT recommended received roughly 2.5× more new visitors the following week than competitors left off the list, and only about **9%** of those visits arrived as visible AI referral traffic. Scrunch's opt-in panel separately associated a genuine recommendation with ~2× the brand-evaluation behaviour of a passing mention. Both are observational panel studies compared against a user's own baseline, not controlled experiments, and both are vendor-published. The Search Console section is on much firmer ground than its source: the article mentions it in passing as "in some accounts" (accurate when written on 2026-08-21), but it was verified independently on 2026-09-06 as a shipped Google feature — announced 2026-06-03, UK first, and **rolled out to all sites worldwide by 2026-08-31**, covering AI Overviews, AI Mode and Discover, alongside a control for blocking content from AI features. The same verification is what establishes the impressions-only limit above, which the article does not mention at all.

**Fit here (8/10):** The second instrument already exists here and is already disagreeing with the first, which is what makes this actionable rather than theoretical. RSC's Zigpoll post-purchase survey asks how buyers heard about the store, and AI assistants account for roughly 18% of the open-ended answers ("Claude", "Chatgpt", "Chat") — against an `ai-assistant` referral channel that books a far smaller share of orders. n=17 and therefore directional only, but the direction is the one the study predicts, and it is free evidence already being collected. Two consequences worth adopting: `lib/order-attribution.js`'s `AI_ASSISTANT_HOSTS` channel should be read as a floor rather than a total, and the Zigpoll answer is the corroborating source to quote beside it. The Search Console check is the third and is the stop/continue instrument for everything else in this file — it is the cheapest way to find out whether Google AI surfaces are showing this store at all, which is the question the seeding work above will need an answer to in a quarter. Note the trap this closes: the ChatGPT product-card channel that died on 2026-05-13 was diagnosed as "100% clicks, 0% conversion" on referral data alone, which is right about the clicks and silent about buyers who arrived by name. Held at 8 because both studies are vendor-published panels, the local corroboration is n=17, and Search Console covers Google surfaces only and reports no clicks — so it can confirm we are being surfaced and can never confirm anyone came.

## When a page is worth optimising for citation at all, spend the effort on cited sources, specific statistics and named quotations rather than on keyword density — and never on keyword stuffing, which actively reduces AI visibility rather than merely failing to help it.

**Why it works:** AI systems select passages they can safely repeat, so the properties that make a passage repeatable — an attributable number, a named source, an expert quote with a title — are the properties that get it selected. Keyword repetition is the opposite signal: it makes a passage less quotable and reads as manipulation, so it carries a penalty rather than a null effect. That asymmetry is the useful part, because it inverts the reflex carried over from traditional SEO where stuffing is merely wasted effort.

**Evidence offered:** The Princeton GEO study (KDD 2024), measured across Perplexity.ai, ranking nine optimisation methods by visibility change: cite sources +40%, statistics +37%, quotations +30%, authoritative tone +25%, clarity +20%, and keyword stuffing **−10%**. Lower-ranking sites reportedly benefit more, up to +115% with citations. One engine, one academic study, 2024 — old relative to everything else here, and Perplexity is not where this store's buyers are. The relative ordering is more trustworthy than any individual percentage.

**Fit here (7/10):** Partly implemented already, which is what caps the score rather than the evidence: `agents/citation-finder` exists to add sourced references to article bodies and is on the pipeline, so the top-ranked lever is running. What is not systematically applied is the statistics-and-attribution half, and there is a live constraint the source skill does not know about — this is a cosmetics brand, so any statistic or quotation added to a page passes `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` and `agents/ad-studio/health-claims.js`, and a compelling third-party number about treating a skin condition is exactly the shape those gates exist to refuse. So the usable version here is narrower than the study's: ingredient composition, formulation facts, category context and dated third-party research reported as research, never an efficacy figure attached to the product. Held at 7 because the biggest lever is already built, the study is single-engine and two years old, and the health gate removes the most citable class of statistic in this category by design.

*Source: Corey Haines — "marketingskills / ai-seo" (github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills)*

## Collect the questions buyers actually ask AI about the category, then restructure existing pages so each answer stands alone in its own block — one question, one direct answer, no dependence on the rest of the page.

**Why it works:** Extraction is passage-level, not page-level. An engine lifts the span that answers the query unambiguously on its own; a passage that only makes sense after reading two sections above it loses to a source that states the answer outright, however much better the surrounding page is. So the work is not new content, it is discovery followed by reorganisation: find the real questions, then move the answer to the front of its own block under an ordinary question-shaped heading. The boundary matters — self-contained blocks under normal headings is good structure and is safe; splitting a page into tiny fragments "so AI can understand it" is the scaled-content-abuse pattern named below, and the difference is whether a human still reads the page as one coherent document.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from a year of agency testing, with no numbers, no named accounts and no before/after. The video is days old, so age is not the discount here — the absence of any measurement is. It is consistent with the Princeton clarity lever above and with the format shift toward primary sources, which is the reason to give it any weight at all.

**Fit here (5/10):** Runnable today by one person across 12 SKUs — "does natural deodorant actually work", "why does baking soda sting", "is it safe during pregnancy" are known category questions and giving each a self-contained on-page block costs an afternoon. `answer-first-rewriter` already does this for articles, so the uncovered surface is PDP and collection copy, which the format-volatility section identifies as the rising citable class and which the Prime Directive says converts. Two caps hold the score at 5. First, this is comprehension work, and the off-site test above has already answered that the binding constraint here is the trust rung — so it must not be scheduled in place of the seeding motion, and "we restructured the FAQs" is not a reason to expect the mention rate to move. Second, the most-asked questions in this category are the ones the health gates exist to police: a pregnancy-safety answer routes straight into `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` and `agents/ad-studio/health-claims.js`, so answers must stay on formulation and ingredient facts rather than drift into efficacy or safety claims.

## Audit robots.txt for search-time AI crawler access separately from training crawler access — blocking a search bot makes citation impossible, while blocking a training-only bot costs nothing in visibility.

**Why it works:** The bots are not interchangeable and the vendors document which is which. A search crawler builds the index an assistant retrieves from at answer time; blocking it removes you from that engine's answers entirely, no matter how good the page is. A training crawler feeds model pre-training and has no bearing on whether you can be retrieved and cited today. Conflating them produces one of two errors in opposite directions: an over-broad block that silently makes a whole engine unreachable, or an over-broad allow that concedes training use for no visibility gain. It is a five-minute check, it is binary, and it invalidates every other AI-visibility hypothesis when it fails.

**Evidence offered:** Vendor documentation rather than a study, which for this particular claim is the right kind of evidence — the bot behaviour is a published product contract. Verified directly at OpenAI's bot docs on 2026-09-05: "Sites that are opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers", while "Disallowing GPTBot indicates a site's content should not be used in training generative AI foundation models", and search opt-out is explicitly governed by `OAI-SearchBot` and not by `GPTBot` or `ChatGPT-User`. The other vendors' bots are named in the source skill without equivalent verification here.

**Fit here (6/10):** Already satisfied on this site, and the score reflects that it is a completed audit rather than an available lever. Verified live on 2026-09-05: `robots.txt` allows `OAI-SearchBot`, `ChatGPT-User`, `ClaudeBot`, `PerplexityBot` and `Google-Extended`, and blocks `GPTBot` and `CCBot` — which is precisely the training-blocked, search-allowed split the vendor docs describe, so the current configuration costs nothing in citability and should not be changed on an AI-visibility argument. Recorded here so the next person who proposes "maybe we are blocked" can be answered from the file instead of re-running the check, and so a future theme or platform change that silently rewrites `robots.txt` has a documented prior state to be compared against. Genuinely valuable as a standing audit item on a Shopify store where the platform owns that route; not a growth lever today.

## Do not write separate content "for AI" and do not chunk pages into small fragments for extraction — write for people and organise with ordinary headings, because the fragment-generating pattern is the one Google names as scaled content abuse.

**Why it works:** The two audiences do not actually diverge. Google states its generative features are rooted in core Search ranking and quality systems, so the same content and the same E-E-A-T signals serve both, and no special markup or file is required to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode. Producing an AI-targeted variant of a page therefore buys nothing on Google while creating a near-duplicate that trips a spam policy, and the fragment pattern — breaking content into small pieces so a model can "understand it better" — is called out by name in Google's own guidance. The structural advice that genuinely helps other engines (a direct answer leading each section, tables for comparisons, ordinary question-shaped headings) is simply good organisation and is indistinguishable from writing clearly, which is why it is safe.

**Evidence offered:** Google's own AI-features optimisation guide, quoted directly — "The best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because our generative AI features on Google Search are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems" and "Don't break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it." A platform grading its own homework and describing its intent rather than its behaviour, so it constrains what is *safe* more reliably than what is *effective*. The non-Google engines demonstrably do reward extractable structure, so this is a restraint on producing separate content, not on structuring content well.

**Fit here (7/10):** A spend-avoidance rule rather than an activity, and it protects a real exposure: this fleet generates article HTML unattended, so an instruction to produce AI-targeted variants would be executed at volume, quietly, across a corpus of live indexed pages — the highest-risk write class in the repo. ## Enrich the Merchant Center feed with Google's conversational attributes — above all `question_and_answer`, up to 30 real buyer questions per product — because that is NEW data about the product, not a completeness checkbox, and it is the input Google's AI shopping surfaces read.

**Why it works:** AI Overview, AI Mode and Gemini answer product questions by comparing structured attributes, and a feed that carries only title/price/image forces the model to infer the rest from prose or skip the product. Six optional attributes exist for exactly this: `question_and_answer` (up to 30 Q&A pairs, 1,000 characters each side), `document_link` (spec sheets and manuals Google may pull FAQs from), `related_product` (accessory / substitute / often-bought-with, referenced by internal id or GTIN), `item_group_title` + `variant_option` (cleaner variant grouping), and `popularity_rank`. The Q&A field is the one that matters most, because unlike every other feed attribute it is not a restatement of something Google can already read — it is the buyer's actual question and your actual answer, which is the shape an AI answer needs.

**Evidence offered:** The source is a Google Ads agency owner writing lead-gen content ("my dms are open"), which predicts his pro-Google framing, and he offers no measured result for this tactic at all — no lift, no account, no before/after. **So the attributes were verified independently against Google's own Merchant Center documentation on 2026-09-06 rather than taken on trust**: all six names are correct, `question_and_answer` really does cap at 30 pairs, and submission is via a supplemental data source, the primary feed, or the Merchant API. Nothing anywhere establishes that any of this moves a ranking; treat the attributes as verified-to-exist and unproven-to-work.

The inputs are already collected: 389 Judge.me reviews, the Zigpoll survey, and `agents/demand-miner`'s harvested buyer questions are all sources of real questions, and 12 SKUs is small enough that the article's own advice — a supplemental feed — applies. It is free and needs no ad spend, which matters with paid paused. Held at 6, not higher, for two reasons. **`popularity_rank` is self-asserted and relative to your OWN catalogue**, so it decides which RSC product Google surfaces and can do nothing about being outranked by national incumbents — which is precisely the wall the Shopify Catalog test hit, inherited intact. Do this because it is cheap, novel and plausibly compounding, not because it is expected to fix AI visibility.
