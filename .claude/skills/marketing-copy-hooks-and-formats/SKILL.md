# Copy Hooks and Formats

## A hook must open a loop and leave it unresolved — never open and close it in the same breath. The most common failure, especially from an LLM, is a 'hook' that summarizes the entire ad.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is an itch the reader cannot scratch without continuing. A line that states the setup, the cause and the resolution has already scratched it, so there is no reason to read on. LLMs are trained toward resolution and completion, so their default output is a tidy summary that reads like a hook but functions as an abstract.

**Evidence offered:** A worked negative example he wrote himself ('My 77 year-old mother used to love walking her dog… Here's how a no-pull harness gave her back those precious mornings') dissected against six better alternatives that all stop short of resolution. Practitioner teardown, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable direct-response curiosity mechanics, age irrelevant. This is the construction rule; the curiosity-loop entry further down governs the other end — that the destination actually pays the loop off. Both are needed and neither replaces the other. Free, and applies today to the Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and preheaders on the retention surface, and to PDP hero lines. The named LLM failure mode matters here specifically because a solo operator drafts with an LLM and will otherwise ship the summarizing version: check every drafted opener for whether it has already given away the answer. The same draft pass should check register — see the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, since the LLM's other default is polished brand prose. Not higher because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list the effect is unreadable, so it is a judgement rule applied at draft-review time rather than something testable.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Inject a trigger word or emotionally loaded phrase ('ruining my life', 'quits by noon') into a hook structure that already converts — don't rebuild the structure.

**Why it works:** The loaded phrase raises emotional temperature and stops the scroll, while the underlying structure that already worked is left intact — so you add attention without discarding the converting logic. This is the word-level lever that makes the taboo and negative-framing angles in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` land; it is an edit pass on a proven line, not a new concept.

**Evidence offered:** Claims both hooks shown had hook rates above 45%; agency practice with DTC brands. No breakdown of which brands or over what spend.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class and transfers off-platform at zero cost: email subject lines and preview text (the live surface, since retention is the binding constraint), Amazon bullets, and PDP headlines. Scored mid because its native measurement is hook rate on video ads, which does not exist here — Real Skin Care can only judge it by email opens on a small list. Practical use: take the best-performing existing subject line or bullet and swap one flat noun phrase for a loaded one, keeping everything else fixed. One loaded phrase is the point; a draft where every noun has been upgraded fails the understatement rule below.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open in the middle of the action, with something already happening, rather than setting the scene first.

**Why it works:** A line that drops the reader into an event in progress creates an immediate gap — what is happening, and why — that they have to keep reading to fill. A scene-setting opener asks them to invest attention before anything has happened, which is a worse trade for a scrolling reader.

**Evidence offered:** Six alternative hooks with the operative verbs called out ('was laying', 'I saw', 'was mad/felt sorry', 'she quit', 'she stopped'), contrasted against the summarizing version. Illustrative, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable opening-construction principle, age irrelevant, and additive — this skill covers transformation-first ordering and negative framing, but nothing else owns where in the timeline the first sentence lands. It is the sentence-level companion to the withheld-resolution rule above: mid-action is the easiest way to open a loop without closing it. Free and directly usable on the live Meta primary text, on email subject lines, and on PDP hero copy — 'I kept my arms down through the whole meeting' is a mid-action opening the category actually supports, and the raw material already exists in the pain-based review quotes `marketing-review-mining` tells you to elicit. Capped at 6 because it is a craft rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and because for a body-care brand any mid-action scene must be a real customer moment rather than an invented one.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Put roughly 90% of your copy effort into the concept and the hook — the body is a formulaic outline you can model off an existing ad.

**Why it works:** The body of a direct-response ad follows a repeatable structure (story → pain → discovery → proof → CTA) that is largely interchangeable across brands, so time spent rewriting it produces almost no differentiation. The concept and hook are the only parts that decide whether anyone reads the body at all, so effort concentrated there is the only effort that changes the outcome.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, with the supporting claim that his four-step LLM modeling process produces 'a good ad' 99% of the time in 1–2 minutes plus ~10 minutes of tweaks. No spend, CTR, or conversion figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable effort-allocation principle, age irrelevant. Directly useful now that a $30/day hand-run Meta campaign is live: one operator with finite hours building a 3-2-2 flexible ad needs two meaningfully different primary texts, and this says the two should differ in **concept and hook** while the body structure stays fixed — which is what makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings. The verbalization-transform checklist in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` is how you produce the two headline variants without drifting off the claim. It also transfers to email subject lines and Amazon A+ block leads. Capped at 6 because it is a prioritization rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month and $30/day the payoff of concentrating effort will be judged, never measured.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## In organic-styled social copy, keep the product out of the hook — the discovery should feel almost accidental.

**Why it works:** The format's whole advantage is that it reads as a post rather than an ad. Naming the product in the opening line breaks that frame immediately and re-triggers the reader's ad-avoidance. Withholding it lets the emotional story earn attention first, so the product arrives as the answer to a problem the reader is already invested in.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as the third fault of his bad example hook, with the reasoning that the poster's recommendation should feel incidental. Assertion only.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable positioning-within-a-format principle. Real overlap with the primal-desire rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` ('the product is the mechanism, not the headline'), which is what caps it; the additive part is narrower and format-specific — in an organic-styled Meta ad or a founder-voice caption, naming the deodorant in line one destroys the only advantage the format has. Usable now on the live giveaway campaign's primary text at zero cost. Held at 5 because it does not transfer to the surfaces carrying most revenue: an Amazon title and bullets must name the product immediately, and a PDP hero that hides it is just confusing. Scope it to social copy and do not let it leak onto the listing.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Lead with the transformation — the 'day without pain' after-state — instead of the conventional problem-then-solution order.

**Why it works:** Most advertisers open on the pain, so opening on the after-state is itself a pattern break. It also resonates with buyers who have already tried and failed at other fixes, because it names the outcome they gave up on rather than re-describing a problem they know intimately. This is the ordering variant of the primal-desire angle in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: same insistence that the end state leads, applied to which sentence comes first.

**Evidence offered:** Points to Brex's ad account as the exemplar across multiple formats; otherwise assertion.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable positioning principle, free to apply to PDP hero copy, Amazon bullets, and email subject lines. Real Skin Care's buyer is often someone whose previous natural deodorant failed, so 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' is a legitimate lead. Scored mid rather than high because the existing product-image-stack skill already covers transformation as a visual format — this is the copy-order variant, which is why it lives here rather than in its own skill. Note the tension with negative framing in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: fear-first and after-state-first are competing openings, so pick by audience — negative framing for buyers still unaware of the stakes, transformation-first for buyers already burned by a previous attempt. When you pick transformation-first for a burned buyer, the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` are the body copy that follows the headline. Whichever opening you pick, it still has to withhold the resolution — 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' works; 'how our baking-soda-free formula gave me a day where I stopped checking my shirt' has already closed the loop.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open by getting the prospect to agree — a question, or a rhythmic recital of what they have been putting up with ('you swat, you shoo, you spray, but those flies still don't go away') — before you make any claim.

**Why it works:** Once a prospect has silently acknowledged a current pain, the likelihood of a purchase decision rises sharply, because they have conceded the premise the rest of the pitch rests on. It is the copy equivalent of getting a foot in the door: the reader has agreed to something before they have been asked for anything, and a claim that arrives after a conceded premise is evaluated as an answer rather than as an assertion.

**Evidence offered:** Four Billy Mays opening lines quoted (Awesome Auger, FLIES-Away, Slider Station, Tool Bandit), plus an unsourced appeal to 'advances in Neuromarketing have proven beyond a shadow of a doubt.' No data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Applies to live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines, PDP hero copy. The category has an unusually clean version of the rhythmic form — 'you reapplied, you switched scents, you gave it two weeks, and it still quit by noon' — and the raw material is already in the voice-of-customer file. Capped at 5 because it sits very close to three rules in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: negative framing, the prior-attempt walkthrough, and calling the buyer out by name. The additive part is narrow but real — the interrogative or concessive opener whose only job is to extract a silent yes before any claim is made. It is also the first link in the believability chain the claim-ordering rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` describes. Reconcile it with the hook rules: the yes-opener still has to withhold the resolution and must not close the loop in line one.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Carry one dominant idea through the whole piece and intensify it by re-presenting it in successive fresh settings rather than repeating the same phrasing — and drop any additional perspective that is not different or dramatic enough to renew interest.

**Why it works:** A vague desire has no purchase-forcing power; concrete images give it weight, and the reader leaves with one dominant image no matter how many you present, so each additional legitimate setting reinforces rather than repeats, building emotional weight and making the price feel smaller. Repeating the same phraseology bores a sophisticated reader, so a fresh perspective is the only way to say the same thing twice; a perspective that is not genuinely fresh adds reading cost with no return.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion framed as the opening of the 'thirteen ways to strengthen desire' section, with a stated stopping rule ('as long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire... use it'). No test data, no worked examples.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure/craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Nothing else here governs how a long PDP body, an Amazon A+ sequence or a multi-block email holds one idea across several sections. Clean form for this catalogue: one promise — you stop checking your shirt — carried through four distinct settings (the 3pm meeting, the twelve-hour shift, the gym bag on Friday, the hug at the door), each drawn verbatim from the review corpus rather than invented. The stopping rule is the load-bearing half for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which will happily generate ten near-identical restatements. The price-salience argument is directly relevant to a $12 stick sitting next to Native. Held at 6 because it overlaps the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and transformation-first ordering above, it must be bounded by the three-job length test in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, and it will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Insert explicit momentum phrases into transition sentences — 'let me explain', 'here is what you'll find', 'all I ask from you is this', 'for example —' — so interest never flags between sections.

**Why it works:** Transitions are where a reader's attention has the cleanest place to stop, because one idea has closed and the next has not opened. A short invitation to continue removes that exit by carrying the reader across the seam, and because it costs a few words it does not slow the piece down.

**Evidence offered:** A list of four example phrases with the framing that they are 'invitations to read on'. Assertion only.

**Fit here (4/10):** Durable-principle class (long-copy craft), age irrelevant and free, but the ceiling is low. Half the source's momentum material is already owned by the open-loop hook rule above and the curiosity-loop rule below, so only the transition-phrase half is additive, and it is a device for long continuous prose. The revenue-carrying surfaces are scannable — Amazon bullets and A+ blocks read in fragments, the PDP ordered by decision-relevance. The one honest home is a long founder-voice email or a transition-period education send. Adopted because it is a distinct mechanism nothing else owns, but scored 4 because its application is narrow and unmeasurable at a sub-1,000 subscriber list.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Use the 'this one thing' hook — promise that a single change or product resolves the problem.

**Why it works:** The world is complex and the brain craves simplicity, so a one-thing promise offers clarity and relief; even partial delivery is enough to earn the attention.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus psychological reasoning; explicitly acknowledges the cognitive dissonance in the claim. No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable retention/persuasion psychology, age irrelevant, and free to test on email subject lines and PDP headlines. Fits a 12-SKU catalog where the honest version is real ('the one switch that fixed my transition period', 'one bar replaces three products'). Capped at 6 because in a body-care category the simplicity promise is easy to push into unsupportable claim territory — pair it with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` so the 'one thing' always has a defensible referent (a named SKU, a named swap, a countable replacement) rather than an implied cure. Note that the one-thing framing names *that* there is a single fix without naming *what* it is — keep it that way, or you have closed the loop in the hook.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open a curiosity loop — show the setup, hide the payoff behind the click — and make sure the destination actually satisfies it.

**Why it works:** An unresolved gap compels the click; failing to close the loop on the destination page breaks trust and kills the conversion you just bought.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with the explicit caveat about closing the loop on the landing page.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle but its native home is ad-to-landing-page, which is gated behind the traffic stage. It transfers usefully to email subject lines and preview text, which is a live surface given retention is the binding constraint — hence a middling rather than high score. This entry owns the *closure* half of the loop: whatever the opener withheld, the destination has to deliver. The construction half — how to write an opener that withholds in the first place, and the LLM summarizing failure mode — is the hook rule near the top of this skill.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Escape the hard-sell stereotype with understatement: simplicity, few colour words, fewer adjectives than the reader expects, no superlatives, and short sentences that fall rather than rise in tone.

**Why it works:** The reader has a trained ear for sales language, and superlatives and stacked adjectives trip it, causing the whole piece to be discounted. Copy pitched below the expected intensity reads as reporting rather than selling, so the claims inside it are evaluated on their content instead of being pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, given as a list of stylistic constraints. No examples, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy register), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial, and a better fit than the source could have known: a body-care catalogue is already gated by the specificity and claim rules, and 'no superlatives, fewer adjectives' is the cheapest mechanical way to keep an LLM draft from producing unsupportable efficacy language — the failure mode is compliance as well as tone. Pairs directly with the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, and it is the filter every verbalization transform's output has to pass. Additive next to the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which governs conversational tone but says nothing about intensity — a draft can be chatty and still stacked with superlatives. Applies to PDP body copy, Amazon bullets, post-purchase emails and live Meta primary text. Held at 6 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review, never measured at ~54 orders/month. Scope limit: the Amazon title still needs its keyword and claim terms stated plainly.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Close with an unhedged imperative — 'order now', 'enter now' — repeated through the back half of the piece rather than softened into 'you can learn more here'.

**Why it works:** Once the pain has been surfaced, the product explained, the alternatives contrasted and the risk reversed, the only step left is telling the reader exactly what to do. A hedged ask leaves a persuaded reader with no instruction to act on and lets the momentum you just built dissipate; the direct form also signals that the seller believes in the product enough to ask for the order plainly.

**Evidence offered:** Two phrases pulled from Billy Mays' pitches plus the Zig Ziglar line 'timid salespeople have skinny kids'. Assertion only, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Narrowly additive: `marketing-email-design-production` owns CTA placement and bridging the ask to what was just read, and this skill owns register and pronouns, but nothing owned the ask being an *unhedged imperative repeated after the argument lands* — which is the exact failure mode of a solo operator drafting with an LLM, since brand prose defaults to 'you can learn more here'. Applies to the Shopify PDP CTA, the post-purchase and reorder emails where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign ('enter now' rather than 'buy now'). Capped at 5 because it is a one-line craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it is close kin to CTA rules already recorded, it must be scoped away from Amazon A+ content where cart-directing language is policy-constrained, and at ~54 orders/month no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better. The risk-reversal half of the source's pairing is **not** additive — the guarantee rule in `marketing-offer-construction` and the urgency rules in `marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing` already cover it, and bare 'order now' pressure with no expiring thing behind it is the empty version this catalogue has already ruled out.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Borrow conviction by camouflaging the piece as the medium it sits in — study the channels people already trust, adopt their tone, feel, style and format, and make the piece blend in rather than announce itself as advertising.

**Why it works:** Trust attaches to a publication and bleeds from its editorial pages onto its advertising pages, and phraseology itself acquires an aura of truth independent of the material it carries. Matching the native format means the reader processes the piece with the posture they bring to content rather than the ad-avoidance posture they bring to advertising.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with print-specific devices listed — issue date, city of origin, a by-line — plus the general instruction to study trusted channels and adopt their tone and sincerity. No performance data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection and trust transfer); the newspaper devices drop away but the transferable form is live: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign written as an organic post, and plain-text education and reorder emails, whose format-by-job rule exists precisely because designed image-led mail gets classified as promotional. Free, solo-executable, and the founder-voice register is more credible here than it would be for Native. Capped at 5 because it overlaps the long-form native-style ad entry directly below, the spoken peer register in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, and the promo-versus-education email split in `marketing-email-design-production` — the additive part is only that format itself is a persuasion lever — and because of a hard modern limit the source never anticipated: **an ad may adopt a native register but may not be dressed as editorial or a third-party news item.**

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Run a long-form native-style ad: an organic-looking image with no text overlay, paired with a long first-person emotional story that ends in the accidental discovery of the product.

**Why it works:** The untreated image and story-led caption make the ad read as an organic post rather than a placement, so it earns reading time a designed ad never gets. The long story builds emotional investment before any claim is made, and the product then lands as a resolution rather than a pitch.

**Evidence offered:** The format is described in detail and illustrated with a fully sketched no-pull-harness example (elderly mother, lost agency, discovery, resolution). Assertion and worked illustration; no spend, CTR, or ROAS data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Platform-mechanics class for the format specifics (Meta caption behaviour, no-text organic image), and undated — treat the format detail with suspicion even though the underlying story-lead principle is durable. The reason to adopt at all is that it is one of the very few ad formats a solo operator with no designer and no video can actually produce: a plain phone photo plus written text, which makes it a legitimate second primary text inside the 3-2-2 flexible ad now running at $30/day. Scale-down stated: 300–800 words in the founder's own voice, not 3,000, and **the story must be true** — either the founder's reason for formulating baking-soda-free, or a real customer's transition-period account sourced through `marketing-review-mining`. A fabricated emotional testimonial in body care is an FTC problem, not a creative choice. This is absolute for this catalogue, and deliberately stricter than the law: disclosed dramatization is legal in general, but nothing in this repo ENFORCES a disclosure — `claims.js` demands a source only for claims flagged `factual`, and an invented narrative asserts no fact, so it would pass both gates unchallenged. The rule is the enforcement. If you want borrowed drama, use the reaction/plan format below, which fabricates nothing. Pair with the mid-action opening, the product-out-of-the-hook rule, the camouflage rule directly above and the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which are the craft rules this format depends on. Held at 5 because the evidence is entirely illustrative, and long-form emotional copy is a poor fit for a $12 consumable giveaway entry compared with a direct problem-aware angle.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Get the same borrowed drama with zero fabrication by making a reaction or plan piece off someone else's real story — 'if I had four weeks to make my skin look smoother and less wrinkled, here's exactly what I'd do' — instead of inventing a customer narrative.

**Why it works:** The borrowed story supplies the attention and the stakes; the 'here's exactly what I'd do' framing supplies the education and the product placement. Because you are reacting to someone else's real situation rather than claiming it as your own customer's, nothing has to be invented, and the hypothetical framing keeps the claim to a plan you can actually stand behind rather than a result you would have to substantiate.

**Evidence offered:** Two illustrative examples (a high-ticket fitness coach reacting to the bodybuilding punishment video; a skincare brand's expert or creator doing a four-week plan). Assertion only, no ads run, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection), age irrelevant, and the honest version here is close to something already known to work: 'if I had two weeks to get off aluminium without stinking at work, here's exactly what I'd do' is the transition-period plan the business already needs to publish, which is the single biggest driver of the 18–22.5% repeat rate. It is also the safest of the story-borrowing formats because nothing is fabricated and the hypothetical framing avoids an efficacy claim. Two real caps. First, it substantially overlaps the 'how to start' stem already recorded in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` and the published-results-timeline artifact in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` — the additive part is only that the piece can be pegged to a third party's real story as its hook. Second, the source's native execution is an on-camera reaction, and `marketing-paid-creative-testing`'s static-first rule plus the absence of a videographer, editor or creator roster means the runnable version here is written primary text or an email, not a clip. Adopted at 5 with that scale-down stated.
