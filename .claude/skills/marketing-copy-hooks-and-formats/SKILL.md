# Copy Hooks and Formats

## A hook must open a loop and leave it unresolved — but the loop has to be opened on a named topic. Never open and close it in the same breath, and never open with context-free suspense.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is an itch the reader cannot scratch without continuing. A line that states the setup, the cause and the resolution has already scratched it, so there is no reason to read on. LLMs are trained toward resolution and completion, so their default output is a tidy summary that reads like a hook but functions as an abstract. The opposite error is just as fatal: a suspense line with no stated subject ('this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen') gives the reader nothing to evaluate against their own interests, so they cannot decide to stay. Where context-free suspense appears to work, the retention is actually coming from the creator's face, emotion or on-screen text — the words are contributing nothing, which makes the tactic unreproducible for anyone without that pull. So the thing withheld is the *resolution*, never the *subject*.

**Evidence offered:** A worked negative example he wrote himself ('My 77 year-old mother used to love walking her dog… Here's how a no-pull harness gave her back those precious mornings') dissected against six better alternatives that all stop short of resolution. Practitioner teardown, no data. For the named-topic constraint: a named bad example plus the claim that context-free hooks 'work way less often', with the attribution of TikTok's vague-hook success to face, emotion and text overlay offered as assertion only.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable direct-response curiosity mechanics, age irrelevant. This is the construction rule; the two-part hook spec directly below is the pass/fail test it serves (the withheld resolution keeps the curiosity half alive, the named topic *is* the clarity half), the curiosity-loop entry further down governs the other end — that the destination actually pays the loop off — and the lists/steps/stories rule below supplies the scaffolds that keep the loop open through the body rather than only in line one. All are needed and none replaces the others. Free, and applies today to the Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and preheaders on the retention surface, to PDP hero lines, and to the spoken and on-screen openings of the organic Reels/TikTok the founder films himself. The named-topic constraint bites hardest there, because a solo founder has no celebrity pull to carry a vague opener: 'the natural deodorant rule nobody tells you about week two' names the topic and withholds the answer; 'this is the craziest thing I've learned about deodorant' names nothing and is the version to kill. The named LLM failure mode matters here specifically because a solo operator drafts with an LLM and will otherwise ship the summarizing version: check every drafted opener for whether it has already given away the answer. That check is one item on the larger LLM draft audit below, whose other item is the golden thread — the same next-token bias that closes the loop in line one also lets the hook's premise swallow the body. The same draft pass should check register — see the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, since the LLM's other default is polished brand prose. Not higher because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list the effect is unreadable, so it is a judgement rule applied at draft-review time rather than something testable.

## A hook has exactly one job — get the reader or viewer to opt in — and it does that by delivering exactly two things: topic clarity (they know precisely what this is about) and on-target curiosity (they believe it is for them and want what comes next). Everything else in the hook is decoration.

**Why it works:** The continue-or-scroll decision is made in the first seconds, and it can only go your way if both halves are present: the reader has to know what the content is about *and* that it is relevant to them. A line that supplies only one leaves the decision unmade, and the default outcome of an unmade decision is scrolling. Reducing the hook to two named deliverables also turns it from a matter of taste into a pass/fail check, which is what a solo operator with no second reader actually needs.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from having 'studied literally thousands of videos'; creator cites a million followers and billions of views. No data shown.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle class (attention and retention psychology), so the source's recency is doing none of the scoring work. Organic short-form video is a live surface the operator scripts, films and publishes himself with a phone, so a two-item spec on the line he writes and speaks is runnable today at zero cost; the written form re-runs on email subject lines and preheaders and on the first line of Meta primary text for the $30/day giveaway campaign. This is the spec most of the hook rules in this skill exist to serve: the withheld-resolution and named-topic rule above is the constraint that keeps both halves alive, speed-to-value below governs where the clarity half lands, the contrast rule below is the standard engine for the curiosity half, the seven interest components above stack under curiosity, and second-person framing below is what makes the curiosity *on-target* rather than merely interesting. On video the spec has to be satisfied twice, once by the spoken line and once by the on-screen text, per the two-deliverables rule below. Clarity is the half a founder skips, because he already knows what the piece is about — so the working test is to cover everything but the hook and ask whether a stranger could say what this is about and who it is for. Held at 8 because it is a specification rather than a revenue mechanism, and because at ~54 orders/month only organic short-form gives any per-piece signal to check it against; everywhere else it is applied at draft review. Note scope: the spoken version applies to organic video, since the paid side here stays static-first per `marketing-paid-creative-testing`.

## When a piece underperforms, diagnose the hook against four named failure modes in order — delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest — instead of guessing at the whole piece.

**Why it works:** Each failure mode blocks a different half of the opt-in decision and has a different fix. Delay and confusion break clarity: the topic arrived too late, or arrived in language the reader could not absorb. Irrelevance and disinterest break curiosity: they understood it and decided it was not for them, or it was for them and they did not want what came next. Naming which one is broken stops you from rewriting the wrong thing — adding suspense to a hook whose actual problem is that the topic shows up three lines in.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, presented as a taxonomy derived from studying thousands of videos.

**Fit here (8/10):** A cheap written debug checklist for content the operator already produces, with zero spend and no dependency on order volume, and durable enough that the source date is moot. It complements the 'run down the production stack before retiring an angle' discipline in `marketing-paid-creative-testing` by giving the hook link four specific, checkable defects rather than one vague one. Every fix is already in this skill: delay → the speed-to-value rule directly below; confusion → the sixth-grade reading level rule, the LLM clarity prompt, the isolation read and the 'not sure what it means' kill-test below; irrelevance → second-person framing below, plus checking the awareness level per `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`; disinterest → the contrast rule below, the seven interest components above, the power-phrase cloud and the trigger-word edit. One honest limit at this scale: only organic short-form returns enough per-piece retention signal to diagnose against, so on email and Meta the taxonomy is applied at draft review against opens and thumbstop rather than as a post-mortem on real data.

## Fix 'delay' by cutting the fluff lines and moving the topic-introducing sentence to the very top, so the reader knows the subject within the first one to two seconds.

**Why it works:** Short-form retention is exponential decay with the steepest drop in the first two seconds. Every second the reader spends without the context needed to decide, a large share of them are already gone — so the topic sentence has to arrive before the cliff, not after two lines of setup. The fix is almost always subtractive: the topic sentence is usually already written, sitting third.

**Evidence offered:** The retention decay curve is described but not shown; illustrated with a 13-line script where line three is the first mention of the topic. Assertion plus example.

**Fit here (8/10):** Concrete script surgery — reorder the lines, delete the first two — that one person applies to every video he already films, at no cost and with no dependency on volume. Durable principle about attention, so no decay. It transfers directly to the first line of Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, to email subject lines and preview text, and to PDP hero copy. Concrete form: a deodorant video that opens 'so a few years ago I got really into reading ingredient labels…' should open 'your natural deodorant probably quit on you around day four'. Two boundaries. Moving the *topic* up is not moving the *resolution* up — the named-topic rule above wants the subject stated and the answer withheld, and speed-to-value is the commonest excuse an LLM uses to summarise the whole ad in line one. And this is distinct from the mid-action opening below, which decides what kind of sentence line one is; speed-to-value decides only that the topic-bearing sentence occupies that position. It pairs with the two-to-three-line budget below: topic in line one, contrast in lines two and three.

## Fix 'confusion' by writing the hook at roughly a sixth-grade reading level — fewer and simpler words, and active rather than passive voice ('the dog jumped', not 'the jump of the dog').

**Why it works:** A reader who only absorbs half the words cannot assess whether the piece is for them, so a comprehension failure blocks the opt-in even when the topic is technically stated in line one. Raising the share of words that land raises the share of readers who can make the decision at all — clarity is a prerequisite for relevance, not a stylistic preference.

**Evidence offered:** A deliberately mangled example hook rewritten into a clear one, side by side. Assertion plus demonstration.

**Fit here (7/10):** Copy craft the operator applies himself, first to the spoken and on-screen hooks of his own videos and then to Amazon bullets, PDP headlines and email subject lines. Not covered by the understatement rule below, which governs intensity and adjective restraint and says nothing about readability or voice — a hook can be perfectly restrained and still unparseable. This category is where it bites: 'aluminium-free', 'magnesium hydroxide', 'the transition period' are the exact vocabulary a founder reaches for in line one, and they belong in the body. Keep the hook at 'the burning stopped by day four' and let the chemistry arrive in the mechanism beat of the seven-beat sequence below. It binds hardest on the on-screen text hook, which is read in under a second. Held at 7 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review and unmeasurable at ~54 orders/month.

## Run a written hook through an LLM with a specific clarity prompt rather than editing it by feel: 'I've written a hook for a short form video about X; keep the meaning identical but rewrite it at a sixth grade reading level so the viewer cannot misunderstand it.'

**Why it works:** The rewrite task is mechanical — simplify vocabulary and sentence order while holding the meaning fixed — which is exactly what a language model does reliably, so it returns several clearer framings of the same idea in seconds. Because the meaning is pinned by the prompt, the model is not being asked to invent anything, which is where its output usually goes wrong.

**Evidence offered:** The prompt is given verbatim; no before/after performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** A named, copy-pasteable prompt one person can run on every hook he writes for his own videos, and equally on the first line of Meta primary text and on email subject lines. Zero cost, no people, no volume requirement, and it is a generic LLM instruction rather than a platform feature, so the current-era tool names do not decay it. It is also the one LLM copy task this skill trusts, and worth understanding why: meaning is held fixed and only vocabulary and word order move, so the next-token bias that makes the model summarise an ad in line one or re-theme a body around the hook has nothing to grip. That trust does not extend to the output being shippable — the rewrite still has to pass the withheld-resolution and named-topic check above, the three kill-tests below, the understatement filter below, and the health gate. Add it as a step on the LLM draft audit directly below rather than treating it as a separate ritual.

## Before shipping, read the hook alone, out of the script, and ask how many ways it could be interpreted — if more than one, rewrite until only the intended reading survives.

**Why it works:** The writer reads the hook with the rest of the piece already in his head, so ambiguity is invisible to him. Isolating the one or two sentences and actively hunting for alternative readings surfaces what a cold reader might take from it, and every unintended reading is a chance they decide the piece is not for them.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, offered as a 'clarity-proofing' tip.

**Fit here (7/10):** A real QA pass rather than motivational framing: specific input (the hook alone), specific test (count the interpretations), specific action (rewrite to one). Runnable today by one person on his own video scripts, and identical in form to the imagery QA passes in `marketing-ai-product-imagery` and the LLM draft audit directly below — add it there as an item rather than running it separately. Concrete failures this catches here: 'I stopped using deodorant' reads as either 'I switched to a natural one' or 'I now use nothing', and the second reading loses the buyer; 'this fixed my armpits' reads as a skin claim. That second case is why the pass earns its place beyond craft — a hook read in isolation can sound like a disease claim even when the body is plainly cosmetic, which is exactly what `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks, so run the isolation read before the gate does it for you. This is the back-end check on a finished hook; the three kill-tests directly below are the front-end filter that stops most ambiguous candidates ever reaching it.

## Write hooks by pulling up your own past hooks sorted by performance, adapting the strongest phrasing to today's topic across several variants, then deleting each variant against three explicit tests — too corny, too long-winded, or 'I like the sound of it but I'm not sure what it means'.

**Why it works:** Generating variants off phrasing that already earned attention removes the blank page, and a named kill-test turns the choice between them from inspiration into a repeatable filter. The third test is the load-bearing one: it catches the specific self-deception where the writer likes the rhythm of his own line and therefore cannot see that it names no topic — the same defect the two-part hook spec calls a clarity failure and the four failure modes call confusion.

**Evidence offered:** Live walkthrough of the creator writing and killing four or five variants of one hook out loud.

**Fit here (7/10):** One person, a list of his own past hooks and a blank page — runnable this afternoon on a deodorant Reel, and the same three tests apply to Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and to Amazon bullet leads. Distinct from the isolation read above without restating it: that pass interrogates one finished hook for ambiguity, this one produces several candidates and kills most of them fast, and 'I'm not sure what it means' names the writer's own blind spot rather than a cold reader's. The phrasing to adapt comes from the power-phrase cloud below; 'too corny' is the understatement rule below applied as a kill-test; 'too long-winded' is bounded by the two-to-three-line budget below, which is a length allowance, not a licence to ramble in line one. Honest limit: with a thin posting history the ranked list of past hooks is short, so on day one seed the variants from the seven interest components and the contrast rule below and rank them as posts accumulate.

## Frame the hook in second person ('if you've struggled with skin problems your whole life…') rather than first person ('I've struggled with skin problems my whole life'), because first person forces the reader to decide whether they resemble you before they can decide the content is relevant.

**Why it works:** A first-person problem statement inserts an identification step: the reader asks 'do I have the same problems this person does?', and any mismatch — age, skin, lifestyle, how they look — discounts the solution before it arrives. Second-person framing removes the step by asserting the relevance directly at the reader, so the only question left is whether they want what comes next.

**Evidence offered:** Paired good/bad example hooks; assertion about the reader's internal question.

**Fit here (8/10):** Directly relevant to a founder-operator who films himself, because his default is to narrate his own deodorant and oral-care story, and this names the specific cost of doing that in the hook position. Runnable today on the videos he already posts, plus Meta primary text and subject lines. It is the standard fix for the 'irrelevance' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the four call-out types in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which name a group rather than choosing a grammatical person, and it deliberately *narrows* the 'write claims as personal experience — how I, not how to' rule recorded there: that rule holds for the body, this one overrides it for the hook. So second person in line one, first person from line two onward. Same narrowing applies to the mid-action opening below — 'I kept my arms down through the whole meeting' becomes 'you know the meeting where you keep your arms down' in the hook position, and the first-person version moves down to beat one of the seven-beat body. It applies to both hook deliverables on a video: the on-screen text as well as the spoken line. One deliberate exception worth keeping straight: the self-exclusion opener below is first person on purpose, but the voice is the *buyer's* ('I'm not a natural-products person'), not the founder's, so it does not trigger a resemblance check — it *is* the resemblance.

## Manufacture the curiosity half with contrast — A is what the reader already believes is the solution, B is your alternative that is faster, better or cheaper — either as stated contrast (name both A and B) or implied contrast (name only B and let the known default stand in for A).

**Why it works:** Setting a comparison against the reader's current baseline re-agitates the pain that baseline failed to solve, and that re-agitation is what produces the urge to keep reading. The gap between A and B *is* the curiosity. Naming B alone is enough when the audience already knows what the default is, because they supply A themselves.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples in a skincare category ('most people solve acne with Accutane, but I have an herbal remedy that does it three times faster' vs 'this herbal supplement is eight times more effective'). Assertion about the subconscious mechanism.

**Fit here (8/10):** The reusable engine for the curiosity half of the hook spec above, and this catalogue has a natural A in every category: conventional aluminium antiperspirant, mainstream toothpaste, drugstore lip balm — and sharpest of all, the natural deodorant that already failed them. It is the standard fix for the 'disinterest' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the open-loop rule, which says what a loop is rather than how to generate one, and not a duplicate of the compare-and-contrast mechanism claim in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which governs mechanism copy on a page rather than the opening lines of a video. The stated-versus-implied choice is a real per-SKU decision: state A where the buyer has not consciously named their default ('most people fix a smelly shirt by buying a stronger antiperspirant — that's backwards'), imply it where the default is universal ('nothing to reapply at 3pm'). Two hard constraints. Comparative numbers are exactly what this catalogue cannot assert — 'three times faster' and 'eight times more effective' would fail the claim-calibration and specificity rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and the health gate — so the safe form is an attribute or mechanism contrast ('no baking soda, so no day-four burn'), not a quantified efficacy contrast. And A is a hook premise, not a theme: the body still pivots to odour duration, irritation and re-application per the golden-thread rule below, rather than spending its word count litigating aluminium. At headline level this is the same lever as the conflict component in the seven interest components below.

## Treat the hook as two to three lines rather than one punchy line — topic clarity in the first sentence, the contrast set up in the following one or two — and reuse the rare cases where both fit in a single line.

**Why it works:** Clarity and curiosity are two separate jobs and rarely compress into one sentence, so hunting for a single killer line usually means one job gets sacrificed for snappiness. Budgeting two to three lines lets each be done properly, and it is still short enough to land inside the window where the decision is made.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, framed as how he writes hooks tactically.

**Fit here (7/10):** A structural rule for the script the operator writes before filming — it tells him how much of the opening to spend and in what order, which is a decision he makes on every video, at no cost. It also gives a template for the first two lines of Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, and for the subject-line/preheader pair on email (subject carries the topic, preheader carries the contrast). Interactions: speed-to-value above governs line one, the contrast rule above governs lines two and three, and line one still has to withhold the resolution while naming the subject. On video the same split runs across media rather than only across lines — see the two-deliverables rule directly below, where the overlay usually carries the topic and the spoken line carries the contrast. Two boundaries. It sharpens the 90%-effort rule below — the thing you concentrate effort on is two to three lines, not one, and the two primary texts in the 3-2-2 flexible ad should differ across all of them rather than only in the first sentence. And it moves the body's start: beat one of the seven-beat sequence below begins at line three or four, not line two.

## Treat the spoken hook and the on-screen text hook as two separate deliverables for the same video, and generate several options of each plus two full script variants before choosing.

**Why it works:** On a muted-autoplay feed the two do different jobs for different viewers: the text overlay is the only hook a sound-off viewer ever receives, while the first spoken line is what a sound-on viewer decides against. Writing them as one thing means one of the two audiences gets an opening that was not designed for them. Producing three of each and two full script variants also means you are choosing a hook rather than committing to whichever line arrived first, which is the default when hook and script are drafted in a single pass.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated output — three text hook options rendered on a phone mock, three spoken hook options, and two script variants (context-only and research-informed).

**Fit here (7/10):** The founder films, writes and edits his own social video, so producing an overlay line and a spoken line per post costs nothing but a minute of drafting, and it is runnable on the next Reel. Not a duplicate: the two-part hook spec above says what any hook must deliver, the four failure modes diagnose a broken one, and `marketing-paid-creative-testing` covers hook variation counts on paid ads — none of them splits the spoken line from the text overlay as two assets for the same video. Both copies have to satisfy the spec independently: each names its topic, each withholds the resolution, both in second person, and the overlay in particular has to clear the sixth-grade reading level rule above because it is read in a fraction of a second. Do not let them duplicate each other word for word — the workable division here is overlay carries the topic ('the week-two natural deodorant problem'), spoken line carries the contrast ('you probably blamed yourself, not the switch'), which is the same labour split as the subject/preheader pair on email. Scope: organic video only, since the paid side stays static-first per `marketing-paid-creative-testing`. Held at 7 because it is a production discipline judged at draft review, and because only organic short-form returns per-piece retention signal to tell you which of the options was right.

## The hook only has to earn attention and stay loosely congruent — it must not become the ad's theme. Once it lands, pivot the body onto the reasons the market actually buys.

**Why it works:** Attention-grabbing premises — shock, disgust, novelty — are chosen because they stop the scroll, not because they are the purchase driver. If the body keeps returning to the hook's premise, the ad spends nearly all its word count arguing a pain the buyer does not buy on, and never makes the case that would actually convert. A good hook therefore produces a losing ad unless the theme is deliberately handed over to the market's real motives after line one.

**Evidence offered:** Two fully written versions of the same script off an identical hook, with the specific bracketed passage where V1 goes off the rails; assertion plus worked demonstration, no performance data.

**Fit here (9/10):** Durable copy principle, age-independent, and the highest-value entry here because it is a concrete audit the solo operator can run on the primary text he is writing this week — for the live $30/day Meta campaign driving soap-giveaway entries, and equally for PDP copy and email. The category's most tempting hooks are exactly the golden-thread traps: aluminium, parabens, 'what's in your deodorant'. The actual buying motives are odour control that lasts the day, no armpit irritation, and not having to re-apply. So a 'what's in your antiperspirant' opener is legitimate as a hook and fatal as a theme. Practical test on any draft: strike the hook, then ask whether the remaining body argues odour, irritation and re-application, or whether it is still relitigating ingredients. This is what the one-dominant-idea rule below was always missing — that rule says carry one idea through the piece, this one says the idea must be the market's motive rather than the hook's premise. It also sets the content of the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below: the symptom, root cause, mechanism and result steps belong to the buying motive, not to whatever stopped the scroll. Do not overcorrect into abandoning the premise entirely — the callback rule directly below is the fix for that.

## Keep the piece congruent by paying the hook's premise back once, late, as a small bonus benefit — not as the closing argument.

**Why it works:** A reader pulled in by a specific promise feels cheated if the piece never returns to it, so pure abandonment reads as bait-and-switch. But the callback costs one short paragraph and can be framed as an extra ('and here's a nice little bonus…'), so congruence is preserved without ceding the body's word count to a pain the market does not buy on.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in the V2 script, where the germ/handle premise reappears as a single bonus paragraph after the real selling points; assertion only otherwise.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable copy principle, free, runnable today by one person. It is the operational half of the golden-thread rule above and the thing that stops the overcorrection. Direct form for this catalogue: open on 'what's actually in your antiperspirant', spend the body on odour that lasts the day and skin that stops burning by day four, then close with one line — 'and yes, there's no aluminium in it either' — before the ask. Placement is after the mechanism and objection-bridge steps and before the unhedged imperative below, so the callback never displaces the CTA. Slightly below the parent claim because it is a refinement of the decision rather than the decision itself, and because at ~54 orders/month the difference between a callback and no callback will be judged at draft review, never measured.

*Source: Stefan Georgi (@StefanGeorgi) — "Secret #12: Beware the Golden Thread (X post)" (social post)*

## Never ship LLM-generated copy as-is — run a named audit for the golden thread, because next-token generation structurally turns your hook into the whole theme.

**Why it works:** An LLM builds each line off the one before it, optimising for local continuity with what was just written. Given a hook, the most probable continuation elaborates that hook — so the model will keep circling the attention premise instead of pivoting to the buying motive. The defect is systematic rather than occasional, which means the remedy is a fixed QA pass on every generated draft rather than hoping a better prompt avoids it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion grounded in how LLMs generate text; no test data.

**Fit here (8/10):** A solo operator with no copywriter is exactly who drafts ad copy in an LLM, and this names a specific, checkable defect in that output rather than generic 'edit the AI'.
