# Copy Hooks and Formats

> Split 2026-09-07: this skill covers the OPENING and the format. Congruence, the body skeletons, the close and the drafting process live in `marketing-copy-body-structure`. Some "above"/"below" cross-references point across that boundary.

## A hook must open a loop and leave it unresolved — but the loop has to be opened on a named topic. Never open and close it in the same breath, and never open with context-free suspense.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is an itch the reader cannot scratch without continuing. A line that states the setup, the cause and the resolution has already scratched it, so there is no reason to read on. LLMs are trained toward resolution and completion, so their default output is a tidy summary that reads like a hook but functions as an abstract. The opposite error is just as fatal: a suspense line with no stated subject ('this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen') gives the reader nothing to evaluate against their own interests, so they cannot decide to stay. Where context-free suspense appears to work, the retention is actually coming from the creator's face, emotion or on-screen text — the words are contributing nothing, which makes the tactic unreproducible for anyone without that pull. So the thing withheld is the *resolution*, never the *subject*.

**Evidence offered:** A worked negative example he wrote himself ('My 77 year-old mother used to love walking her dog… Here's how a no-pull harness gave her back those precious mornings') dissected against six better alternatives that all stop short of resolution. Practitioner teardown, no data. For the named-topic constraint: a named bad example plus the claim that context-free hooks 'work way less often', with the attribution of TikTok's vague-hook success to face, emotion and text overlay offered as assertion only.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable direct-response curiosity mechanics, age irrelevant. This is the construction rule; the two-part hook spec directly below is the pass/fail test it serves (the withheld resolution keeps the curiosity half alive, the named topic *is* the clarity half), the curiosity-loop entry further down governs the other end — that the destination actually pays the loop off — and the lists/steps/stories rule below supplies the scaffolds that keep the loop open through the body rather than only in line one. All are needed and none replaces the others. Free, and applies today to the Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and preheaders on the retention surface, to PDP hero lines, and to the spoken and on-screen openings of the organic Reels/TikTok the founder films himself. The named-topic constraint bites hardest there, because a solo founder has no celebrity pull to carry a vague opener: 'the natural deodorant rule nobody tells you about week two' names the topic and withholds the answer; 'this is the craziest thing I've learned about deodorant' names nothing and is the version to kill. The named LLM failure mode matters here specifically because a solo operator drafts with an LLM and will otherwise ship the summarizing version: check every drafted opener for whether it has already given away the answer. That check is one item on the larger LLM draft audit below, whose other item is the golden thread — the same next-token bias that closes the loop in line one also lets the hook's premise swallow the body. The same draft pass should check register — see the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, since the LLM's other default is polished brand prose. Not higher because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list the effect is unreadable, so it is a judgement rule applied at draft-review time rather than something testable.

## A hook has exactly one job — get the reader or viewer to opt in — and it does that by delivering exactly two things: topic clarity (they know precisely what this is about) and on-target curiosity (they believe it is for them and want what comes next). Everything else in the hook is decoration.

**Why it works:** The continue-or-scroll decision is made in the first seconds, and it can only go your way if both halves are present: the reader has to know what the content is about *and* that it is relevant to them. A line that supplies only one leaves the decision unmade, and the default outcome of an unmade decision is scrolling. Reducing the hook to two named deliverables also turns it from a matter of taste into a pass/fail check, which is what a solo operator with no second reader actually needs.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from having 'studied literally thousands of videos'; creator cites a million followers and billions of views. No data shown.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle class (attention and retention psychology), so the source's recency is doing none of the scoring work. Organic short-form video is a live surface the operator scripts, films and publishes himself with a phone, so a two-item spec on the line he writes and speaks is runnable today at zero cost; the written form re-runs on email subject lines and preheaders and on the first line of Meta primary text for the $30/day giveaway campaign. This is the spec most of the hook rules in this skill exist to serve: the withheld-resolution and named-topic rule above is the constraint that keeps both halves alive, speed-to-value below governs where the clarity half lands, the contrast rule below is the standard engine for the curiosity half, the seven interest components above stack under curiosity, and second-person framing below is what makes the curiosity *on-target* rather than merely interesting. On video the spec has to be satisfied twice, once by the spoken line and once by the on-screen text, per the two-deliverables rule below. Clarity is the half a founder skips, because he already knows what the piece is about — so the working test is to cover everything but the hook and ask whether a stranger could say what this is about and who it is for. The four-point checklist directly below is this spec packaged as a pre-ship filter with two further screens bolted on (audience size and differentiation); run the checklist at draft review and this spec is satisfied as its first two items. Held at 8 because it is a specification rather than a revenue mechanism, and because at ~54 orders/month only organic short-form gives any per-piece signal to check it against; everywhere else it is applied at draft review. Note scope: the spoken version applies to organic video, since the paid side here stays static-first per `marketing-paid-creative-testing`.

## Run every hook through a four-point pre-ship checklist — it must appeal to the ideal customer, trigger curiosity, address a large enough audience, and say or show something different — and rewrite it if it misses any one.

**Why it works:** The hook is the only part of the piece that gets bought in the feed, and each of the four points fails it in a different way: the wrong person stops, the right person stops but is not curious, the audience is too narrow for spend to scale into, or the line blends into every other ad in the category and is read as generic before it is read as anything else. Because the four failures are independent, a fixed pre-launch filter is the only way to catch all of them before money or shooting time is committed — checking by feel reliably catches whichever failure you happened to be thinking about that day and misses the other three.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus the claim that 'this is the filter every $100K+ ad passed' across $4M/month in spend; one worked example ('What would happen to your acne if the oil glands feeding it were shut down for a few days?').

**Fit here (7/10):** A free go/no-go gate the solo operator runs on hooks he is already writing — the spoken and on-screen lines of his own short-form video, the first line of Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines — with no spend, no volume requirement and no second reader, so it is runnable today even with paid paused. Durable-principle class, so the date does nothing to the score. Two of the four points are the hook spec above in different words: 'appeals to the ideal customer' is the on-target half and 'triggers curiosity' is the curiosity half, and neither is additive on its own. What the checklist adds is the packaging — one filter run at a fixed moment beats four principles scattered across a skill — plus the two screens this skill does not otherwise own. Audience size is the screen that kills a hook pinned to a sliver ('if you're switching to natural deodorant before a wedding'); the category is forgiving here, since everyone who wears deodorant is inside the addressable set, so this point usually passes and is cheap to check. Differentiation is the point that actually bites in a category where every hook sounds the same — generate that half from the six-angle menu directly below rather than by staring at the line. One correction to carry over from the spec above: keep topic clarity as an implicit fifth item, because a hook can appeal, intrigue, scale and differ and still never say what it is about. And note that the source's own worked example is a mechanism-efficacy promise ('the oil glands feeding it shut down for a few days') that this catalogue may not make — `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks that shape outright, so borrow the interrogative structure and leave the claim behind.

## Generate the 'different' half of the hook from a fixed menu of six angles: speed of result, the result without the thing they hate, a price reframe of the result, an exaggerated guarantee, a genuinely new mechanism, or dramatised exaggeration of the outcome.

**Why it works:** Differentiation is not a mood you summon, it is a small set of predictable places a claim can be twisted so it stops sounding like every competitor. 'End cigar breath without quitting cigars' works because it removes the sacrifice the buyer assumed was mandatory; each of the other five angles removes a different assumed constraint — the wait, the cost, the risk, the mechanism the category has always used, or the modesty of the outcome. Having the menu in front of you turns 'make it different' from an instruction into six drafts.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus one named winner ('End cigar breath without quitting cigars') and one price-reframe example ('for less than the cost of one premium cigar…').

**Fit here (8/10):** The most directly translatable thing in this batch, because a 12-SKU natural body-care catalogue sits in a category whose hooks are interchangeable. 'The result without the thing they hate' is the natural-deodorant buyer's objection stated as a hook — protection without the baking-soda burn, without aluminium, without reapplying at 3pm — and it is the angle to reach for first. The price reframe is live at a $50.46 AOV (a bar that replaces three products, a stick costed per day). A genuinely new mechanism is available where the formula actually differs, and it must be a real formulation difference, not a renamed commodity. Two angles need governors here. An exaggerated guarantee is only usable if the business will actually honour it — write the guarantee that exists, not the one that sounds good. And dramatised exaggeration runs straight into the understatement rule below and the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`: dramatising a *situation* (the shirt you kept your arms down in) is fine, dramatising an *outcome* is an efficacy claim the health gate will block. The founder writes his own spoken and on-screen hooks, so use the menu on the next batch of organic short-form first, then on Meta primary text and Amazon bullets. Adjacent to the four-predictable-places differentiator rule recorded elsewhere in the marketing catalogue, which operates on the mechanism block; this one operates on the hook line and names two angles that one does not (guarantee exaggeration, dramatisation). It also overlaps the contrast rule below without replacing it: contrast manufactures the *curiosity* half against the buyer's current default, this menu manufactures the *difference* from competitors saying the same thing — the sacrifice-removal angle is where the two coincide.

## When a piece underperforms, diagnose the hook against four named failure modes in order — delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest — instead of guessing at the whole piece.

**Why it works:** Each failure mode blocks a different half of the opt-in decision and has a different fix. Delay and confusion break clarity: the topic arrived too late, or arrived in language the reader could not absorb. Irrelevance and disinterest break curiosity: they understood it and decided it was not for them, or it was for them and they did not want what came next. Naming which one is broken stops you from rewriting the wrong thing — adding suspense to a hook whose actual problem is that the topic shows up three lines in.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, presented as a taxonomy derived from studying thousands of videos.

**Fit here (8/10):** A cheap written debug checklist for content the operator already produces, with zero spend and no dependency on order volume, and durable enough that the source date is moot. It complements the 'run down the production stack before retiring an angle' discipline in `marketing-paid-creative-testing` by giving the hook link four specific, checkable defects rather than one vague one. Every fix is already in this skill: delay → the speed-to-value rule directly below; confusion → the sixth-grade reading level rule, the LLM clarity prompt, the isolation read and the 'not sure what it means' kill-test below; irrelevance → second-person framing below, plus checking the awareness level per `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`; disinterest → the contrast rule below, the six-angle differentiation menu above, the seven interest components above, the power-phrase cloud and the trigger-word edit. One honest limit at this scale: only organic short-form returns enough per-piece retention signal to diagnose against, so on email and Meta the taxonomy is applied at draft review against opens and thumbstop rather than as a post-mortem on real data.

## Fix 'delay' by cutting the fluff lines and moving the topic-introducing sentence to the very top, so the reader knows the subject within the first one to two seconds.

**Why it works:** Short-form retention is exponential decay with the steepest drop in the first two seconds. Every second the reader spends without the context needed to decide, a large share of them are already gone — so the topic sentence has to arrive before the cliff, not after two lines of setup. The fix is almost always subtractive: the topic sentence is usually already written, sitting third.

**Evidence offered:** The retention decay curve is described but not shown; illustrated with a 13-line script where line three is the first mention of the topic. Assertion plus example.

**Fit here (8/10):** Concrete script surgery — reorder the lines, delete the first two — that one person applies to every video he already films, at no cost and with no dependency on volume. Durable principle about attention, so no decay. It transfers directly to the first line of Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, to email subject lines and preview text, and to PDP hero copy. Concrete form: a deodorant video that opens 'so a few years ago I got really into reading ingredient labels…' should open 'your natural deodorant probably quit on you around day four'. Two boundaries. Moving the *topic* up is not moving the *resolution* up — the named-topic rule above wants the subject stated and the answer withheld, and speed-to-value is the commonest excuse an LLM uses to summarise the whole ad in line one. And this is distinct from the mid-action opening below, which decides what kind of sentence line one is; speed-to-value decides only that the topic-bearing sentence occupies that position. It pairs with the two-to-three-line budget below: topic in line one, contrast in lines two and three.

## Fix 'confusion' by writing the hook at roughly a sixth-grade reading level — fewer and simpler words, and active rather than passive voice ('the dog jumped', not 'the jump of the dog').

**Why it works:** A reader who only absorbs half the words cannot assess whether the piece is for them, so a comprehension failure blocks the opt-in even when the topic is technically stated in line one. Raising the share of words that land raises the share of readers who can make the decision at all — clarity is a prerequisite for relevance, not a stylistic preference.

**Evidence offered:** A deliberately mangled example hook rewritten into a clear one, side by side. Assertion plus demonstration.

**Fit here (7/10):** Copy craft the operator applies himself, first to the spoken and on-screen hooks of his own videos and then to Amazon bullets, PDP headlines and email subject lines. Not covered by the understatement rule below, which governs intensity and adjective restraint and says nothing about readability or voice — a hook can be perfectly restrained and still unparseable. This category is where it bites: 'aluminium-free', 'magnesium hydroxide', 'the transition period' are the exact vocabulary a founder reaches for in line one, and they belong in the body. Keep the hook at 'the burning stopped by day four' and let the chemistry arrive in the mechanism beat of the seven-beat sequence below. It binds hardest on the on-screen text hook, which is read in under a second. Held at 7 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review and unmeasurable at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Kallaway — "Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip" (2byPP_9F0-Q)*

## Run a written hook through an LLM with a specific clarity prompt rather than editing it by feel: 'I've written a hook for a short form video about X; keep the meaning identical but rewrite it at a sixth grade reading level so the viewer cannot misunderstand it.'

**Why it works:** The rewrite task is mechanical — simplify vocabulary and sentence order while holding the meaning fixed — which is exactly what a language model does reliably, so it returns several clearer framings of the same idea in seconds. Because the meaning is pinned by the prompt, the model is not being asked to invent anything, which is where its output usually goes wrong.

**Evidence offered:** The prompt is given verbatim; no before/after performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** A named, copy-pasteable prompt one person can run on every hook he writes for his own videos, and equally on the first line of Meta primary text and on email subject lines. Zero cost, no people, no volume requirement, and it is a generic LLM instruction rather than a platform feature, so the current-era tool names do not decay it. It is also the one LLM copy task this skill trusts to come back near-shippable, and worth understanding why: meaning is held fixed and only vocabulary and word order move, so the next-token bias that makes the model summarise an ad in line one or re-theme a body around the hook has nothing to grip. The three LLM *generation* prompts below (swipe file, emotion wheel, amplify-the-winner) do ask the model to invent, which is exactly why each of them ends in a human pass. Even here, trust does not extend to shipping unread — the rewrite still has to pass the withheld-resolution and named-topic check above, the three kill-tests below, the understatement filter below, and the health gate. Add it as a step on the LLM draft audit rather than treating it as a separate ritual.

## Do not prompt an LLM for hooks cold — paste in a swipe file of hooks that actually grabbed YOUR attention and instruct it to rewrite your hooks in those styles.

**Why it works:** A cold prompt returns the median of the training distribution, which is why it hands back 'get rid of X fast' every time. Supplying real examples that already earned a stop transfers their structure and rhythm onto your product instead, which is the whole gap between mediocre first-pass output and lines that are worth polishing. You are not asking the model to be creative; you are asking it to re-cast your subject into a shape that has already been proven to stop someone.

**Evidence offered:** Before/after examples given in the thread: level-1 output described as mediocre versus level-2 output ('The 3-second ritual cigar guys do in the driveway before walking inside so she never smells the lounge on you').

**Fit here (8/10):** Zero cost, one person, runnable this afternoon — the swipe file is built by screenshotting the hooks that stop him in his own feed into one doc, and the output feeds his own filmed short-form hooks first, then Meta primary text and email subject lines. This is the right substitute for the variant-and-kill-test rule below at this volume: that one ranks *his own* past hooks by performance, and at ~54 orders/month with a thin posting history there is barely a ranked list to draw on, whereas other people's proven hooks are available immediately and in quantity. Keep the file tagged by which of the six differentiation angles above each swiped hook is using, so it doubles as the menu's example bank, and it is the same external seed the power-phrase cloud below needs. Two constraints. Swipe the *structure*, not the claim — most high-performing hooks in adjacent categories (supplements, skincare, weight loss) carry an efficacy promise this catalogue cannot make, and lifting one verbatim fails `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js`. And the output is a draft, not a hook: it still has to clear the withheld-resolution and named-topic rule above, the sixth-grade reading level rule above, second person, the three kill-tests below and the understatement filter below.

## Screenshot an emotion wheel, paste it into the LLM, and prompt it for a hook that makes your ideal customer upset — because a hook that provokes an emotion holds attention better than one that describes a benefit.

**Why it works:** Provoked emotion — indignation, being contradicted, mild shame — is a stronger reason to keep watching than curiosity about a benefit, because the reader has to resolve the feeling rather than merely satisfy an interest. The emotion wheel gives the model a concrete vocabulary of feelings to aim at, which is what stops it from defaulting to neutral benefit language; without the wheel, 'write an emotional hook' returns the same benefit sentence with an exclamation mark.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus a generated example ('Most cigar guys think their wife is overreacting. She's not. You actually smell worse than you think. Here's why you can't tell.') and the claim that some of his best ads ever were built this way.

**Fit here (7/10):** Free, solo, runnable today, and the emotional territory is unusually available to this catalogue — deodorant and natural oral care sit directly on top of social self-consciousness, and nose-blindness ('you don't smell what everyone else smells') is exactly the wheel-driven hook this procedure produces. The founder films and publishes the result himself before it is ever tested as Meta primary text, so the line has to survive being said out loud. Not a duplicate of the negative-framing or shared-enemy angles in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: those are angles, this is a named generation procedure that can be pointed at any of them. Three governors. Mild shame is the live wire in body care — provoke the buyer's *situation*, never their character, or the piece buys attention and loses the sale. The output still has to be second person per the rule below, still has to withhold its resolution, and still has to clear the understatement rule below, since provocation is not a licence for superlatives. And an 'upset' hook aimed at skin rather than odour is one word away from an unapproved-drug claim — the SKIN cluster is where `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks exactly this vocabulary, so run the wheel on the deodorant and oral-care problems, not on eczema.

## When an AI-generated hook lands, do not ship it — ask for ten more like it to get variety around the winning idea, then take the core idea into a doc and do the final polish by hand.

**Why it works:** The first acceptable output is a direction, not a finished line. Re-prompting around it explores the neighbourhood of an idea that already worked instead of jumping to a fresh concept, which is where most of the value is; and the human pass supplies the voice and the concrete specifics — the real day, the real product name, the real situation — that the model has no access to.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only ('AI gives you direction; YOU give it the final polish. Never just copy and paste.').

**Fit here (6/10):** Free, immediately runnable, and the correct habit at this volume: at ~54 orders/month and $30/day there will never be a readable per-hook signal, so mining a promising idea by hand is strictly cheaper than shipping fresh concepts and waiting for data that will not arrive. It closes the loop on the two generation prompts above — swipe file or emotion wheel produces the direction, this rule turns one hit into ten candidates, and the three kill-tests below cut them down. Scored 6 rather than higher because it partly restates rules already here: generating several variants and cutting them against explicit tests is the variant-and-kill-test rule below, and never shipping LLM copy as-is is the standing position of this skill. The additive part is narrow but real — the amplify-the-winner re-prompt, which is the instinct a solo operator lacks, since the natural move on a line you like is to stop.

## Before shipping, read the hook alone, out of the script, and ask how many ways it could be interpreted — if more than one, rewrite until only the intended reading survives.

**Why it works:** The writer reads the hook with the rest of the piece already in his head, so ambiguity is invisible to him. Isolating the one or two sentences and actively hunting for alternative readings surfaces what a cold reader might take from it, and every unintended reading is a chance they decide the piece is not for them.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, offered as a 'clarity-proofing' tip.

**Fit here (7/10):** A real QA pass rather than motivational framing: specific input (the hook alone), specific test (count the interpretations), specific action (rewrite to one). Runnable today by one person on his own video scripts, and identical in form to the imagery QA passes in `marketing-ai-product-imagery` and the LLM draft audit — add it there as an item rather than running it separately. Concrete failures this catches here: 'I stopped using deodorant' reads as either 'I switched to a natural one' or 'I now use nothing', and the second reading loses the buyer; 'this fixed my armpits' reads as a skin claim. That second case is why the pass earns its place beyond craft — a hook read in isolation can sound like a disease claim even when the body is plainly cosmetic, which is exactly what `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks, so run the isolation read before the gate does it for you. This is the back-end check on a finished hook; the three kill-tests directly below are the front-end filter that stops most ambiguous candidates ever reaching it.

## Write hooks by pulling up your own past hooks sorted by performance, adapting the strongest phrasing to today's topic across several variants, then deleting each variant against three explicit tests — too corny, too long-winded, or 'I like the sound of it but I'm not sure what it means'.

**Why it works:** Generating variants off phrasing that already earned attention removes the blank page, and a named kill-test turns the choice between them from inspiration into a repeatable filter. The third test is the load-bearing one: it catches the specific self-deception where the writer likes the rhythm of his own line and therefore cannot see that it names no topic — the same defect the two-part hook spec calls a clarity failure and the four failure modes call confusion.

**Evidence offered:** Live walkthrough of the creator writing and killing four or five variants of one hook out loud.

**Fit here (7/10):** One person, a list of his own past hooks and a blank page — runnable this afternoon on a deodorant Reel, and the same three tests apply to Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and to Amazon bullet leads. Distinct from the isolation read above without restating it: that pass interrogates one finished hook for ambiguity, this one produces several candidates and kills most of them fast, and 'I'm not sure what it means' names the writer's own blind spot rather than a cold reader's. The phrasing to adapt comes from the power-phrase cloud below; 'too corny' is the understatement rule below applied as a kill-test; 'too long-winded' is bounded by the two-to-three-line budget below, which is a length allowance, not a licence to ramble in line one. Honest limit: with a thin posting history the ranked list of past hooks is short, so on day one draw the variants from other people's proven hooks via the swipe-file prompt above — that is the designed substitute at this volume — and seed the rest from the six-angle differentiation menu, the seven interest components and the contrast rule below, ranking your own as posts accumulate. The three tests are also where the ten amplify-the-winner variants above get cut.

## Frame the hook in second person ('if you've struggled with skin problems your whole life…') rather than first person ('I've struggled with skin problems my whole life'), because first person forces the reader to decide whether they resemble you before they can decide the content is relevant.

**Why it works:** A first-person problem statement inserts an identification step: the reader asks 'do I have the same problems this person does?', and any mismatch — age, skin, lifestyle, how they look — discounts the solution before it arrives. Second-person framing removes the step by asserting the relevance directly at the reader, so the only question left is whether they want what comes next.

**Evidence offered:** Paired good/bad example hooks; assertion about the reader's internal question.

**Fit here (8/10):** Directly relevant to a founder-operator who films himself, because his default is to narrate his own deodorant and oral-care story, and this names the specific cost of doing that in the hook position. Runnable today on the videos he already posts, plus Meta primary text and subject lines. It is the standard fix for the 'irrelevance' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the four call-out types in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which name a group rather than choosing a grammatical person, and it deliberately *narrows* the 'write claims as personal experience — how I, not how to' rule recorded there: that rule holds for the body, this one overrides it for the hook. So second person in line one, first person from line two onward. ## Manufacture the curiosity half with contrast — A is what the reader already believes is the solution, B is your alternative that is faster, better or cheaper — either as stated contrast (name both A and B) or implied contrast (name only B and let the known default stand in for A).

**Why it works:** Setting a comparison against the reader's current baseline re-agitates the pain that baseline failed to solve, and that re-agitation is what produces the urge to keep reading. The gap between A and B *is* the curiosity. Naming B alone is enough when the audience already knows what the default is, because they supply A themselves.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples in a skincare category ('most people solve acne with Accutane, but I have an herbal remedy that does it three times faster' vs 'this herbal supplement is eight times more effective'). Assertion about the subconscious mechanism.

**Fit here (8/10):** The reusable engine for the curiosity half of the hook spec above, and this catalogue has a natural A in every category: conventional aluminium antiperspirant, mainstream toothpaste, drugstore lip balm — and sharpest of all, the natural deodorant that already failed them. It is the standard fix for the 'disinterest' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the open-loop rule, which says what a loop is rather than how to generate one, and not a duplicate of the compare-and-contrast mechanism claim in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which governs mechanism copy on a page rather than the opening lines of a video. Run it as a four-question screen before drafting: is the problem felt today, is it recurring rather than one-off, is it emotionally loaded (embarrassment, not preference), and do new sufferers keep arriving? On 2026-09-02 that exact language was removed from the homepage, the hero bundle lander and a product card. So the format's own engine — an emotionally loaded problem felt today — is the thing this brand may not describe on its biggest cluster. Screen for it before drafting, not after, or the gate kills a 1,400-word asset that was already written. ## Run a long-form native-style ad: an organic-looking image with no text overlay, paired with a long first-person emotional story that ends in the accidental discovery of the product.

**Why it works:** The untreated image and story-led caption make the ad read as an organic post rather than a placement, so it earns reading time a designed ad never gets. **Evidence offered:** The format is described in detail and illustrated with a fully sketched no-pull-harness example (elderly mother, lost agency, discovery, resolution). Assertion and worked illustration; no spend, CTR, or ROAS data.

## Get the same borrowed drama with zero fabrication by making a reaction or plan piece off someone else's real story — 'if I had four weeks to make my skin look smoother and less wrinkled, here's exactly what I'd do' — instead of inventing a customer narrative.

**Why it works:** The borrowed story supplies the attention and the stakes; the 'here's exactly what I'd do' framing supplies the education and the product placement. Because you are reacting to someone else's real situation rather than claiming it as your own customer's, nothing has to be invented, and the hypothetical framing keeps the claim to a plan you can actually stand behind rather than a result you would have to substantiate.

**Evidence offered:** Two illustrative examples (a high-ticket fitness coach reacting to the bodybuilding punishment video; a skincare brand's expert or creator doing a four-week plan). Assertion only, no ads run, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection), age irrelevant, and the honest version here is close to something already known to work: 'if I had two weeks to get off aluminium without stinking at work, here's exactly what I'd do' is the transition-period plan the business already needs to publish, which is the single biggest driver of the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Written as an ordered step sequence with the count stated up front, per the lists/steps/stories rule above, and with the plan's substance drawn off the dictated brain dump rather than the model's average advice. It is also a naturally golden-thread-safe format: the borrowed story is confined to the opening, and the steps themselves are the buying motive. A stated plan with a countable number of steps is also the cleanest answer to the competence barrier in the self-exclusion rule above — it shows there is nothing to already know. It is the safest of the story-borrowing formats because nothing is fabricated and the hypothetical framing avoids an efficacy claim. Two real caps. First, it substantially overlaps the 'how to start' stem already recorded in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` and the published-results-timeline artifact in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` — the additive part is only that the piece can be pegged to a third party's real story as its hook. Second, the source's native execution is an on-camera reaction, and `marketing-paid-creative-testing`'s static-first rule plus the absence of a videographer, editor or creator roster means the runnable paid version here is written primary text or an email rather than a clip — the founder's own organic Reels/TikTok are the one place a spoken version is actually producible, and there the hook rules above (topic in line one, contrast in lines two to three, second person, and a separate on-screen text hook) govern the opening. Adopted at 5 with that scale-down stated.
