# Copy Hooks and Formats

> Split 2026-09-07, and again 2026-09-19: this skill now covers the named opening GAMBITS and the ad FORMAT the piece takes. What a hook line must deliver lives in `marketing-copy-hook-construction`, where candidate lines come from and how they are cut in `marketing-copy-hook-generation`, and congruence, the body skeletons, the close and the drafting process in `marketing-copy-body-structure`. Some "above"/"below" cross-references point across those boundaries.

## Open in the middle of the action, with something already happening, rather than setting the scene first.

**Why it works:** A line that drops the reader into an event in progress creates an immediate gap — what is happening, and why — that they have to keep reading to fill. A scene-setting opener asks them to invest attention before anything has happened, which is a worse trade for a scrolling reader.

**Evidence offered:** Six alternative hooks with the operative verbs called out ('was laying', 'I saw', 'was mad/felt sorry', 'she quit', 'she stopped'), contrasted against the summarizing version. Illustrative, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable opening-construction principle, age irrelevant, and additive — this skill covers transformation-first ordering and negative framing, but nothing else owns where in the timeline the first sentence lands. It is the sentence-level companion to the withheld-resolution rule above: mid-action is the easiest way to open a loop without closing it, and the named situation is what supplies the clarity half of the hook spec. Free and directly usable on the live Meta primary text, on email subject lines, on PDP hero copy and on the founder's own organic clips — 'you keep your arms down through the whole meeting' is a mid-action opening the category actually supports, and note the second person: in the hook position the mid-action scene is cast at the reader, per the second-person rule above, with the first-person version ('I kept my arms down…') moved down into beat one of the body. The raw material already exists in the pain-based review quotes `marketing-review-mining` tells you to elicit, and it is the same raw material the micro-situation beat below opens on, so a mid-action hook feeds straight into the seven-beat body. Capped at 6 because it is a craft rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and because for a body-care brand any mid-action scene must be a real customer moment rather than an invented one.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## In organic-styled social copy, keep the product out of the hook — the discovery should feel almost accidental.

**Why it works:** The format's whole advantage is that it reads as a post rather than an ad. Naming the product in the opening line breaks that frame immediately and re-triggers the reader's ad-avoidance. Withholding it lets the emotional story earn attention first, so the product arrives as the answer to a problem the reader is already invested in.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as the third fault of his bad example hook, with the reasoning that the poster's recommendation should feel incidental. Assertion only.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable positioning-within-a-format principle. Real overlap with the primal-desire rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` ('the product is the mechanism, not the headline'), which is what caps it; the additive part is narrower and format-specific — in an organic-styled Meta ad or a founder-voice caption, naming the deodorant in line one destroys the only advantage the format has. Usable now on the live giveaway campaign's primary text at zero cost. Reconcile with the clarity half of the hook spec above: keeping the *product* out of line one is not licence to keep the *topic* out — 'your natural deodorant quit on you by noon' names the subject without naming the SKU. Held at 5 because it does not transfer to the surfaces carrying most revenue: an Amazon title and bullets must name the product immediately, and a PDP hero that hides it is just confusing. Scope it to social copy and do not let it leak onto the listing.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Lead with the transformation — the 'day without pain' after-state — instead of the conventional problem-then-solution order.

**Why it works:** Most advertisers open on the pain, so opening on the after-state is itself a pattern break. It also resonates with buyers who have already tried and failed at other fixes, because it names the outcome they gave up on rather than re-describing a problem they know intimately. This is the ordering variant of the primal-desire angle in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: same insistence that the end state leads, applied to which sentence comes first.

**Evidence offered:** Points to Brex's ad account as the exemplar across multiple formats; otherwise assertion.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable positioning principle, free to apply to PDP hero copy, Amazon bullets, and email subject lines. Real Skin Care's buyer is often someone whose previous natural deodorant failed, so 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' is a legitimate lead. Scored mid rather than high because the existing product-image-stack skill already covers transformation as a visual format — this is the copy-order variant, which is why it lives here rather than in its own skill. Note the tension with negative framing in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: fear-first and after-state-first are competing openings, so pick by audience — negative framing for buyers still unaware of the stakes, transformation-first for buyers already burned by a previous attempt. When you pick transformation-first for a burned buyer, the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` are the body copy that follows the headline, and they are the same material as the failed-explanation beat in the seven-beat sequence above. Whichever opening you pick, it still has to withhold the resolution — 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' works; 'how our baking-soda-free formula gave me a day where I stopped checking my shirt' has already closed the loop.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open by getting the prospect to agree — a question, or a rhythmic recital of what they have been putting up with ('you swat, you shoo, you spray, but those flies still don't go away') — before you make any claim.

**Why it works:** Once a prospect has silently acknowledged a current pain, the likelihood of a purchase decision rises sharply, because they have conceded the premise the rest of the pitch rests on. It is the copy equivalent of getting a foot in the door: the reader has agreed to something before they have been asked for anything, and a claim that arrives after a conceded premise is evaluated as an answer rather than as an assertion.

**Evidence offered:** Four Billy Mays opening lines quoted (Awesome Auger, FLIES-Away, Slider Station, Tool Bandit), plus an unsourced appeal to 'advances in Neuromarketing have proven beyond a shadow of a doubt.' No data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Applies to live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines, PDP hero copy. The category has an unusually clean version of the rhythmic form — 'you reapplied, you switched scents, you gave it two weeks, and it still quit by noon' — and the raw material is already in the voice-of-customer file. Note that the yes-opener is natively second person, which is the framing the hook rule above wants anyway, and that the cleanest versions here recite the buying motive itself, which makes the golden-thread pivot above trivial; a yes-opener built on ingredients still has to hand the body over. Capped at 5 because it sits very close to three rules in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: negative framing, the prior-attempt walkthrough, and calling the buyer out by name. The additive part is narrow but real — the interrogative or concessive opener whose only job is to extract a silent yes before any claim is made. Sibling to the self-exclusion opener directly below: both extract a concession before any claim, but that one extracts an *identity* admission ('this isn't for someone like me') rather than a pain admission, and then dissolves it. It is also the first link in the believability chain the claim-ordering rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` describes. Reconcile it with the hook rules: the yes-opener still has to name its subject and withhold the resolution, and must not close the loop in line one.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Build the hook on the buyer's own self-exclusion — an identity statement ('I'm not a coffee person and I don't want to be'), a list of the expertise they lack, or a near-miss confession ('I almost didn't order this, it looked like the kind of thing you're supposed to already know about') — so the ad removes the competence barrier before price is ever considered.

**Why it works:** Many cold buyers never get as far as evaluating the offer, because the category signals it is for enthusiasts and they have already ruled themselves out. Opening on that exact self-exclusion and explicitly disqualifying the need for expertise dissolves the barrier before it can operate, and the near-miss confession does the persuading in the buyer's own voice rather than the brand's — it is a peer reporting that the doubt was unfounded, which is far harder to argue with than a reassurance from the seller.

**Evidence offered:** Three executions written this way, each line drawn from the review file with counts; 23 of 62 surveyed churned buyers said they nearly did not order for this reason.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable copy principle, free, and it translates unusually cleanly to this catalogue: natural deodorant and natural oral care both carry a 'that's for crunchy people / I'd have to change my whole routine' identity and competence barrier, which is a different objection from price or from 'natural doesn't work'. Runnable today on the giveaway campaign's primary text and on PDP hero copy — 'I'm not a natural-products person and I wasn't planning to become one' or 'I almost didn't order it, it looked like something you're supposed to already know how to use'. Draw the exact wording from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` rather than inventing the confession; a near-miss confession is a first-person account, so if it is presented as a customer's it must be a real one, per the truth constraint on the long-form native entry below. This is the deliberate exception to the second-person hook rule above: the first person here is the *buyer's* voice, so it does not force a resemblance check — it is the resemblance. Distinct from the disqualify-the-wrong-reader rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which narrows the audience by excluding readers; this one *includes* the reader by disqualifying the expertise requirement — do not run both in the same opener, since they pull in opposite directions. Three boundaries from this skill: the identity statement still has to withhold the resolution (name that the barrier was wrong, not why); the self-exclusion is a hook premise, not the theme, so the body still pivots to odour duration, irritation and re-application per the golden-thread rule above; and the natural home for the dissolved barrier in the body is the objection bridge in the six-step skeleton. Held at 7 because the identity objection is real but secondary here — 'I tried natural deodorant and it quit by noon' is still the bigger one — and because at ~54 orders/month this will be judged at draft review, never measured.

*Source: Professor Charley T — "Claude Has Officially Changed Facebook Ads Forever! (Tutorial)" (4-ApfzxGhYI)*

## Use the 'this one thing' hook — promise that a single change or product resolves the problem.

**Why it works:** The world is complex and the brain craves simplicity, so a one-thing promise offers clarity and relief; even partial delivery is enough to earn the attention.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus psychological reasoning; explicitly acknowledges the cognitive dissonance in the claim. No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable retention/persuasion psychology, age irrelevant, and free to test on email subject lines and PDP headlines. Fits a 12-SKU catalog where the honest version is real ('the one switch that fixed my transition period', 'one bar replaces three products'). Capped at 6 because in a body-care category the simplicity promise is easy to push into unsupportable claim territory — pair it with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` so the 'one thing' always has a defensible referent (a named SKU, a named swap, a countable replacement) rather than an implied cure. Note that the one-thing framing names *that* there is a single fix without naming *what* it is — keep it that way, or you have closed the loop in the hook; the topic still has to be named, so 'the one switch that fixed my transition period' passes and 'the one thing nobody tells you' does not. It also pairs naturally with the self-exclusion opener above, since 'one switch, nothing else to learn' is the concrete answer to a competence barrier.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open a curiosity loop — show the setup, hide the payoff behind the click — and make sure the destination actually satisfies it.

**Why it works:** An unresolved gap compels the click; failing to close the loop on the destination page breaks trust and kills the conversion you just bought.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with the explicit caveat about closing the loop on the landing page.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle but its native home is ad-to-landing-page, which is gated behind the traffic stage. It transfers usefully to email subject lines and preview text, which is a live surface given retention is the binding constraint — hence a middling rather than high score. This entry owns the *closure* half of the loop: whatever the opener withheld, the destination has to deliver. Closure is not the same as theme — a destination page can pay the loop off in one paragraph and still spend the rest of its length on the buying motive, which is exactly what the golden-thread and callback rules above prescribe. The construction half — how to write an opener that withholds while still naming its subject, and the LLM summarizing failure mode — is the hook rule at the top of this skill.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Escape the hard-sell stereotype with understatement: simplicity, few colour words, fewer adjectives than the reader expects, no superlatives, and short sentences that fall rather than rise in tone.

**Why it works:** The reader has a trained ear for sales language, and superlatives and stacked adjectives trip it, causing the whole piece to be discounted. Copy pitched below the expected intensity reads as reporting rather than selling, so the claims inside it are evaluated on their content instead of being pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, given as a list of stylistic constraints. No examples, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (copy register), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial, and a better fit than the source could have known: a body-care catalogue is already gated by the specificity and claim rules, and 'no superlatives, fewer adjectives' is the cheapest mechanical way to keep an LLM draft from producing unsupportable efficacy language — the failure mode is compliance as well as tone. Pairs directly with the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, and it is the filter every verbalization transform's output, every extracted power phrase, every swipe-file rewrite, every emotion-wheel provocation, every per-image headline candidate and every seven-beat or six-step draft has to pass — the mechanism, superiority-proof and result steps in particular are where an LLM will reach for superlatives, and the dramatisation angle in the six-angle menu above is where a hook will. It is also item four on the LLM draft audit above and the 'too corny' kill-test above. Additive next to the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which governs conversational tone but says nothing about intensity — a draft can be chatty and still stacked with superlatives — and distinct from the sixth-grade reading level rule above, which governs comprehension and voice rather than intensity: a hook can be simple and still hyped, or restrained and still unreadable. Applies to PDP body copy, Amazon bullets, post-purchase emails and live Meta primary text. Held at 6 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review, never measured at ~54 orders/month. Scope limit: the Amazon title still needs its keyword and claim terms stated plainly.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Borrow conviction by camouflaging the piece as the medium it sits in — study the channels people already trust, adopt their tone, feel, style and format, and make the piece blend in rather than announce itself as advertising.

**Why it works:** Trust attaches to a publication and bleeds from its editorial pages onto its advertising pages, and phraseology itself acquires an aura of truth independent of the material it carries. Matching the native format means the reader processes the piece with the posture they bring to content rather than the ad-avoidance posture they bring to advertising.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with print-specific devices listed — issue date, city of origin, a by-line — plus the general instruction to study trusted channels and adopt their tone and sincerity. No performance data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection and trust transfer); the newspaper devices drop away but the transferable form is live: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign written as an organic post, and plain-text education and reorder emails, whose format-by-job rule exists precisely because designed image-led mail gets classified as promotional. Free, solo-executable, and the founder-voice register is more credible here than it would be for Native. Capped at 5 because it overlaps the long-form native-style ad entry below, the spoken peer register in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, and the promo-versus-education email split in `marketing-email-design-production` — the additive part is only that format itself is a persuasion lever — and because of a hard modern limit the source never anticipated: **an ad may adopt a native register but may not be dressed as editorial or a third-party news item.**

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Screen the SKU against the format before committing to a long native story — long-form only carries on urgent, daily, evergreen problems (pain, fear, anxiety, insecurity) where new sufferers keep arriving. 'Nobody reads a 1,400-word story to buy an LED light.'

**Why it works:** Reading 1,400 words is a real cost, and the reader will only pay it if the problem is felt today and emotionally loaded. On a low-stakes or purely aesthetic purchase the format collapses no matter how well the copy is written, because the emotional stakes never justify the reading time. Screening first prevents spending the most expensive asset type on the wrong SKU.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with named qualifying categories (back pain, hair loss, sleep, blood sugar) and a disqualifying counterexample (LED light).

**Fit here (7/10):** Directly useful across a 12-SKU catalogue where format fit varies sharply. Daily underarm odour and irritation, plus the fear of a natural deodorant failing in public, is urgent, evergreen and insecurity-loaded — it can carry long-form. Lip balm, most soaps and the aesthetic body-care SKUs cannot, and a long story written for them is a week of the operator's time thrown away. With Meta live at $30/day and one person writing everything, a gate that decides *which* SKU gets the expensive asset is worth more than the asset itself. Run it as a four-question screen before drafting: is the problem felt today, is it recurring rather than one-off, is it emotionally loaded (embarrassment, not preference), and do new sufferers keep arriving? Deodorant passes all four; if a SKU fails any of them, write the direct problem-aware short version instead. **Add a fifth question this catalogue cannot skip: can the urgent problem be named without a disease claim?** Deodorant passes it too — odour and social embarrassment are cosmetic concerns. The SKIN cluster fails it, and fails it precisely *because* it would otherwise pass the first four: the urgent, daily, insecurity-loaded problem a lotion or cream story would have to open on is eczema or dermatitis, and `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks both as unapproved-drug claims for a cosmetic. On 2026-09-02 that exact language was removed from the homepage, the hero bundle lander and a product card. So the format's own engine — an emotionally loaded problem felt today — is the thing this brand may not describe on its biggest cluster. Screen for it before drafting, not after, or the gate kills a 1,400-word asset that was already written. This is the gate on the long-form native entry directly below, and on the sectional-approval, brain-dump and structure-and-corpus workflows above — do not spend the upstream lock, the dictation, the structure lookup or section-by-section drafting on a SKU that fails the screen. Adjacent to but distinct from the desire-ranking claim in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which ranks which desire to lead on rather than screening whether a *format* is viable at all.

*Source: Lorenzo Pravata (@lorenzo_pravata) — "Native ads with GPT Images 2 + Claude (Full Playbook)" (social post)*

## Run a long-form native-style ad: an organic-looking image with no text overlay, paired with a long first-person emotional story that ends in the accidental discovery of the product.

**Why it works:** The untreated image and story-led caption make the ad read as an organic post rather than a placement, so it earns reading time a designed ad never gets. The long story builds emotional investment before any claim is made, and the product then lands as a resolution rather than a pitch.

**Evidence offered:** The format is described in detail and illustrated with a fully sketched no-pull-harness example (elderly mother, lost agency, discovery, resolution). Assertion and worked illustration; no spend, CTR, or ROAS data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Platform-mechanics class for the format specifics (Meta caption behaviour, no-text organic image), and undated — treat the format detail with suspicion even though the underlying story-lead principle is durable. The reason to adopt at all is that it is one of the very few ad formats a solo operator with no designer and no video can actually produce: a plain phone photo plus written text, which makes it a legitimate second primary text inside the 3-2-2 flexible ad now running at $30/day. Only run it on a SKU that clears the format screen directly above — deodorant does, most of the catalogue does not. Scale-down stated: 300–800 words in the founder's own voice, not 3,000, and **the story must be true** — either the founder's reason for formulating baking-soda-free, or a real customer's transition-period account sourced through `marketing-review-mining`. A fabricated emotional testimonial in body care is an FTC problem, not a creative choice. This is absolute for this catalogue, and deliberately stricter than the law: disclosed dramatization is legal in general, but nothing in this repo ENFORCES a disclosure — `claims.js` demands a source only for claims flagged `factual`, and an invented narrative asserts no fact, so it would pass both gates unchallenged. The rule is the enforcement. If you want borrowed drama, use the reaction/plan format below, which fabricates nothing. Because the piece is long enough to drift, draft it under the upstream-lock and sectional-approval rule above rather than generating it in one pass, off the published flow for the format with your real pitch corpus and dictated substance sheet loaded, per the brain-dump and structure-and-corpus rules. Pair with the mid-action opening, the two-to-three-line hook budget with second-person framing (the story's first person starts at line three, not line one), the seven-beat body sequence, the six-step skeleton's back half (the story cannot end at discovery — it still owes the superiority proof, the objection bridge and the ask), the golden-thread rule (the story is the hook, not the theme — the middle still has to argue odour, irritation and re-application), the product-out-of-the-hook rule, the camouflage rule above and the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which are the craft rules this format depends on. Held at 5 because the evidence is entirely illustrative, and long-form emotional copy is a poor fit for a $12 consumable giveaway entry compared with a direct problem-aware angle.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Get the same borrowed drama with zero fabrication by making a reaction or plan piece off someone else's real story — 'if I had four weeks to make my skin look smoother and less wrinkled, here's exactly what I'd do' — instead of inventing a customer narrative.

**Why it works:** The borrowed story supplies the attention and the stakes; the 'here's exactly what I'd do' framing supplies the education and the product placement. Because you are reacting to someone else's real situation rather than claiming it as your own customer's, nothing has to be invented, and the hypothetical framing keeps the claim to a plan you can actually stand behind rather than a result you would have to substantiate.

**Evidence offered:** Two illustrative examples (a high-ticket fitness coach reacting to the bodybuilding punishment video; a skincare brand's expert or creator doing a four-week plan). Assertion only, no ads run, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection), age irrelevant, and the honest version here is close to something already known to work: 'if I had two weeks to get off aluminium without stinking at work, here's exactly what I'd do' is the transition-period plan the business already needs to publish, which is the single biggest driver of the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Written as an ordered step sequence with the count stated up front, per the lists/steps/stories rule above, and with the plan's substance drawn off the dictated brain dump rather than the model's average advice. It is also a naturally golden-thread-safe format: the borrowed story is confined to the opening, and the steps themselves are the buying motive. A stated plan with a countable number of steps is also the cleanest answer to the competence barrier in the self-exclusion rule above — it shows there is nothing to already know. It is the safest of the story-borrowing formats because nothing is fabricated and the hypothetical framing avoids an efficacy claim. Two real caps. First, it substantially overlaps the 'how to start' stem already recorded in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` and the published-results-timeline artifact in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` — the additive part is only that the piece can be pegged to a third party's real story as its hook. Second, the source's native execution is an on-camera reaction, and `marketing-paid-creative-testing`'s static-first rule plus the absence of a videographer, editor or creator roster means the runnable paid version here is written primary text or an email rather than a clip — the founder's own organic Reels/TikTok are the one place a spoken version is actually producible, and there the hook rules above (topic in line one, contrast in lines two to three, second person, and a separate on-screen text hook) govern the opening. Adopted at 5 with that scale-down stated.

### If an ad dramatizes rather than reports, label it on the asset — 'Advertisement', or 'Fictional events portrayed by a real customer who has been compensated for their time' — and clear the wording with a lawyer rather than shipping an undisclosed dramatization.
**Falsified 2026-08-21:** Rejected on review 2026-08-21, not tested. The rule is sound in general law but unenforceable here: claims.js demands a source only for claims flagged factual, and an invented narrative asserts no fact, so a fabricated customer story passes both copy gates unchallenged. Adopting it replaced an absolute prohibition with a default while nothing enforced the disclosure. For a $12 consumable the narrative freedom is not worth the FTC exposure; the reaction/plan format gets the same borrowed drama with zero fabrication.

**Why it works:** Dramatization is a legitimate advertising device; what makes it deceptive is presenting invented events as a real customer's account. An explicit on-asset disclosure separates the two, so the creative freedom of a staged narrative is preserved without the ad implying a testimonial it cannot substantiate.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with sample disclaimer wordings, plus an explicit disclaimer that he is not a lawyer and the reader should consult their own. No enforcement examples, no cited regulation.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (compliance and disclosure), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. It applies directly to the live surface: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign is exactly where a solo operator drafting with an LLM will produce a plausible-sounding first-person account that never happened. Genuinely additive in one narrow direction — the long-form native entry above says only that the story must be true, which forecloses dramatization entirely; this supplies the disclosed middle path. Capped at 5 because it is largely a restatement of that prohibition from the other side, because the source is explicitly non-legal advice, and because for this catalogue the safe default remains the true version: a real customer's transition-period account sourced via `marketing-review-mining`, or the founder's own reason for formulating baking-soda-free. In a body-care category where efficacy claims are already gated by the specificity and claim-calibration rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, a compensated-actor dramatization is a lot of risk for a $12 consumable — treat this as the rule that governs the rare case rather than an invitation to stage one.
