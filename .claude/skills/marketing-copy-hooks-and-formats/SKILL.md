# Copy Hooks and Formats

## A hook must open a loop and leave it unresolved — never open and close it in the same breath. The most common failure, especially from an LLM, is a 'hook' that summarizes the entire ad.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is an itch the reader cannot scratch without continuing. A line that states the setup, the cause and the resolution has already scratched it, so there is no reason to read on. LLMs are trained toward resolution and completion, so their default output is a tidy summary that reads like a hook but functions as an abstract.

**Evidence offered:** A worked negative example he wrote himself ('My 77 year-old mother used to love walking her dog… Here's how a no-pull harness gave her back those precious mornings') dissected against six better alternatives that all stop short of resolution. Practitioner teardown, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable direct-response curiosity mechanics, age irrelevant. This is the construction rule; the curiosity-loop entry further down governs the other end — that the destination actually pays the loop off — and the lists/steps/stories rule below supplies the scaffolds that keep the loop open through the body rather than only in line one. All three are needed and none replaces the others. Free, and applies today to the Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and preheaders on the retention surface, and to PDP hero lines. The named LLM failure mode matters here specifically because a solo operator drafts with an LLM and will otherwise ship the summarizing version: check every drafted opener for whether it has already given away the answer. That check is one item on the larger LLM draft audit below, whose other item is the golden thread — the same next-token bias that closes the loop in line one also lets the hook's premise swallow the body. The same draft pass should check register — see the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, since the LLM's other default is polished brand prose. Not higher because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list the effect is unreadable, so it is a judgement rule applied at draft-review time rather than something testable.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## The hook only has to earn attention and stay loosely congruent — it must not become the ad's theme. Once it lands, pivot the body onto the reasons the market actually buys.

**Why it works:** Attention-grabbing premises — shock, disgust, novelty — are chosen because they stop the scroll, not because they are the purchase driver. If the body keeps returning to the hook's premise, the ad spends nearly all its word count arguing a pain the buyer does not buy on, and never makes the case that would actually convert. A good hook therefore produces a losing ad unless the theme is deliberately handed over to the market's real motives after line one.

**Evidence offered:** Two fully written versions of the same script off an identical hook, with the specific bracketed passage where V1 goes off the rails; assertion plus worked demonstration, no performance data.

**Fit here (9/10):** Durable copy principle, age-independent, and the highest-value entry here because it is a concrete audit the solo operator can run on the primary text he is writing this week — for the live $30/day Meta campaign driving soap-giveaway entries, and equally for PDP copy and email. The category's most tempting hooks are exactly the golden-thread traps: aluminium, parabens, 'what's in your deodorant'. The actual buying motives are odour control that lasts the day, no armpit irritation, and not having to re-apply. So a 'what's in your antiperspirant' opener is legitimate as a hook and fatal as a theme. Practical test on any draft: strike the hook, then ask whether the remaining body argues odour, irritation and re-application, or whether it is still relitigating ingredients. This is what the one-dominant-idea rule below was always missing — that rule says carry one idea through the piece, this one says the idea must be the market's motive rather than the hook's premise. It also sets the content of the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below: the symptom, root cause, mechanism and result steps belong to the buying motive, not to whatever stopped the scroll. Do not overcorrect into abandoning the premise entirely — the callback rule directly below is the fix for that.

*Source: Stefan Georgi (@StefanGeorgi) — "Secret #12: Beware the Golden Thread (X post)" (social post)*

## Keep the piece congruent by paying the hook's premise back once, late, as a small bonus benefit — not as the closing argument.

**Why it works:** A reader pulled in by a specific promise feels cheated if the piece never returns to it, so pure abandonment reads as bait-and-switch. But the callback costs one short paragraph and can be framed as an extra ('and here's a nice little bonus…'), so congruence is preserved without ceding the body's word count to a pain the market does not buy on.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in the V2 script, where the germ/handle premise reappears as a single bonus paragraph after the real selling points; assertion only otherwise.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable copy principle, free, runnable today by one person. It is the operational half of the golden-thread rule above and the thing that stops the overcorrection. Direct form for this catalogue: open on 'what's actually in your antiperspirant', spend the body on odour that lasts the day and skin that stops burning by day four, then close with one line — 'and yes, there's no aluminium in it either' — before the ask. Placement is after the mechanism and objection-bridge steps and before the unhedged imperative below, so the callback never displaces the CTA. Slightly below the parent claim because it is a refinement of the decision rather than the decision itself, and because at ~54 orders/month the difference between a callback and no callback will be judged at draft review, never measured.

*Source: Stefan Georgi (@StefanGeorgi) — "Secret #12: Beware the Golden Thread (X post)" (social post)*

## Never ship LLM-generated copy as-is — run a named audit for the golden thread, because next-token generation structurally turns your hook into the whole theme.

**Why it works:** An LLM builds each line off the one before it, optimising for local continuity with what was just written. Given a hook, the most probable continuation elaborates that hook — so the model will keep circling the attention premise instead of pivoting to the buying motive. The defect is systematic rather than occasional, which means the remedy is a fixed QA pass on every generated draft rather than hoping a better prompt avoids it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion grounded in how LLMs generate text; no test data.

**Fit here (8/10):** A solo operator with no copywriter is exactly who drafts ad copy in an LLM, and this names a specific, checkable defect in that output rather than generic 'edit the AI'. Applies today to the Meta primary text for the giveaway campaign, and to PDP and email drafts. Make it a literal checklist run on every draft before it ships: (1) does line one still withhold the resolution, or has the model summarised the ad; (2) strike the hook and check the body argues the market's motive, not the hook's premise; (3) does the premise get exactly one late callback rather than zero or five; (4) has the model reached for superlatives, per the understatement rule below; (5) is the register a spoken peer, per `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`; (6) does the draft still reach the objection bridge and an unhedged close, or has the model stopped at the mechanism, per the six-step skeleton below. Not a duplicate: `marketing-ai-product-imagery` holds AI QA passes for imagery (hallucination audit, text proofreading) but nothing about copy structure, and `marketing-competitor-messaging-teardown` only covers briefing an LLM off a surviving ad. The class is a durable principle about a tool behaviour, not platform mechanics, so the recency of the source is not doing the scoring work. Capped at 8 only because it is a QA discipline rather than a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Stefan Georgi (@StefanGeorgi) — "Secret #12: Beware the Golden Thread (X post)" (social post)*

## Build headlines from the seven components a meta-analysis of news found drive interest — recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing — and include at least two in every headline.

**Why it works:** The reader uses the headline to estimate the likelihood of being rewarded, compared against every competing alternative in the feed or inbox. These seven components are the empirically recurring reasons a headline wins that comparison, so stacking two or more raises the pick rate rather than relying on a single lever.

**Evidence offered:** Cited as a meta-analysis of news headlines, with an illustrative example per component. No ad-specific spend or conversion data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle, and the most concretely usable thing in this skill because it is a checklist rather than a taste judgement: run every Meta primary text and headline on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, every email subject line, and every Amazon title/bullet angle against the seven and confirm at least two are present. Distinct from the verbalization-transform list in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which transforms a claim you have already fixed, and from the six headline jobs in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which selects the intent the headline serves — this supplies the interest components you stack once intent and claim are chosen. Category note: celebrity is unavailable to a solo brand with no roster, so the workable stacks here are relevancy + unusual ('the natural deodorant rule that only applies in week two'), proximity + ongoing (a local, still-running giveaway), and conflict + recency (baking soda versus sensitive skin, framed as a current argument). Two components is the floor and roughly the ceiling — a headline carrying five reads as hype and fails the understatement rule below. Whatever you stack, the headline still has to withhold the resolution — and stacking an unusual or conflict component does not license that component to become the ad's theme, per the golden-thread rule above.

## Inject a trigger word or emotionally loaded phrase ('ruining my life', 'quits by noon') into a hook structure that already converts — don't rebuild the structure.

**Why it works:** The loaded phrase raises emotional temperature and stops the scroll, while the underlying structure that already worked is left intact — so you add attention without discarding the converting logic. This is the word-level lever that makes the taboo and negative-framing angles in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` land; it is an edit pass on a proven line, not a new concept.

**Evidence offered:** Claims both hooks shown had hook rates above 45%; agency practice with DTC brands. No breakdown of which brands or over what spend.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class and transfers off-platform at zero cost: email subject lines and preview text (the live surface, since retention is the binding constraint), Amazon bullets, and PDP headlines. Scored mid because its native measurement is hook rate on video ads, which does not exist here — Real Skin Care can only judge it by email opens on a small list. Practical use: take the best-performing existing subject line or bullet and swap one flat noun phrase for a loaded one, keeping everything else fixed. One loaded phrase is the point; a draft where every noun has been upgraded fails the understatement rule below. And the loaded phrase is an attention device only — if the body then spends its word count justifying the loaded phrase rather than arguing the buying motive, the golden-thread rule above has been broken.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Open in the middle of the action, with something already happening, rather than setting the scene first.

**Why it works:** A line that drops the reader into an event in progress creates an immediate gap — what is happening, and why — that they have to keep reading to fill. A scene-setting opener asks them to invest attention before anything has happened, which is a worse trade for a scrolling reader.

**Evidence offered:** Six alternative hooks with the operative verbs called out ('was laying', 'I saw', 'was mad/felt sorry', 'she quit', 'she stopped'), contrasted against the summarizing version. Illustrative, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable opening-construction principle, age irrelevant, and additive — this skill covers transformation-first ordering and negative framing, but nothing else owns where in the timeline the first sentence lands. It is the sentence-level companion to the withheld-resolution rule above: mid-action is the easiest way to open a loop without closing it. Free and directly usable on the live Meta primary text, on email subject lines, and on PDP hero copy — 'I kept my arms down through the whole meeting' is a mid-action opening the category actually supports, and the raw material already exists in the pain-based review quotes `marketing-review-mining` tells you to elicit. It is also the same raw material the micro-situation beat below opens on, so a mid-action hook feeds straight into the seven-beat body. Capped at 6 because it is a craft rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and because for a body-care brand any mid-action scene must be a real customer moment rather than an invented one.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Sequence the body through seven ordered beats — micro-situation, symptom, inner reaction, failed explanation, pattern recognition, root cause, mechanism — rather than opening on the problem callout or the mechanism.

**Why it works:** Each beat earns a successively larger concession. The exact moment of the problem produces self-recognition; the symptom names what he already knows; the inner reaction proves you are inside his head; naming the explanation he currently holds and then replacing it creates doubt in his own model; pattern recognition makes the doubt general rather than a one-off; and only once that doubt exists does a mechanism read as necessary rather than as another product claim. Leading with the mechanism inverts this — it asks a reader with an intact explanation to care about a fix for a cause he does not yet believe in.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only — 'one of the BEST frameworks we've tested lately', with no numbers, no example ad, and no named account.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle class (copy structure), so age is irrelevant and the source's date is moot. This is a template a solo operator can execute today with no budget: it is exactly what the Meta primary text pointing at the soap giveaway needs, and it re-runs into PDP copy, Amazon bullets and the pre-purchase nurture emails. Natural deodorant has an unusually strong micro-situation beat available (the arm-raise at 2pm; the shirt pulled out of the wash still smelling) and an unusually strong failed-explanation beat (the buyer blames his own sweat or decides 'natural doesn't work for me' rather than the antiperspirant transition or a bacteria mechanism) — draw both verbatim from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` rather than inventing them. Not a duplicate: the individual beats echo recorded claims — the prior-attempt walkthrough and blame-absolution rules in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` sit inside beats four and five — but no existing skill records an ordered beat sequence for the *body* of an ad, which is precisely the outline the 90%-effort rule below assumes you already have. Note the sequence's own limit: it is an emotional on-ramp that stops at the mechanism, so it says nothing about proving your mechanism is the better one, about the result to expect, about the objection bridge, or about the close — the six-step skeleton directly below covers that back half, and the clean composite is beats one through seven as the front of the piece and steps three through six as the back. One caution held it back from a 9: `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` instructs cutting the root-cause education block in a saturated category, and natural deodorant is stage 4/5, so the root-cause beat must stay one belief-shifting line, not a paragraph of education. Three boundaries from this skill: beat one is the body, not the hook — line one still has to withhold the resolution; the beats must be about the market's buying motive rather than the hook's premise, per the golden-thread rule above; and the finished draft still has to pass the understatement filter below.

*Source: Igor (@IgorWoorts) — "Micro-situation to mechanism ad framework (X post)" (social post)*

## Run the whole ad on a six-step skeleton: state the problem or want, show the mechanism that solves it, prove why your mechanism is better, state the result they can expect, bridge the skepticism by handling the main objection, then call to action.

**Why it works:** The order matches the sequence of questions a cold reader silently asks — what is this about, how does it work, why this one, what do I get, yes but what about X, what do I do now. Answering them in that order means no unanswered question is left blocking the click when the CTA arrives; skipping the superiority proof or the objection bridge leaves the reader convinced the problem is real and still unconvinced that *this* is the fix.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from claimed experience scaling brands past 8 figures — no data shown.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure), no decay concern, and directly usable this week: one person can write to a six-line skeleton in an hour, which is exactly what the live $30/day Meta campaign needs for both the soap-giveaway primary text and the deodorant statics, and it re-runs into PDP body copy, Amazon bullets and the pre-purchase emails. It extends rather than restates the seven-beat sequence above, which stops at the mechanism: the beats are the emotional on-ramp, this supplies the three closing steps the beats never had. Concrete form for this catalogue: problem (the shirt that still smells after the wash) → mechanism (odour is bacteria, not sweat, so the job is the bacteria) → why ours is better (baking-soda-free, so it works through the transition without the day-four burn) → result (a full workday without checking) → objection bridge (the main one here is 'I tried natural deodorant and it didn't work', answered with the transition-period explanation) → ask. Three constraints from this skill bind every step: the superiority proof and the result step are exactly where an LLM reaches for superlatives and unsupportable efficacy language, so they must clear the understatement rule and the claim-calibration and specificity rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`; the objection to bridge is the market's real one, drawn from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` and from the objection inventory in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, not the hook's premise, per the golden-thread rule above; and step six is the unhedged imperative below, not 'you can learn more here'. Step one is the body's opening, not the hook — line one still has to withhold the resolution. Held at 8 rather than higher because it is a skeleton rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month the six-step version will be judged at draft review against a four-step one, never measured.

*Source: Nick Theriot (@nicktheriot_) — "UGC beat structure, PDP layout, and 6-step ad copy (X posts)" (social post)*

## Put roughly 90% of your copy effort into the concept and the hook — the body is a formulaic outline you can model off an existing ad.

**Why it works:** The body of a direct-response ad follows a repeatable structure (story → pain → discovery → proof → CTA) that is largely interchangeable across brands, so time spent rewriting it produces almost no differentiation. The concept and hook are the only parts that decide whether anyone reads the body at all, so effort concentrated there is the only effort that changes the outcome.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, with the supporting claim that his four-step LLM modeling process produces 'a good ad' 99% of the time in 1–2 minutes plus ~10 minutes of tweaks. No spend, CTR, or conversion figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable effort-allocation principle, age irrelevant. Directly useful now that a $30/day hand-run Meta campaign is live: one operator with finite hours building a 3-2-2 flexible ad needs two meaningfully different primary texts, and this says the two should differ in **concept and hook** while the body structure stays fixed — which is what makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings. The seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton directly above are the fixed outlines this rule assumes: hold the beats and steps constant, vary the concept and the opener. Note the boundary the source's own LLM workflow ignores: cheap body copy is not unaudited body copy — the LLM draft audit above still has to run, because the model will quietly re-theme the fixed body around whichever new hook you dropped in. The verbalization-transform checklist in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` is how you produce the two headline variants without drifting off the claim, and the angle-count rule below is the companion for the opposite job — reusing one asset across surfaces of different lengths. It also transfers to email subject lines and Amazon A+ block leads. Capped at 6 because it is a prioritization rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month and $30/day the payoff of concentrating effort will be judged, never measured.

## In organic-styled social copy, keep the product out of the hook — the discovery should feel almost accidental.

**Why it works:** The format's whole advantage is that it reads as a post rather than an ad. Naming the product in the opening line breaks that frame immediately and re-triggers the reader's ad-avoidance. Withholding it lets the emotional story earn attention first, so the product arrives as the answer to a problem the reader is already invested in.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as the third fault of his bad example hook, with the reasoning that the poster's recommendation should feel incidental. Assertion only.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable positioning-within-a-format principle. Real overlap with the primal-desire rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` ('the product is the mechanism, not the headline'), which is what caps it; the additive part is narrower and format-specific — in an organic-styled Meta ad or a founder-voice caption, naming the deodorant in line one destroys the only advantage the format has. Usable now on the live giveaway campaign's primary text at zero cost. Held at 5 because it does not transfer to the surfaces carrying most revenue: an Amazon title and bullets must name the product immediately, and a PDP hero that hides it is just confusing. Scope it to social copy and do not let it leak onto the listing.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #2: Concept and Hook" (social post, undated)*

## Lead with the transformation — the 'day without pain' after-state — instead of the conventional problem-then-solution order.

**Why it works:** Most advertisers open on the pain, so opening on the after-state is itself a pattern break. It also resonates with buyers who have already tried and failed at other fixes, because it names the outcome they gave up on rather than re-describing a problem they know intimately. This is the ordering variant of the primal-desire angle in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: same insistence that the end state leads, applied to which sentence comes first.

**Evidence offered:** Points to Brex's ad account as the exemplar across multiple formats; otherwise assertion.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable positioning principle, free to apply to PDP hero copy, Amazon bullets, and email subject lines. Real Skin Care's buyer is often someone whose previous natural deodorant failed, so 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' is a legitimate lead. Scored mid rather than high because the existing product-image-stack skill already covers transformation as a visual format — this is the copy-order variant, which is why it lives here rather than in its own skill. Note the tension with negative framing in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: fear-first and after-state-first are competing openings, so pick by audience — negative framing for buyers still unaware of the stakes, transformation-first for buyers already burned by a previous attempt. When you pick transformation-first for a burned buyer, the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` are the body copy that follows the headline, and they are the same material as the failed-explanation beat in the seven-beat sequence above. Whichever opening you pick, it still has to withhold the resolution — 'a day where you stop checking your shirt' works; 'how our baking-soda-free formula gave me a day where I stopped checking my shirt' has already closed the loop.

## Open by getting the prospect to agree — a question, or a rhythmic recital of what they have been putting up with ('you swat, you shoo, you spray, but those flies still don't go away') — before you make any claim.

**Why it works:** Once a prospect has silently acknowledged a current pain, the likelihood of a purchase decision rises sharply, because they have conceded the premise the rest of the pitch rests on. It is the copy equivalent of getting a foot in the door: the reader has agreed to something before they have been asked for anything, and a claim that arrives after a conceded premise is evaluated as an answer rather than as an assertion.

**Evidence offered:** Four Billy Mays opening lines quoted (Awesome Auger, FLIES-Away, Slider Station, Tool Bandit), plus an unsourced appeal to 'advances in Neuromarketing have proven beyond a shadow of a doubt.' No data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Applies to live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines, PDP hero copy. The category has an unusually clean version of the rhythmic form — 'you reapplied, you switched scents, you gave it two weeks, and it still quit by noon' — and the raw material is already in the voice-of-customer file. Note that the cleanest yes-openers here recite the buying motive itself, which makes the golden-thread pivot above trivial; a yes-opener built on ingredients still has to hand the body over. Capped at 5 because it sits very close to three rules in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`: negative framing, the prior-attempt walkthrough, and calling the buyer out by name. The additive part is narrow but real — the interrogative or concessive opener whose only job is to extract a silent yes before any claim is made. It is also the first link in the believability chain the claim-ordering rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` describes. Reconcile it with the hook rules: the yes-opener still has to withhold the resolution and must not close the loop in line one.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Hold attention past the hook with three scaffolds — lists, steps and stories — and state the number of items up front.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is the durable retention driver, and each scaffold embeds an unresolved question in the reader's mind for the length of the piece rather than only in line one. A numbered list creates an explicit open question ('what are the other five?'); an ordered step sequence does the same with a stated reward at the end; a story creates the implicit version ('what happened next?'). Naming the count up front tells the audience exactly what they are staying for, which is why it raises completion rather than spoiling it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus example headlines for each device, with the distinction drawn that steps carry an explicit reward while lists are more flexible. No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle and genuinely additive: the open-loop rule at the top of this skill says a hook must leave a question hanging but supplies nothing that keeps it hanging through the body, which is where a solo operator's drafts collapse into a flat paragraph. These are the scaffolds, and they map onto assets the business already needs — a '5 reasons your natural deodorant stopped working' email, a numbered switch-period routine post, a 3-step first-use sequence for the post-purchase flow that drives the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Steps are the right scaffold when there is a real end state to promise (getting through the transition period); lists when the material is a set of independent reasons; story when the raw material is a single real customer account from `marketing-review-mining` — and the story scaffold is where the seven-beat sequence above supplies the internal order. Two constraints from elsewhere in this skill: the stated count must be honest, and the one-dominant-idea rule below still applies — five list items should be five fresh settings for one promise, not five different promises. Capped at 6 because it is a structure rule judged at draft review and unmeasurable on a sub-1,000 list.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 5 of 16))*

## Carry one dominant idea through the whole piece — and make that idea the market's buying motive, not the hook's premise — intensifying it by re-presenting it in successive fresh settings rather than repeating the same phrasing.

**Why it works:** A vague desire has no purchase-forcing power; concrete images give it weight, and the reader leaves with one dominant image no matter how many you present, so each additional legitimate setting reinforces rather than repeats, building emotional weight and making the price feel smaller. Repeating the same phraseology bores a sophisticated reader, so a fresh perspective is the only way to say the same thing twice; a perspective that is not genuinely fresh adds reading cost with no return. Which idea gets carried is decided by the golden-thread rule above — a piece can be perfectly unified around the wrong idea and still lose.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion framed as the opening of the 'thirteen ways to strengthen desire' section, with a stated stopping rule ('as long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire... use it'). No test data, no worked examples.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure/craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Nothing else here governs how a long PDP body, an Amazon A+ sequence or a multi-block email holds one idea across several sections. Clean form for this catalogue: one promise — you stop checking your shirt — carried through four distinct settings (the 3pm meeting, the twelve-hour shift, the gym bag on Friday, the hug at the door), each drawn verbatim from the review corpus rather than invented. Note that the promise chosen there is a buying motive, not an ingredient premise, which is the pairing this rule now requires. The stopping rule is the load-bearing half for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which will happily generate ten near-identical restatements. The price-salience argument is directly relevant to a $12 stick sitting next to Native. Held at 6 because it overlaps the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and transformation-first ordering above, it must be bounded by the three-job length test in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, and it will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Insert explicit momentum phrases into transition sentences — 'let me explain', 'here is what you'll find', 'all I ask from you is this', 'for example —' — so interest never flags between sections.

**Why it works:** Transitions are where a reader's attention has the cleanest place to stop, because one idea has closed and the next has not opened. A short invitation to continue removes that exit by carrying the reader across the seam, and because it costs a few words it does not slow the piece down.

**Evidence offered:** A list of four example phrases with the framing that they are 'invitations to read on'. Assertion only.

**Fit here (4/10):** Durable-principle class (long-copy craft), age irrelevant and free, but the ceiling is low. Half the source's momentum material is already owned by the open-loop hook rule above and the curiosity-loop rule below, so only the transition-phrase half is additive, and it is a device for long continuous prose. ## Use the 'this one thing' hook — promise that a single change or product resolves the problem.

**Why it works:** The world is complex and the brain craves simplicity, so a one-thing promise offers clarity and relief; even partial delivery is enough to earn the attention.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus psychological reasoning; explicitly acknowledges the cognitive dissonance in the claim. No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable retention/persuasion psychology, age irrelevant, and free to test on email subject lines and PDP headlines. Fits a 12-SKU catalog where the honest version is real ('the one switch that fixed my transition period', 'one bar replaces three products'). Capped at 6 because in a body-care category the simplicity promise is easy to push into unsupportable claim territory — pair it with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` so the 'one thing' always has a defensible referent (a named SKU, a named swap, a countable replacement) rather than an implied cure. Note that the one-thing framing names *that* there is a single fix without naming *what* it is — keep it that way, or you have closed the loop in the hook.

## When the same piece has to fit a shorter placement, cut angles — keep the opening callout and the CTA identical. Length is angle count, nothing else.

**Why it works:** The hook and the close are the two elements that decide whether the piece is noticed and whether it is acted on; everything between them is compressible content. So a short version is not a trimmed long version — it is the same callout and the same ask with fewer angles in the middle. Trimming the hook to save room removes the only thing earning the read, and softening the ask removes the only thing converting it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable formatting principle and practically the repurposing rule a solo operator is missing: one angle has to appear as Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, as a PDP block, as an Amazon bullet and as an email, and without a rule for what gets cut the four versions drift into four different promises. Concretely: write the long email version first with four angles, then cut to two for the PDP block and one for the bullet, holding the opening callout and the ask word-for-word across all four. When the long version is built on the seven beats and the six steps above, the elements that survive compression are micro-situation, failed explanation, mechanism and the ask — those are the spine, the rest are the angles you cut. The superiority proof and the objection bridge are the first two steps to compress to a single clause each; the late premise callback is an angle, not spine, and is the first thing to go on the shortest surface, since a one-line bullet cannot read as bait-and-switch. It is the complement of the 90%-effort rule above — that one says where to spend effort when writing a new piece, this one says what is safe to remove when moving an existing piece to a smaller surface. The unhedged-imperative rule below is what 'keep the CTA identical' means in practice, with the scope limit that Amazon A+ content is policy-constrained on cart-directing language, so there the ask is the one element that legitimately changes. Modest score because it is largely a refinement of what this skill already implies, and unmeasurable at ~54 orders/month.

## Open a curiosity loop — show the setup, hide the payoff behind the click — and make sure the destination actually satisfies it.

**Why it works:** An unresolved gap compels the click; failing to close the loop on the destination page breaks trust and kills the conversion you just bought.

## Escape the hard-sell stereotype with understatement: simplicity, few colour words, fewer adjectives than the reader expects, no superlatives, and short sentences that fall rather than rise in tone.

**Why it works:** The reader has a trained ear for sales language, and superlatives and stacked adjectives trip it, causing the whole piece to be discounted. Copy pitched below the expected intensity reads as reporting rather than selling, so the claims inside it are evaluated on their content instead of being pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, given as a list of stylistic constraints. No examples, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy register), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial, and a better fit than the source could have known: a body-care catalogue is already gated by the specificity and claim rules, and 'no superlatives, fewer adjectives' is the cheapest mechanical way to keep an LLM draft from producing unsupportable efficacy language — the failure mode is compliance as well as tone. Pairs directly with the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, and it is the filter every verbalization transform's output and every seven-beat or six-step draft has to pass — the mechanism, superiority-proof and result steps in particular are where an LLM will reach for superlatives. It is also item four on the LLM draft audit above. Additive next to the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which governs conversational tone but says nothing about intensity — a draft can be chatty and still stacked with superlatives. Applies to PDP body copy, Amazon bullets, post-purchase emails and live Meta primary text. Held at 6 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review, never measured at ~54 orders/month. Scope limit: the Amazon title still needs its keyword and claim terms stated plainly.

## Close with an unhedged imperative — 'order now', 'enter now' — repeated through the back half of the piece rather than softened into 'you can learn more here'.

**Why it works:** Once the pain has been surfaced, the product explained, the alternatives contrasted and the risk reversed, the only step left is telling the reader exactly what to do. A hedged ask leaves a persuaded reader with no instruction to act on and lets the momentum you just built dissipate; the direct form also signals that the seller believes in the product enough to ask for the order plainly.

**Evidence offered:** Two phrases pulled from Billy Mays' pitches plus the Zig Ziglar line 'timid salespeople have skinny kids'. Assertion only, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Narrowly additive: `marketing-email-design-production` owns CTA placement and bridging the ask to what was just read, and this skill owns register and pronouns, but nothing owned the ask being an *unhedged imperative repeated after the argument lands* — which is the exact failure mode of a solo operator drafting with an LLM, since brand prose defaults to 'you can learn more here'. Applies to the Shopify PDP CTA, the post-purchase and reorder emails where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign ('enter now' rather than 'buy now'). It is step six of the six-step skeleton above, arriving after the objection bridge — and after the late premise callback where one is used — and the element the angle-count rule tells you to hold constant when the same piece is cut down for a shorter placement. Capped at 5 because it is a one-line craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it is close kin to CTA rules already recorded, it must be scoped away from Amazon A+ content where cart-directing language is policy-constrained, and at ~54 orders/month no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better. The risk-reversal half of the source's pairing is **not** additive — the guarantee rule in `marketing-offer-construction` and the urgency rules in `marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing` already cover it, and bare 'order now' pressure with no expiring thing behind it is the empty version this catalogue has already ruled out.

## Run a long-form native-style ad: an organic-looking image with no text overlay, paired with a long first-person emotional story that ends in the accidental discovery of the product.

**Why it works:** The untreated image and story-led caption make the ad read as an organic post rather than a placement, so it earns reading time a designed ad never gets. The long story builds emotional investment before any claim is made, and the product then lands as a resolution rather than a pitch.

**Evidence offered:** The format is described in detail and illustrated with a fully sketched no-pull-harness example (elderly mother, lost agency, discovery, resolution). Assertion and worked illustration; no spend, CTR, or ROAS data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Platform-mechanics class for the format specifics (Meta caption behaviour, no-text organic image), and undated — treat the format detail with suspicion even though the underlying story-lead principle is durable. The reason to adopt at all is that it is one of the very few ad formats a solo operator with no designer and no video can actually produce: a plain phone photo plus written text, which makes it a legitimate second primary text inside the 3-2-2 flexible ad now running at $30/day. Scale-down stated: 300–800 words in the founder's own voice, not 3,000, and **the story must be true** — either the founder's reason for formulating baking-soda-free, or a real customer's transition-period account sourced through `marketing-review-mining`. ## Get the same borrowed drama with zero fabrication by making a reaction or plan piece off someone else's real story — 'if I had four weeks to make my skin look smoother and less wrinkled, here's exactly what I'd do' — instead of inventing a customer narrative.

**Why it works:** The borrowed story supplies the attention and the stakes; the 'here's exactly what I'd do' framing supplies the education and the product placement. Because you are reacting to someone else's real situation rather than claiming it as your own customer's, nothing has to be invented, and the hypothetical framing keeps the claim to a plan you can actually stand behind rather than a result you would have to substantiate.

**Evidence offered:** Two illustrative examples (a high-ticket fitness coach reacting to the bodybuilding punishment video; a skincare brand's expert or creator doing a four-week plan). Assertion only, no ads run, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (format selection), age irrelevant, and the honest version here is close to something already known to work: 'if I had two weeks to get off aluminium without stinking at work, here's exactly what I'd do' is the transition-period plan the business already needs to publish, which is the single biggest driver of the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Written as an ordered step sequence with the count stated up front, per the lists/steps/stories rule above. It is also a naturally golden-thread-safe format: the borrowed story is confined to the opening, and the steps themselves are the buying motive. It is the safest of the story-borrowing formats because nothing is fabricated and the hypothetical framing avoids an efficacy claim. Two real caps. First, it substantially overlaps the 'how to start' stem already recorded in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` and the published-results-timeline artifact in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` — the additive part is only that the piece can be pegged to a third party's real story as its hook.
