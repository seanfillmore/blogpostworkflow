# Affiliate Program Design

## Recruit affiliates as a lead channel separate from your own advertising. Affiliates are independent businesses that promote your product to their own audience in return for commission or free product.

**Why it works:** Each affiliate adds a compounding stream of leads. They pay for their own advertising, and they reach an audience that already trusts them. Every affiliate you sign adds recurring lead flow for a single recruiting effort, so you stop buying every customer yourself.

**Evidence offered:** Prestige Labs: 400+ affiliates, 300+ orders/day, ~$450k weeks with no paid ads or sales team. ALAN scaled to $1.7M/mo in six months on a few super-affiliates. $75M combined across 5000+ affiliates.

**Fit here (6/10):** One person can run the honest scaled-down version today. Skip the 'army'. Approach 3-5 non-competing local businesses whose customers sweat or read ingredient labels, such as yoga studios, CrossFit boxes, salons, midwives and natural grocers. Set them up to sell or sample the deodorant line. At $50.46 AOV this will not fix revenue this quarter. It is an acquisition surface with zero media spend that runs in parallel with the $30/day Meta campaign.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Advertise to potential affiliates the way you advertise to customers. Use one of five callout entry points: the affiliate owner's title, their customers, the result they promise, the products they already sell, or your own customers ('do you know anyone who owns a spa?').

**Why it works:** Recruiting affiliates is an offer with its own audience. Naming the affiliate by role, clientele or product line lets them self-select. Asking your existing customers for introductions turns your buyer list into a recruiting list.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, illustrated with example callout lines aimed at spa owners.

**Fit here (5/10):** Runnable today with zero budget. The fifth callout is the cheapest recruiting surface for a list under 1,000 people: ask the existing ~54-orders/month buyer base whether they know a studio or salon owner. Scored mid because it partly overlaps the existing 'call your audience out by name' claim. What is new here is the five entry points on the affiliate side.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Treat affiliates as a second class of customer. Build the offer as a money-making offer stated in their numbers, not as a product pitch. It should give them:
- more revenue from customers they already have
- a high likelihood of sales, because their audience already wants the product
- no build, fulfilment or support burden
- the ability to start selling tomorrow

**Why it works:** Affiliates buy income, not skincare. A partner promotes only when the deal improves their own P&L or makes their offer stand out. Running the four value terms against 'a new income stream' is what gets a busy owner to sign up instead of politely declining.

**Evidence offered:** Presented as the standard structure of every money-making offer. Two cases support it. In the chiropractor case, included adjustments make a gym membership worth more than a rival's. In the Prestige Labs case, supplement sales are extra revenue for the gym.

**Fit here (6/10):** Runnable today. A one-page partner pitch costs nothing to write. The partner really does carry zero fulfilment burden, because the solo operator ships everything. The honest pitch is 'this adds a $12 margin item to every new-member kit and makes your welcome package look premium', not 'please share my brand'. This applies the four-term value equation to a different audience, which changes what goes in each term.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Qualify affiliates by making them invest: they must buy and actually use the product to keep affiliate status.

**Why it works:** Money paid is attention paid. An affiliate who spent their own cash on the product believes in it and sells it more credibly. Anyone who does not believe in it will opt out on their own.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from experience across two affiliate companies ('nine times out of ten, if they pay, they'll pay attention'). He describes it as the lowest-barrier investment that has worked for him.

**Fit here (7/10):** Directly runnable at this size, and it costs nothing to impose. Any studio owner, salon or ambassador who wants a commission code first buys a small assortment at around $50. For a natural-deodorant brand, the partner has to vouch that it works, and this solves that credibility problem. It also filters out people who would never sell but whose codes a solo operator would otherwise have to administer.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Set affiliate commission from your maximum allowable CAC, which is gross profit per order divided by your target LTGP:CAC ratio. Pay for the specific action you want: new customers, repeat customers, or earlier steps such as a lead magnet download once you can track them.

**Why it works:** Once you decide what the affiliate is paid for, 'how much' and 'how often' nearly solve themselves. Tying the payout to allowable CAC makes the channel profitable by design rather than by hope.

**Evidence offered:** Worked example: a $200 product with $40 fulfilment leaves $160 contribution. At a 3:1 LTGP:CAC ratio, $40 goes to the affiliate.

**Fit here (7/10):** Runnable today, and needed before any code is issued. At $50.46 AOV with 18-22.5% repeat, the allowable-CAC calculation stops a solo operator from handing out a 30% commission that loses money on the first purchase. It also forces the same lifetime-value math the Meta campaign needs. Related to the existing allowable-cost-per-lead claim, but distinct from it.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Use a three-tier payout ladder instead of one flat rate, so the blended payout lands well below your maximum CAC:
- 25% of allowable CAC for anyone who signs up
- 50% once they activate (finish onboarding, do a launch, hit a post count)
- 100% once they sustain performance

**Why it works:** Tiers reward the behaviour you actually want, which is activation and sustained selling. Most affiliates never reach the top tier, so the blended cost per acquisition falls. That improves LTGP:CAC and frees money for contests and more recruiting.

**Evidence offered:** Worked example: a 20/20/60 tier mix gives a $30 blended payout against a $40 maximum, moving the ratio from 3:1 to 4:1.

**Fit here (6/10):** Sound and correctly aimed, but the blend means nothing without a roster of partners. With three partners and a maximum commission near $10-15, a ladder adds admin work and makes no readable difference. Park behind scale. Trigger: roughly 10+ active partners, or enough partner-sourced orders that the gap between blended and maximum payout is real money.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Do all the promotional work for your affiliates before the launch so they can plug and play. Hand them the assets, copy and schedule instead of asking them to create anything.

**Why it works:** Affiliates promote in proportion to how easy you make it. Removing every production step on their side is what turns a signed-up affiliate into one who actually advertises.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion: 'good launches have the work done ahead of time. So do all the work for them.'

**Fit here (6/10):** One person can do this today. A small partner kit takes an afternoon: three social captions, two product photos, a discount code mechanism, an email template and dates. That kit is what makes a busy salon owner actually post. It sits next to the fixed-component creator brief but differs from it. The brief covers asking someone to produce a video for you. This covers handing a partner finished assets to distribute.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Integrate with each partner at one of three increasing depths so lead flow lasts:
1. The partner gives your lead magnet or sample away free with every purchase of their own product.
2. The partner sells your lead magnet or trial product and keeps all the cash.
3. The partner sells your core offer for a share of the revenue.

**Why it works:** Level 1 makes the partner's own offer more valuable at no cost to them, so promoting it serves their own interest. Level 2 gives them pure-profit revenue with no fulfilment. Level 3 makes you a product line in their business. At every level you acquire the customer and own every later sale. Naming the level up front settles who pays for goods and who makes the pitch, which avoids a vague cross-promotion that nobody carries out.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples from massage and personal training for each level. He also cites what he actually ran: gyms giving a free nutrition consult to every new member, gyms selling a $99-$199 consult and keeping the money, and gyms selling supplements on a split. Further examples: a national tax prep firm giving away free LLC setup, and chiropractors bundling adjustments into gym memberships.

**Fit here (8/10):** The most transferable tactic in these excerpts and the cheapest to test.
- Level 1: a yoga studio, gym, salon or midwife puts a lip balm or deodorant sample in every client bag or new-member welcome kit, with a card that routes to email capture. This is a physical lead magnet the operator already manufactures, and the opt-in feeds the retention flows, which are the binding constraint.
- Level 2: the partner places a small wholesale order and retails it.
- Level 3: a plain commission code.

No budget, team or ad account needed.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Pay affiliates on the revenue their customers generate for life, and never cap payouts.

**Why it works:** A lifetime, uncapped split motivates the affiliate to keep the customer buying instead of collecting a one-time bounty. Their incentive lines up with retention rather than churn.

**Evidence offered:** A stated personal preference: 'I prefer to pay forever so my affiliates stay motivated to keep my customers forever. And, I never cap payouts.'

**Fit here (6/10):** Cheap to promise, and well matched to the binding constraint. Repeat customers are 45-52% of revenue, so paying a partner on repeat orders gives you a second party who cares about the second purchase. Check it against the allowable-CAC math so lifetime commission does not eat the retention margin. Runnable today in Shopify with a standard affiliate app for a handful of partners.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Give every shopper who arrives through an affiliate's or partner's link a unique single-use discount code instead of one shared code. Shared codes leak to coupon sites, and then you pay commission on sales the partner did not drive.

**Why it works:** Shared codes spread to Honey, coupon sites and Reddit. Buyers who would have paid full price then get a discount, and the partner gets credit for the sale, so you lose margin twice. A single-use code cannot be reused, so leakage stops at one order.

**Evidence offered:** Sponsor read: Social Snowball claims a client recovered $684K in five months. This is a paid endorsement, so the evidence is weak.

**Fit here (5/10):** The leakage mechanism is real. It starts to matter once RSC hands codes to creators, partners or giveaway entrants. You do not need the sponsor's tool. Shopify's free bulk generation of unique codes does the same job today. Make every code given to Trybe creators, partners or giveaway lists unique and single-use. Give each partner's batch a shared prefix so attribution still works per partner. The score reflects modest merit and evidence that comes only from a sponsor.

*Source: Sweat Equity — "The Ad Creative Strategy That Takes Brands to $100M in Record Time" (QbCGvRnWC9Y)*

## Run partners in two phases. First, a one-time 'launch' promotion to their audience proves the deal works. Then 'integrate' the product into their ongoing core offer so the flow continues without another campaign.

**Why it works:** A launch produces a visible burst of results, which gives the partner a reason to believe. Integration turns a one-off promo into a standing line item in their offer. Every new customer of theirs then becomes a lead or sale for you automatically, with no repeated persuasion.

**Evidence offered:** Three worked case studies:
- a blast-off seminar, then an embedded LLC offer
- a free 28-day challenge, then a nutrition orientation for every new member
- a paid workshop, then adjustments included in every gym membership

**Fit here (7/10):** Translates directly at this size. The launch is a one-week deodorant giveaway or sampling event with one local studio. Integration means that studio puts a travel-size deodorant or lip balm in every new-member welcome kit, bought wholesale monthly. Integration is the part that matters here, because one relationship then produces recurring orders instead of a one-time spike. Runnable now with no team and no spend.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 15 of 16))*

## Give the partner all or nearly all of the money from the launch event. You take the customers and their contact details, not the front-end revenue.

**Why it works:** The partner does the promoting. Paying them 100% of the front end makes the deal free money for them, so they will repeat it. You earn later from the customers you acquired, whose lifetime value is larger than the front-end margin you gave up.

**Evidence offered:** Chiropractor case study: the gym owner keeps the full $2,970 from a $99 workshop for thirty people. Their only work is a few emails and posts. The chiropractor gets a room full of prospects.

**Fit here (7/10):** Solves the exact problem a brand with a list under 1,000 faces when approaching a partner with a bigger audience. You have nothing of equal value to trade, so pay in margin instead. For example, let a studio keep 100% of retail at a sampling event or pop-up, or give them the full margin on a first wholesale order, in exchange for the email/SMS opt-ins. At $50 AOV and 18-22% repeat, giving up the first sale's margin is affordable if the opt-in enters the flows.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 15 of 16))*

## Judge a partner program by comparing what each affiliate cost to recruit and enable against the gross profit of every customer that affiliate sends. Aim for 3:1 or better. A weak ratio can be improved in exactly three ways: cheaper recruiting, a launch process that activates signed partners, or deeper integration that makes each partner worth more.

**Why it works:** Affiliates rarely buy much themselves, so judging them on their own purchases gives a false reading. Crediting all downstream customer gross profit to the partner who sent those customers makes the spend decision clear. The three levers tell you which end of the program to fix.

**Evidence offered:** Worked arithmetic: $4,000 affiliate CAC, $120k lifetime sales, 75% margins and a 40% payout give a 12.5:1 ratio. Stated minimum is 3:1.

**Fit here (5/10):** The measurement logic lasts. The dollar figures are B2B-scale and do not apply here. A scaled-down version is runnable today. Give every partner their own batch of Shopify codes under a partner-specific prefix, using the single-use code approach above so leaked codes do not distort the numbers. Then track revenue and gross profit per partner against the hours and free product spent recruiting them. The real value is the diagnosis of whether activation or integration is the weak point. Scored mid because at ~54 orders/month, one partner's contribution will not be precise enough to act on for months.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 15 of 16))*

## Sign a first group of affiliates and use their feedback to debug the offer, terms, launch and integration. Then turn their documented results into recruiting material for the next batch.

**Why it works:** Early partners expose friction in the deal before you scale it. Their results then become third-party proof. That answers the biggest objection from the next partner, who wants to know it worked for someone like them.

**Evidence offered:** Stated action step: 'advertise your affiliate offer until you get ten to twenty affiliates… turn their results into your first batch of affiliate lead magnets'.

**Fit here (5/10):** Ten to twenty partners is far more than one operator at $2,700/mo can service. The honest scale-down is two or three local studios or shops. Run them, fix the wholesale terms and the display, then open with a line like 'the studio on X street sells eight units a month in their welcome kits' when approaching partner four. Not parked, because the scaled-down version can run now.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 15 of 16))*

## Point some of your paid ads at recruiting affiliates and partners, not only at customers. The same targeting machinery can build an affiliate program.

**Why it works:** An affiliate acquired through paid ads becomes a lasting distribution point rather than a single purchase. The same ad dollar buys recurring reach instead of one order.

**Evidence offered:** The author expanded his paid ads to target potential affiliates, which 'paved the way' for his affiliate programs.

**Fit here (5/10):** A legitimate ecommerce channel at any size, but the $30/day budget has one job right now: buying giveaway entries. Splitting it across a second objective would leave neither ad set readable at ~54 orders/month. Park behind scale. Trigger: the giveaway campaign reaches a stable cost per entry with budget room for a second ad set, or the list grows large enough to recruit affiliates organically.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 16 of 16))*
