# Copy Hook Construction

> Split 2026-09-19 out of `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`: this skill covers what the hook LINE must deliver, and how one is screened and diagnosed. Where candidate lines come from and how they are cut lives in `marketing-copy-hook-generation`; the named opening gambits and the ad formats stay in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`; congruence, the body skeletons, the close and the drafting process live in `marketing-copy-body-structure`. Some "above"/"below" cross-references point across those boundaries.

## A hook must open a loop and leave it unresolved — but the loop has to be opened on a named topic. Never open and close it in the same breath, and never open with context-free suspense.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is an itch the reader cannot scratch without continuing. A line that states the setup, the cause and the resolution has already scratched it, so there is no reason to read on. LLMs are trained toward resolution and completion, so their default output is a tidy summary that reads like a hook but functions as an abstract. The opposite error is just as fatal: a suspense line with no stated subject ('this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen') gives the reader nothing to evaluate against their own interests, so they cannot decide to stay. Where context-free suspense appears to work, the retention is actually coming from the creator's face, emotion or on-screen text — the words are contributing nothing, which makes the tactic unreproducible for anyone without that pull. So the thing withheld is the *resolution*, never the *subject*.

**Evidence offered:** A worked negative example he wrote himself ('My 77 year-old mother used to love walking her dog… Here's how a no-pull harness gave her back those precious mornings') dissected against six better alternatives that all stop short of resolution. Practitioner teardown, no data. For the named-topic constraint: a named bad example plus the claim that context-free hooks 'work way less often', with the attribution of TikTok's vague-hook success to face, emotion and text overlay offered as assertion only.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable direct-response curiosity mechanics, age irrelevant. This is the construction rule; the two-part hook spec directly below is the pass/fail test it serves (the withheld resolution keeps the curiosity half alive, the named topic *is* the clarity half), the curiosity-loop entry further down governs the other end — that the destination actually pays the loop off — and the lists/steps/stories rule below supplies the scaffolds that keep the loop open through the body rather than only in line one. All are needed and none replaces the others. Free, and applies today to the Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, to email subject lines and preheaders on the retention surface, to PDP hero lines, and to the spoken and on-screen openings of the organic Reels/TikTok the founder films himself. The named-topic constraint bites hardest there, because a solo founder has no celebrity pull to carry a vague opener: 'the natural deodorant rule nobody tells you about week two' names the topic and withholds the answer; 'this is the craziest thing I've learned about deodorant' names nothing and is the version to kill. The named LLM failure mode matters here specifically because a solo operator drafts with an LLM and will otherwise ship the summarizing version: check every drafted opener for whether it has already given away the answer. That check is one item on the larger LLM draft audit below, whose other item is the golden thread — the same next-token bias that closes the loop in line one also lets the hook's premise swallow the body. The same draft pass should check register — see the spoken peer register rule in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, since the LLM's other default is polished brand prose. Not higher because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list the effect is unreadable, so it is a judgement rule applied at draft-review time rather than something testable.

## A hook has exactly one job — get the reader or viewer to opt in — and it does that by delivering exactly two things: topic clarity (they know precisely what this is about) and on-target curiosity (they believe it is for them and want what comes next). Everything else in the hook is decoration.

**Why it works:** The continue-or-scroll decision is made in the first seconds, and it can only go your way if both halves are present: the reader has to know what the content is about *and* that it is relevant to them. A line that supplies only one leaves the decision unmade, and the default outcome of an unmade decision is scrolling. Reducing the hook to two named deliverables also turns it from a matter of taste into a pass/fail check, which is what a solo operator with no second reader actually needs.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from having 'studied literally thousands of videos'; creator cites a million followers and billions of views. No data shown.

**Fit here (9/10):** Durable principle class (attention and retention psychology), so the source's recency is doing none of the scoring work. Organic short-form video is a live surface the operator scripts, films and publishes himself with a phone, so a two-item spec on the line he writes and speaks is runnable today at zero cost; the written form re-runs on email subject lines and preheaders and on the first line of Meta primary text for the $30/day giveaway campaign. This is the spec most of the hook rules in this skill exist to serve: the withheld-resolution and named-topic rule above is the constraint that keeps both halves alive, speed-to-value below governs where the clarity half lands, the contrast rule below is the standard engine for the curiosity half, the seven interest components above stack under curiosity, and second-person framing below is what makes the curiosity *on-target* rather than merely interesting. On video the spec has to be satisfied twice, once by the spoken line and once by the on-screen text, per the two-deliverables rule below. Clarity is the half a founder skips, because he already knows what the piece is about — so the working test is to cover everything but the hook and ask whether a stranger could say what this is about and who it is for. The four-point checklist directly below is this spec packaged as a pre-ship filter with two further screens bolted on (audience size and differentiation); run the checklist at draft review and this spec is satisfied as its first two items. Held at 8 because it is a specification rather than a revenue mechanism, and because at ~54 orders/month only organic short-form gives any per-piece signal to check it against; everywhere else it is applied at draft review. Note scope: the spoken version applies to organic video, since the paid side here stays static-first per `marketing-paid-creative-testing`.

## Run every hook through a four-point pre-ship checklist — it must appeal to the ideal customer, trigger curiosity, address a large enough audience, and say or show something different — and rewrite it if it misses any one.

**Why it works:** The hook is the only part of the piece that gets bought in the feed, and each of the four points fails it in a different way: the wrong person stops, the right person stops but is not curious, the audience is too narrow for spend to scale into, or the line blends into every other ad in the category and is read as generic before it is read as anything else. Because the four failures are independent, a fixed pre-launch filter is the only way to catch all of them before money or shooting time is committed — checking by feel reliably catches whichever failure you happened to be thinking about that day and misses the other three.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus the claim that 'this is the filter every $100K+ ad passed' across $4M/month in spend; one worked example ('What would happen to your acne if the oil glands feeding it were shut down for a few days?').

**Fit here (7/10):** A free go/no-go gate the solo operator runs on hooks he is already writing — the spoken and on-screen lines of his own short-form video, the first line of Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines — with no spend, no volume requirement and no second reader, so it is runnable today even with paid paused. Durable-principle class, so the date does nothing to the score. Two of the four points are the hook spec above in different words: 'appeals to the ideal customer' is the on-target half and 'triggers curiosity' is the curiosity half, and neither is additive on its own. What the checklist adds is the packaging — one filter run at a fixed moment beats four principles scattered across a skill — plus the two screens this skill does not otherwise own. Audience size is the screen that kills a hook pinned to a sliver ('if you're switching to natural deodorant before a wedding'); the category is forgiving here, since everyone who wears deodorant is inside the addressable set, so this point usually passes and is cheap to check. Differentiation is the point that actually bites in a category where every hook sounds the same — generate that half from the six-angle menu directly below rather than by staring at the line. One correction to carry over from the spec above: keep topic clarity as an implicit fifth item, because a hook can appeal, intrigue, scale and differ and still never say what it is about. And note that the source's own worked example is a mechanism-efficacy promise ('the oil glands feeding it shut down for a few days') that this catalogue may not make — `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` blocks that shape outright, so borrow the interrogative structure and leave the claim behind.

## Generate the 'different' half of the hook from a fixed menu of six angles: speed of result, the result without the thing they hate, a price reframe of the result, an exaggerated guarantee, a genuinely new mechanism, or dramatised exaggeration of the outcome.

**Why it works:** Differentiation is not a mood you summon, it is a small set of predictable places a claim can be twisted so it stops sounding like every competitor. 'End cigar breath without quitting cigars' works because it removes the sacrifice the buyer assumed was mandatory; each of the other five angles removes a different assumed constraint — the wait, the cost, the risk, the mechanism the category has always used, or the modesty of the outcome. Having the menu in front of you turns 'make it different' from an instruction into six drafts.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus one named winner ('End cigar breath without quitting cigars') and one price-reframe example ('for less than the cost of one premium cigar…').

**Fit here (8/10):** The most directly translatable thing in this batch, because a 12-SKU natural body-care catalogue sits in a category whose hooks are interchangeable. 'The result without the thing they hate' is the natural-deodorant buyer's objection stated as a hook — protection without the baking-soda burn, without aluminium, without reapplying at 3pm — and it is the angle to reach for first. The price reframe is live at a $50.46 AOV (a bar that replaces three products, a stick costed per day). A genuinely new mechanism is available where the formula actually differs, and it must be a real formulation difference, not a renamed commodity. Two angles need governors here. An exaggerated guarantee is only usable if the business will actually honour it — write the guarantee that exists, not the one that sounds good. And dramatised exaggeration runs straight into the understatement rule below and the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`: dramatising a *situation* (the shirt you kept your arms down in) is fine, dramatising an *outcome* is an efficacy claim the health gate will block. The founder writes his own spoken and on-screen hooks, so use the menu on the next batch of organic short-form first, then on Meta primary text and Amazon bullets. Adjacent to the four-predictable-places differentiator rule recorded elsewhere in the marketing catalogue, which operates on the mechanism block; this one operates on the hook line and names two angles that one does not (guarantee exaggeration, dramatisation). It also overlaps the contrast rule below without replacing it: contrast manufactures the *curiosity* half against the buyer's current default, this menu manufactures the *difference* from competitors saying the same thing — the sacrifice-removal angle is where the two coincide.

## When a piece underperforms, diagnose the hook against four named failure modes in order — delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest — instead of guessing at the whole piece.

**Why it works:** Each failure mode blocks a different half of the opt-in decision and has a different fix. Delay and confusion break clarity: the topic arrived too late, or arrived in language the reader could not absorb. Irrelevance and disinterest break curiosity: they understood it and decided it was not for them, or it was for them and they did not want what came next. Naming which one is broken stops you from rewriting the wrong thing — adding suspense to a hook whose actual problem is that the topic shows up three lines in.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, presented as a taxonomy derived from studying thousands of videos.

**Fit here (8/10):** A cheap written debug checklist for content the operator already produces, with zero spend and no dependency on order volume, and durable enough that the source date is moot. It complements the 'run down the production stack before retiring an angle' discipline in `marketing-paid-creative-testing` by giving the hook link four specific, checkable defects rather than one vague one. Every fix is already in this skill: delay → the speed-to-value rule directly below; confusion → the sixth-grade reading level rule, the LLM clarity prompt, the isolation read and the 'not sure what it means' kill-test below; irrelevance → second-person framing below, plus checking the awareness level per `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`; disinterest → the contrast rule below, the six-angle differentiation menu above, the seven interest components above, the power-phrase cloud and the trigger-word edit. One honest limit at this scale: only organic short-form returns enough per-piece retention signal to diagnose against, so on email and Meta the taxonomy is applied at draft review against opens and thumbstop rather than as a post-mortem on real data.

## Fix 'delay' by cutting the fluff lines and moving the topic-introducing sentence to the very top, so the reader knows the subject within the first one to two seconds.

**Why it works:** Short-form retention is exponential decay with the steepest drop in the first two seconds. Every second the reader spends without the context needed to decide, a large share of them are already gone — so the topic sentence has to arrive before the cliff, not after two lines of setup. The fix is almost always subtractive: the topic sentence is usually already written, sitting third.

**Evidence offered:** The retention decay curve is described but not shown; illustrated with a 13-line script where line three is the first mention of the topic. Assertion plus example.

**Fit here (8/10):** Concrete script surgery — reorder the lines, delete the first two — that one person applies to every video he already films, at no cost and with no dependency on volume. Durable principle about attention, so no decay. It transfers directly to the first line of Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, to email subject lines and preview text, and to PDP hero copy. Concrete form: a deodorant video that opens 'so a few years ago I got really into reading ingredient labels…' should open 'your natural deodorant probably quit on you around day four'. Two boundaries. Moving the *topic* up is not moving the *resolution* up — the named-topic rule above wants the subject stated and the answer withheld, and speed-to-value is the commonest excuse an LLM uses to summarise the whole ad in line one. And this is distinct from the mid-action opening below, which decides what kind of sentence line one is; speed-to-value decides only that the topic-bearing sentence occupies that position. It pairs with the two-to-three-line budget below: topic in line one, contrast in lines two and three.

## Fix 'confusion' by writing the hook at roughly a sixth-grade reading level — fewer and simpler words, and active rather than passive voice ('the dog jumped', not 'the jump of the dog').

**Why it works:** A reader who only absorbs half the words cannot assess whether the piece is for them, so a comprehension failure blocks the opt-in even when the topic is technically stated in line one. Raising the share of words that land raises the share of readers who can make the decision at all — clarity is a prerequisite for relevance, not a stylistic preference.

**Evidence offered:** A deliberately mangled example hook rewritten into a clear one, side by side. Assertion plus demonstration.

**Fit here (8/10):** Copy craft the operator applies himself, first to the spoken and on-screen hooks of his own videos and then to Amazon bullets, PDP headlines and email subject lines. Not covered by the understatement rule below, which governs intensity and adjective restraint and says nothing about readability or voice — a hook can be perfectly restrained and still unparseable. This category is where it bites: 'aluminium-free', 'magnesium hydroxide', 'the transition period' are the exact vocabulary a founder reaches for in line one, and they belong in the body. Keep the hook at 'the burning stopped by day four' and let the chemistry arrive in the mechanism beat of the seven-beat sequence below. It binds hardest on the on-screen text hook, which is read in under a second. Held at 7 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review and unmeasurable at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Kallaway — "Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip" (2byPP_9F0-Q)*

## Frame the hook in second person ('if you've struggled with skin problems your whole life…') rather than first person ('I've struggled with skin problems my whole life'), because first person forces the reader to decide whether they resemble you before they can decide the content is relevant.

**Why it works:** A first-person problem statement inserts an identification step: the reader asks 'do I have the same problems this person does?', and any mismatch — age, skin, lifestyle, how they look — discounts the solution before it arrives. Second-person framing removes the step by asserting the relevance directly at the reader, so the only question left is whether they want what comes next.

**Evidence offered:** Paired good/bad example hooks; assertion about the reader's internal question.

**Fit here (8/10):** Directly relevant to a founder-operator who films himself, because his default is to narrate his own deodorant and oral-care story, and this names the specific cost of doing that in the hook position. Runnable today on the videos he already posts, plus Meta primary text and subject lines. It is the standard fix for the 'irrelevance' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the four call-out types in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which name a group rather than choosing a grammatical person, and it deliberately *narrows* the 'write claims as personal experience — how I, not how to' rule recorded there: that rule holds for the body, this one overrides it for the hook. So second person in line one, first person from line two onward. Same narrowing applies to the mid-action opening below — 'I kept my arms down through the whole meeting' becomes 'you know the meeting where you keep your arms down' in the hook position, and the first-person version moves down to beat one of the seven-beat body. It applies to both hook deliverables on a video: the on-screen text as well as the spoken line. One deliberate exception worth keeping straight: the self-exclusion opener below is first person on purpose, but the voice is the *buyer's* ('I'm not a natural-products person'), not the founder's, so it does not trigger a resemblance check — it *is* the resemblance.

## Manufacture the curiosity half with contrast — A is what the reader already believes is the solution, B is your alternative that is faster, better or cheaper — either as stated contrast (name both A and B) or implied contrast (name only B and let the known default stand in for A).

**Why it works:** Setting a comparison against the reader's current baseline re-agitates the pain that baseline failed to solve, and that re-agitation is what produces the urge to keep reading. The gap between A and B *is* the curiosity. Naming B alone is enough when the audience already knows what the default is, because they supply A themselves.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples in a skincare category ('most people solve acne with Accutane, but I have an herbal remedy that does it three times faster' vs 'this herbal supplement is eight times more effective'). Assertion about the subconscious mechanism.

**Fit here (8/10):** The reusable engine for the curiosity half of the hook spec above, and this catalogue has a natural A in every category: conventional aluminium antiperspirant, mainstream toothpaste, drugstore lip balm — and sharpest of all, the natural deodorant that already failed them. It is the standard fix for the 'disinterest' failure mode above. Not a duplicate of the open-loop rule, which says what a loop is rather than how to generate one, and not a duplicate of the compare-and-contrast mechanism claim in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which governs mechanism copy on a page rather than the opening lines of a video. It also overlaps but does not replace the six-angle differentiation menu above: contrast positions you against the buyer's *default*, the menu positions you against *competitors saying the same thing*, and the sacrifice-removal angle is where the two produce the same line. The stated-versus-implied choice is a real per-SKU decision: state A where the buyer has not consciously named their default ('most people fix a smelly shirt by buying a stronger antiperspirant — that's backwards'), imply it where the default is universal ('nothing to reapply at 3pm'). Two hard constraints. Comparative numbers are exactly what this catalogue cannot assert — 'three times faster' and 'eight times more effective' would fail the claim-calibration and specificity rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and the health gate — so the safe form is an attribute or mechanism contrast ('no baking soda, so no day-four burn'), not a quantified efficacy contrast. And A is a hook premise, not a theme: the body still pivots to odour duration, irritation and re-application per the golden-thread rule below, rather than spending its word count litigating aluminium. At headline level this is the same lever as the conflict component in the seven interest components below.

## Treat the hook as two to three lines rather than one punchy line — topic clarity in the first sentence, the contrast set up in the following one or two — and reuse the rare cases where both fit in a single line.

**Why it works:** Clarity and curiosity are two separate jobs and rarely compress into one sentence, so hunting for a single killer line usually means one job gets sacrificed for snappiness. Budgeting two to three lines lets each be done properly, and it is still short enough to land inside the window where the decision is made.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, framed as how he writes hooks tactically.

**Fit here (7/10):** A structural rule for the script the operator writes before filming — it tells him how much of the opening to spend and in what order, which is a decision he makes on every video, at no cost. It also gives a template for the first two lines of Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, and for the subject-line/preheader pair on email (subject carries the topic, preheader carries the contrast). Interactions: speed-to-value above governs line one, the contrast rule above governs lines two and three, and line one still has to withhold the resolution while naming the subject. On video the same split runs across media rather than only across lines — see the two-deliverables rule directly below, where the overlay usually carries the topic and the spoken line carries the contrast. Two boundaries. It sharpens the 90%-effort rule below — the thing you concentrate effort on is two to three lines, not one, and the two primary texts in the 3-2-2 flexible ad should differ across all of them rather than only in the first sentence. And it moves the body's start: beat one of the seven-beat sequence below begins at line three or four, not line two.

## Treat the spoken hook and the on-screen text hook as two separate deliverables for the same video, and generate several options of each plus two full script variants before choosing.

**Why it works:** On a muted-autoplay feed the two do different jobs for different viewers: the text overlay is the only hook a sound-off viewer ever receives, while the first spoken line is what a sound-on viewer decides against. Writing them as one thing means one of the two audiences gets an opening that was not designed for them. Producing three of each and two full script variants also means you are choosing a hook rather than committing to whichever line arrived first, which is the default when hook and script are drafted in a single pass.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated output — three text hook options rendered on a phone mock, three spoken hook options, and two script variants (context-only and research-informed).

**Fit here (7/10):** The founder films, writes and edits his own social video, so producing an overlay line and a spoken line per post costs nothing but a minute of drafting, and it is runnable on the next Reel. Not a duplicate: the two-part hook spec above says what any hook must deliver, the four failure modes diagnose a broken one, the per-image headline rule above covers the static equivalent, and `marketing-paid-creative-testing` covers hook variation counts on paid ads — none of them splits the spoken line from the text overlay as two assets for the same video. Both copies have to satisfy the spec independently: each names its topic, each withholds the resolution, both in second person, and the overlay in particular has to clear the sixth-grade reading level rule above because it is read in a fraction of a second. Do not let them duplicate each other word for word — the workable division here is overlay carries the topic ('the week-two natural deodorant problem'), spoken line carries the contrast ('you probably blamed yourself, not the switch'), which is the same labour split as the subject/preheader pair on email. Scope: organic video only, since the paid side stays static-first per `marketing-paid-creative-testing`. Held at 7 because it is a production discipline judged at draft review, and because only organic short-form returns per-piece retention signal to tell you which of the options was right.

## Build headlines from the seven components a meta-analysis of news found drive interest — recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing — and include at least two in every headline.

**Why it works:** The reader uses the headline to estimate the likelihood of being rewarded, compared against every competing alternative in the feed or inbox. These seven components are the empirically recurring reasons a headline wins that comparison, so stacking two or more raises the pick rate rather than relying on a single lever.

**Evidence offered:** Cited as a meta-analysis of news headlines, with an illustrative example per component. No ad-specific spend or conversion data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle, and the most concretely usable thing in this skill because it is a checklist rather than a taste judgement: run every Meta primary text and headline on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, every email subject line, and every Amazon title/bullet angle against the seven and confirm at least two are present. Distinct from the verbalization-transform list in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, which transforms a claim you have already fixed, and from the six headline jobs in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, which selects the intent the headline serves — this supplies the interest components you stack once intent and claim are chosen. It is also the generator to reach for when producing the eight per-image candidates above. Category note: celebrity is unavailable to a solo brand with no roster, so the workable stacks here are relevancy + unusual ('the natural deodorant rule that only applies in week two'), proximity + ongoing (a local, still-running giveaway), and conflict + recency (baking soda versus sensitive skin, framed as a current argument) — and note that the conflict component is the headline-level form of the A-versus-B contrast rule above. Two components is the floor and roughly the ceiling — a headline carrying five reads as hype and fails the understatement rule below. Whatever you stack, the headline still has to name its subject and withhold the resolution — and stacking an unusual or conflict component does not license that component to become the ad's theme, per the golden-thread rule above.
