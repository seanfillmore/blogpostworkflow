# Conversion Copy Angles

## Hold the claim fixed and generate headline variants by running it through a checklist of verbalization transforms — measure its size, measure its speed, compare it, metaphorize it, sensitize it, demonstrate it, dramatize it, state it as a paradox, remove a limitation from it, state it as a question, offer information about how to accomplish it, tie authority to it, put it before-and-after, name the problem it solves, turn it into a case history, or warn about the pitfall of not acting.

**Why it works:** The claim decides whether the ad can work at all; the verbalization decides whether the claim gets read. Because the transforms are mechanical, they turn 'write another headline' from a blank-page problem into a checklist, and each transform binds a different secondary image to the same underlying claim — so you get variety of expression without drifting onto a different, weaker claim.

**Evidence offered:** A list of roughly two dozen transforms, each with one or two real 1950s–60s headlines as examples ('I am 61 pounds lighter', 'Six times whiter washes!', 'Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery!', 'Who else wants a whiter wash, with no hard work?'). Illustrative only, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (claim construction), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. Directly useful this week: the 3-2-2 flexible ad at $30/day needs two meaningfully different headlines and two primary texts, and this generates them from one already-chosen claim — which also makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings, and is the headline-level complement to the concentrate-effort-on-concept-and-hook rule below. Same use for email subject lines and Amazon bullet leads. The claim/expression split is the additive part: this skill owns many individual transforms (authority attribution, before/after contrast, case history, the question opener, the pitfall warning) but nothing owned the discipline of fixing the claim first and varying only its expression. Held at 6 because that overlap is substantial and because the register of the examples is a live hazard — 'melts away ugly fat' is exactly the phrasing that becomes an unsupportable efficacy claim in body care, so every output must clear the specificity rule above, the body-care claim rules, and the understatement rule below.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Call your audience out by name in the copy — self-selection is a targeting mechanic, not just a stylistic choice.

**Why it works:** Naming the reader makes them stop and identify, which filters in the right buyer and filters out the wrong one before they cost you anything.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle, executable solo at zero cost on PDP headlines, Amazon bullets, and email subject lines. Especially relevant for natural deodorant where the buyer is a self-identified group (people who switched off aluminum, people whose natural deodorant failed them). Its close kin is the agreement-opener rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`: this one names *who* the reader is, that one gets them to concede *what they are putting up with*. The disqualification rule directly below is the negative form of the same mechanic — it dismisses the wrong reader rather than naming the right one.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Disqualify the wrong reader up front — say plainly that if a stated condition is true of them they should stop reading right now, that this is not for everyone, that it will demand something of them.

**Why it works:** Naming who the offer is *not* for makes the reader run the qualification test themselves. Those who fail it leave, costing nothing later in refunds and complaints; those who pass have just privately argued themselves into being the right person, which is a stronger commitment than a claim handed to them. Inviting the wrong reader to leave also signals a seller who is not needy, which raises perceived value, and it reads as candour rather than salesmanship, so the claims that follow are evaluated rather than pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated rather than taught: 'if you know in your heart of hearts that you have already achieved everything you're capable of achieving — then please stop reading this report right now. There's nothing here that will help you.' And later: 'this program is definitely NOT for everyone', 'you'll need a pretty thick skin because I'm going to tear your beloved business to shreds', 'securing a place will NOT be cheap'. No test, no conversion, refund or take-rate figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), so the 2008 date is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, and it lands on live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject and opening lines, the PDP hero, and the non-buyer nurture flow. The honest form here is real rather than manufactured — natural deodorant genuinely is not for someone who wants sweat stopped, and the two-week transition genuinely defeats people who will not ride it out, so saying that plainly filters out the buyer most likely to conclude the product failed, which is the exact churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Held at 5 because it sits close to the call-out-by-name rule directly above and to the admit-flaws rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` (that one confesses a limitation of the product, this one dismisses a reader from buying at all, before any claim is asserted). Must stay honest rather than becoming false scarcity of attention on a $12 consumable, and **scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy. At ~54 orders/month no version of the opening line will ever be measurably better.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 1 of 6)*

## Audit every asset against five human needs — encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions, help them throw rocks at their enemies — and make sure at least one is doing real work, then work the missing ones in.

**Why it works:** Persuasion bonds form when a deep emotional need is met, not when an argument is well constructed. These five needs are what people reward others for meeting, and they recur behind every successful campaign and every strong relationship, so an asset that hits none of them is asking the reader to be convinced by logic alone — the weakest available lever. Because each lever is a different emotional job, running them as a checklist surfaces the ones a draft has silently skipped and stops a writer defaulting to the same one or two angles they are comfortable with.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus a challenge ('try to find a truly successful ad campaign that does not use one or more of these five'), a worked teardown of the Pelmanism sales letter showing three of the five stacked in the opening paragraphs with an unverified '$9,000 almost instantly' figure, and a self-demonstration in which the author re-reads his own three opening paragraphs and labels which lever each sentence performs, arguing they worked precisely because they never read as technique. Illustrative only; no controlled comparison, no split test, no performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), so the 2005 date is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, and it works as a coverage check across surfaces already live: PDP body copy, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks, the post-purchase and non-buyer nurture emails, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign. Its real value is as a generator that widens the angle set beyond the two this catalogue already leans on — natural deodorant copy defaults to fear (aluminium) and dream (confidence), while 'justify their failures' and 'confirm their suspicions' are wide open and map exactly onto a buyer whose last natural deodorant quit by noon. Use it as the index into the rest of this skill: **dreams** are the primal-desire headline rule below, **fears** are negative framing plus the fear-handling rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, **failures** are the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule beside it, and **suspicions** and **enemies** are the two sections directly below. This audit checks emotional coverage; the three-demands audit further down checks whether proof and mechanism arrive where the reader asks for them — run both on the same draft. Held at 7 because three of the five levers are already owned elsewhere, it is a checklist rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is the author grading his own essay, and at ~54 orders/month nothing it produces will ever be measurably better than what it replaced. Every 'suspicion' confirmed and every 'enemy' named must be true and defensible under the existing body-care claim rules.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Lean into the taboo — say the thing competitors are too polite to say.

**Why it works:** Naming the uncomfortable reality both stops the scroll and signals that you actually understand the buyer's lived experience, creating identification at a deeper level than benefit copy.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, reinforced in the demo where she argues Dr. Squatch is 'playing it very clean and very safe' and should go more visceral on gunky pits and pit stains.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle and unusually well-matched to the category: deodorant, body odor, and oral care are inherently taboo-adjacent, and the competitors named in this very video (Native, Every Man Jack, Harry's) are the ones playing it safe. Free to execute on PDP, Amazon, and email copy. Its close kin is the suspicion-confirmation rule directly below: taboo says the uncomfortable thing nobody says, suspicion-confirmation says the thing the buyer already half-believes but has never seen stated. Use taboo when you want a scroll-stop, suspicion when you want credibility.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Confirm the suspicion the reader already half-holds — state plainly the thing they suspect but have not seen said out loud.

**Why it works:** A suspicion the reader is already carrying costs nothing to install and can't be argued with, because they got there themselves. Confirming it makes the reader feel astute rather than sold to — the 'I knew it' surge — and they credit the confirmer with the good feeling. It also converts a private hunch into a shared belief, which is the fastest route to trust.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with historical and cult examples (Hitler confirming German suspicions about the cause of their troubles; cults confirming that prospects' families are sabotaging them), plus the Pelmanism letter confirming that an answer to the reader's dilemma exists. No commercial data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Genuinely additive: nothing else here owns 'say the thing the buyer already suspects'. Natural deodorant has a dense supply of true, already-held suspicions in the review corpus and voice-of-customer file — that most 'natural' deodorants are the same base with a different scent, that the transition period is real and nobody warns you, that baking soda is what burns. Stating one plainly on the PDP or as a Meta primary-text opener costs nothing and buys credibility a claim cannot. Held at 6 because it is close kin to the taboo rule above, the source's examples are demagogic rather than commercial, and in body care a 'suspicion' about a competitor's formulation is a disparagement risk unless it is a defensible fact — **confirm suspicions about the category and the experience, not about named competitors.**

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Name a shared enemy and help the reader throw rocks at it — but make the enemy a condition, never a competitor.

**Why it works:** Everyone you are selling to is fighting something — a person, a group, an illness, a setback, a rival philosophy. Someone who joins that fight stops being a vendor and becomes a partner, which is a materially different relationship from a satisfied-customer one. Refusing to name the enemy leaves that bond available to whoever will.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with a hedge that the author knows how ugly it sounds ('rest assured that people have enemies. All people'), plus the aphorism that everyone you meet is engaged in a great struggle. No examples from advertising, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free. The honest enemy for this catalogue is not a competitor but a condition: aluminium antiperspirants, baking-soda irritation, the ruined-shirt afternoon, the two weeks nobody warns you about. Framing the brand as the thing that fights alongside the buyer against that is available on the PDP hero, the Meta primary text going live now, and the founder-voice emails, and it is one of the few angles that suits a small brand better than it suits Native or Dr. Squatch. Held at 6 because `marketing-product-image-stack` already owns the us-vs-them comparison frame and `marketing-offer-construction` owns the differentiator audit — the additive part is the emotional register (join the fight) rather than the comparison table — and because the enemy must stay a category condition: disparaging another brand's formulation in body care is a claims and legal problem, not a creative choice. Write these lines in the first person plural (see the pronoun rule below), which is what keeps 'we're up against X' from reading as an attack. The contrast rule directly below is the argumentative counterpart: same refusal to name a competitor, aimed at the product class rather than at a condition.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Eliminate the alternatives with sharp explicit contrast — and aim it at the product class the buyer already owns, not at a named competitor. Generate on four axes: before/after, without your solution/with it, you/competitors, now/later.

**Why it works:** When a prospect cannot easily see the difference between your product and the thing already in their bathroom cabinet, the decision stalls and the default wins. A stated contrast lets them reach the superiority conclusion themselves — which they believe far more than a claim handed to them — and gives the deciding brain two concrete things to choose between rather than one thing to evaluate in the abstract.

**Evidence offered:** Two Billy Mays contrast lines (Grater Plater vs food processor and garlic press; Quick Chop vs a knife), plus an unsourced appeal to 'experts in Neuromarketing.' No figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, editorial. The additive part is specific and useful: the contrast target is the product class the buyer already owns — a conventional aluminium antiperspirant, or the natural stick that quit by noon — rather than a named brand, which is exactly the constraint this catalogue operates under, since disparaging Native's or Dr. Squatch's formulation is a claims and legal risk. The four-axis list is a cheap generator for PDP sections, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three recorded rules: the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, the us-vs-them graphic capped at three or four attributes in `marketing-product-image-stack`, and the shared-enemy-as-a-condition rule directly above. This rule chooses the axis; the rhythm rule directly below governs how the two halves are arranged on the page. Anything it generates must still be a defensible formulation fact.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Deliver that contrast in an alternating 'bad… good… bad… good' rhythm — name what the product class the buyer already owns does badly, immediately follow with what yours does instead, and choose weak-sounding words for that side and powerful ones for yours.

**Why it works:** Concentration is not just criticism: pointing out a weakness and then, in the same breath, proving your product delivers the same want without it makes the reader draw the superiority conclusion themselves. Alternating the two keeps each contrast adjacent so the comparison is unavoidable, and deliberate word selection widens the felt gap without adding a new claim.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with one illustrative structure ('competitor's product does this badly… mine however does this') and a compressed single-phrase example, 'SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY'. No test data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. The additive part is narrow but real: the contrast rule directly above supplies four axes and the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` supplies a one-line form — neither owns the alternating rhythm or the contrastive-vocabulary instruction. Natural fit for a PDP section or A+ block where 'baking soda burns / ours does not' is the whole argument. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three existing rules and because the hard constraint here inverts the source: the target must stay the product class the buyer already owns, never a named competitor, and every 'bad' half must be a defensible formulation fact rather than a chosen adjective. The word-selection half also has to survive the understatement rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — pick the accurate word, not the loudest one.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Write to a primal desire (status, sex, belonging, safety, approval) and treat the product as the mechanism, not the headline.

**Why it works:** People buy the end state, not the feature; the product is only the route to the desire, so the desire belongs in the headline and the feature belongs in the supporting line.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion; framed as one of the recurring patterns across all her winning creative, video and static.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable positioning principle, free to apply. This is the 'encourage their dreams' lever of the five-needs audit above, and the reader-centred half of the strip-yourself-out audit below. Scored slightly lower because natural body care sits closest to 'safety' and 'approval' and the risk of overreach into unsupportable claims is real — but reframing 'aluminum-free' as a safety/confidence outcome is straightforward PDP and Amazon work. The format-specific hard version of this rule — keeping the product out of the opening line entirely — is the product-out-of-the-hook rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`, and applies only to organic-styled social copy, not to Amazon or the PDP.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Strip yourself out of the draft — audit a finished piece for sentences about your wants, your offer mechanics, your story or what you think.

**Why it works:** Attention is legible. Copy organised around the seller's intentions reads as someone talking past the reader, and no amount of solid argument or polish repairs that. Copy organised entirely around the reader's needs creates a natural desire to reciprocate, and the seller's own goals then get expressed and met incidentally rather than having to be pushed. Pushing produces resistance; meeting a need almost never does.

**Evidence offered:** The observation that the one-sentence framework contains no reference to the persuader at all, plus a thought experiment (a conversation where the argument is perfect but you are staring at the wall behind the person). Assertion and analogy, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy discipline), age irrelevant, free, and runnable as a literal draft-review pass: highlight every sentence in a PDP section, Amazon bullet set or email that is about the brand's story, the offer mechanics or what the founder thinks, and check whether the reader gets anything from it. That is a real catch for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which reliably produces brand-voice throat-clearing before it gets to the reader. Capped at 5 because it overlaps the primal-desire rule above and the product-out-of-the-hook rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — so the additive part is only the whole-draft audit. Hold it in tension with the 'how I' framing rule below: first-person *experience* offered as evidence for the reader is not the same thing as first-person throat-clearing, and only the second is what this audit strips. It also needs one scope limit: **an Amazon title and bullet set must state what the product is immediately, so the audit governs proportion and emphasis, not the removal of product facts.**

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Use negative framing — name what the audience is afraid of rather than only what they'll gain.

**Why it works:** Fear and avoidance language cuts through where benefit language blends in; naming the feared outcome proves you understand the stakes.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable copy principle, zero cost. Fits the category (aluminum, ruined shirts, natural deodorant that quits by noon) and is testable on Amazon bullets and PDP copy without any ad spend or traffic. Treat as the alternative opening to transformation-first, not a companion to it — running both in one headline dilutes each. Note also that this names a *feared future* outcome; the prior-attempt walkthrough below names an *already-experienced* failure, which is the version to reach for once the buyer has tried and been let down. Naming the fear is only half the job: the fear-handling rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` governs how you put it down again, and 'don't worry' does not count.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Never publicise that many people do the thing you don't want — state the desired behaviour as the norm instead.

**Why it works:** One of the best predictors of an individual's behaviour is what the people around them are doing. Announcing that an undesirable behaviour is widespread licenses it, because the reader concludes 'everyone else is doing it, so I can too'. The same social-proof mechanism runs in reverse when the stated norm is the behaviour you actually want.

**Evidence offered:** The Cialdini-sourced IRS anecdote: the IRS publicly announced that too many people were cheating on taxes and raised penalties, and tax cheating then rose rather than fell. Plus the 'urinal rule' illustration. Secondhand anecdote, no figures, no test.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It lands on a live temptation: the transition-period copy on the PDP, in the A+ block and in post-purchase emails is exactly where a solo operator drafting with an LLM will write 'most people give up in week two' — which normalises quitting at the precise churn point driving the 18–22.5% repeat rate. The corrected form states the persistence norm ('most people are through it by around day ten') and is fine to ship if it is true. Hold the tension with the fear-handling and admit-flaws rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`: naming the rough patch honestly is required, but stating a quit rate as a norm is not the same thing and is what this prohibits. Capped at 5 because it sits close to the suspicion-confirmation and believability rules, the evidence is a single secondhand anecdote, and no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better at this volume.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 5 of 6)*

## Walk the buyer through what they have already tried and how it went — then associate the parts they liked with your solution and the parts they disliked with the specific reason it failed and why yours is different.

**Why it works:** Prior failed attempts are where the buyer's frustration already lives, so surfacing them widens the felt gap between where they are and where they want to be, which is the motivation to act. Splitting the prior attempt into 'the part that was right' and 'the part that broke' lets you inherit the credibility of the category while attributing the failure to a named cause your product removes — so you are not asking them to abandon the belief that got them here, only the product that let them down.

**Evidence offered:** Described as the 'pain cycle' step of his CLOSER framework, refined over 4,000+ consults: 'what have you done before, how'd that work out for you' repeated to keep expanding the gap. Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable persuasion principle, age irrelevant, and it has an unusually exact form here: the natural-deodorant buyer has almost always tried another natural deodorant that quit by noon or burned with baking soda. Copy that says 'you were right to get off aluminium — the problem was X, and here is what we do instead' does both jobs at once and is free to write on the PDP, in Amazon bullets and in the non-buyer nurture flow. In a saturated category this is also the natural vehicle for the named mechanism the sophistication rule demands. Held at 6 because it partially overlaps negative framing above and transformation-first ordering in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — treat it as the body-copy expansion of whichever opening you chose, not a third competing headline — and because attributing a competitor's failure to a specific cause in body care must stay to defensible formulation facts, not invented ones. The blame-absolution rule directly below is its front end: this one blames the *previous product*, that one absolves the *buyer*, and it fixes placement at the very top of the piece. The believability rule two entries down is its mirror image: this one names the *competitor's* flaw, that one names *yours*, and a body section that does both reads as honest rather than as a hit piece.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

## Absolve the buyer of blame for their last failure in the opening lines — attribute it to a specific, truthful external cause outside their control ('it may not be your fault, it may be your metabolism').

**Why it works:** A reader who has already failed at this category is carrying private self-blame, and self-blame is both a barrier to hearing anything else and the reason they expect to fail again. Naming an external, factual cause hands them a scapegoat, which is itself something of value delivered before any claim is made, so the reason to expect another failure is removed and a bond forms with whoever removed it. Placing it in the first lines is what buys attention for everything after it. The truth constraint separates this from manipulation: depression genuinely can be a chemical imbalance, obesity genuinely can be metabolic, so the scapegoat is a real explanation rather than a flattering lie.

**Evidence offered:** Two US TV direct-response openings quoted or paraphrased (antidepressant: 'it may be the result of a chemical imbalance in your brain'; weight loss: 'it may not be your fault, it may be your metabolism'), the Pelmanism letter's 'you've done everything that you can... but there's still something holding you back', and a landscaper anecdote in which attributing a client's ugly yard to drought and bad soil 'increased significantly' the number of clients he secured. Teardowns and anecdote; no figures, no performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. This is the 'justify their failures' lever of the five-needs audit above, and the fit is unusually exact because the true scapegoat already exists: the natural-deodorant buyer's private belief is almost always self-blaming — 'I sweat more than most people', 'nothing works on me' — and the review corpus surfaces those lines verbatim, while the real causes are that baking soda irritates a large share of skin and nearly every competitor ships without warning that the transition takes two weeks. 'You didn't fail at going aluminium-free — the baking soda burned, and that is a formulation problem, not you' is literally defensible and is the exact opening the non-buyer nurture flow and the transition-period PDP section need. It lands on acquisition surfaces (PDP hero, Amazon bullets, Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway) and, more valuably, the post-purchase transition emails, where a week-two buyer concluding 'this failed because of me' is the exact churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Capped at 7 because it partially overlaps the prior-attempt walkthrough above; the additive part is narrow but real — that walkthrough blames the previous product, this absolves the buyer, and it fixes placement at the top rather than in the body. Every scapegoat named must clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before it ships.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Pitch exactly three differentiating pillars, each one or two sentences, each explained by an analogy to something the buyer already understands — and keep the pitch about their destination, not the product's internals.

**Why it works:** A long monologue about product detail loses the sale because the buyer cares about their own problem, not your mechanism. Capping it at three pillars keeps the argument holdable, and an analogy transfers understanding by mapping the unfamiliar onto something familiar, so a mechanism claim becomes believable without a technical explanation the buyer has to work through.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples — SEO explained as a paycheck versus an investment account that compounds, and a mortgage-broker lead script built on exactly three pillars (exclusive, timely, qualified). Practitioner assertion from his own scripts, no conversion data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable copy-construction principle, age irrelevant, and free to apply to PDP body copy, Amazon A+ blocks and the non-buyer nurture emails. The three-pillar cap fits how much a shopper actually reads and lines up with the existing 'cap the us-vs-them graphic at three or four attributes you win on' rule, so it reinforces rather than contradicts. The analogy device is the additive part — nothing else in the copy skills covers explaining a mechanism by comparison, and natural deodorant needs exactly that for the transition period and for why an aluminium-free formula works differently. Held at 6 because analogies in body care slide easily into implied physiological claims, so each one has to be paired with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and kept descriptive, and at ~54 orders/month the choice of three pillars is judged rather than tested.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

## Write the way people actually talk — 'just you and me' copy addressed to an old friend, informal, tolerant of the odd grammar mistake, in the voice of someone who actually uses the product rather than the voice of a brand.

**Why it works:** Informality signals a peer rather than a seller, so the reader drops the ad-avoidance posture and reads the piece as a recommendation. Copy written in a spoken register also carries the reader forward line to line, where formal marketing prose gives them a clean place to stop.

**Evidence offered:** Carlton's own retrospective quotes from Volume 1 of his Collected Letters ('the letter is written the way people talk', 'talks just like a real golfer'), plus an LLM-written yapper script as an illustration of the same register in a modern format. Practitioner assertion, no conversion data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable craft rule about register, age irrelevant, and free. Genuinely additive: this skill owns specificity, taboo framing, hook construction and attribution, but nothing else owns the *register* the copy is written in, and a solo operator drafting with an LLM will default to polished brand prose that reads as advertising. Natural homes are the founder-voice email sends, the Meta primary text going live now, and PDP body copy — all surfaces where a peer voice is credible for a small natural body-care brand in a way it is not for Native. Read a draft aloud; if you would not say it to a friend, rewrite it. **Scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy at thumbnail speed rather than conversational prose. Held at 6 because it is a craft rule rather than a revenue mechanism and will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month. ## Write the adversarial and problem-solving lines in the first person plural — 'we're about to take aim' rather than 'I'm about to take aim'.

**Why it works:** Pronoun choice assigns sides. 'I' puts the writer on one side of the transaction and the reader on the other; 'we' places both on the same side of the problem, so the same sentence reads as shared cause rather than a pitch — which is what makes the shared-adversary lever land instead of sounding like a competitor attack.

**Evidence offered:** The author points at two of his own sentences and notes the deliberate 'we' in each, stating it 'helps assure readers that I'm on their side.' Pure self-annotation, no comparison against an 'I' version and no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable craft rule about register, age irrelevant, free, and it takes seconds to apply. Genuinely additive in a narrow direction: the spoken peer register above owns *how* the copy sounds, but nothing owns the pronoun as an alliance signal, which is what keeps a competitor-contrast or shared-enemy sentence from reading as a hit piece for a small brand with no authority to attack Native or Dr. Squatch. Natural homes are founder-voice emails, the non-buyer nurture flow, and Meta primary text on the live campaign. Capped at 5 because it is a one-word craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it substantially overlaps the peer-register rule it sits beside, and it must be scoped away from Amazon titles and bullets, which need scannable claim copy rather than conversational solidarity. Note the division of labour with the 'how I' rule directly below: 'we' is for the lines where you and the reader face a shared condition, 'I' is for the lines where you are reporting your own experience as evidence — what neither should become is 'you should'.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 2 of 2)*

## Frame the claim as experience rather than prescription — 'how I' not 'how to', 'my favourite way' not 'the best way'. Report what you did and what happened; do not tell the reader what they should do.

**Why it works:** An experience claim cannot be argued with — you are the only authority on what you did — so it is credibility-proof and needs no track record to support it. A prescriptive claim from someone with no established credentials reads as preachy and hands the reader an obvious rebuttal ('says who?'), which turns a persuasion problem into an argument.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with three paired before/after rewrites (oatmeal, agency, lead gen). No test data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable craft rule about framing, age irrelevant, free and purely editorial, and unusually well-matched to a founder-operated body-care brand with no lab or clinical credentials. 'How I got through the two-week armpit detox' is defensible where 'the best way to detox your armpits' invites a fight in the comments and edges toward an efficacy claim. Natural homes are the founder-voice emails, the Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, PDP body copy, and the post-purchase transition sequence — where an experiential account of week two is exactly what a wavering buyer needs and where a prescriptive instruction reads as the brand defending itself. It is genuinely additive: the peer-register and pronoun rules above cover *tone* and *whose side you are on*, and the persona rule below covers *consistency*, but none of them own experiential versus prescriptive framing. It also does real compliance work — reporting an experience is a weaker and safer form than asserting a general rule about bodies, though it still must clear the specificity and body-care claim rules, and a first-person anecdote cannot smuggle in a claim the formulation cannot support. **Scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need direct claim copy rather than narrated experience. Held at 7 because it is a framing habit rather than a revenue mechanism and will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 6 of 16)*

## Hold one persona constant across every surface — same voice, same reason-for-formulating story, same signature framing — because consistency is itself a sales asset.

**Why it works:** Repeated exposure produces familiarity, familiarity produces trust, and a pitch never stands on its own: the odds of a sale rise sharply when some relationship exists before the pitch arrives. Signature elements — the voice, the recurring story, the same way of framing the problem — are what let scattered exposures accumulate into one recognisable person rather than several forgettable ones. The same pitch from a familiar figure outsells the identical pitch from an equally skilled unknown.

**Evidence offered:** The Pitchmen Grater Plater split test: Mays and Sullivan pitched the same product, same clothes, same lines, same scenes and camera angles, and Mays's version brought in 40% more revenue over a week of TV testing. One test, self-reported through a TV show, no spend or sample detail.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning and trust), age irrelevant, free, and it is the rare item in this catalogue with a real controlled comparison behind it. The honest solo-operator form: the founder is the only persona available, so hold the same voice, the same reason-for-formulating-baking-soda-free story and the same signature framing constant across the Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, the founder-voice emails and the PDP — rather than writing each surface in a different register. Held at 5 because it substantially overlaps the spoken peer register above and the authority-attribution lever in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` (the additive part is only that consistency *across surfaces* compounds), and because the evidence is a celebrity effect built over years of national TV exposure, which a sub-1,000-subscriber list and ~54 orders/month cannot reproduce. **Scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy rather than a persona. The mascot rule directly below is the non-human version of the same compounding-recognition idea, and a far weaker one here.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Consider a mascot as a permanent spokesperson — a recurring character never ages, never asks for more money and never takes days off (Geico Gecko, Tony the Tiger, the Michelin Man).

**Why it works:** A recurring non-human character accumulates recognition across every asset and every year, giving the brand a consistent attention-grabbing face without a person's cost, availability or aging. It is the same compounding-familiarity mechanism as the persona rule above, detached from any individual.

**Evidence offered:** Named brand examples only (Geico Gecko, Tony the Tiger, the Michelin Man). No data, no comparison against a person-fronted brand.

**Fit here (4/10):** Executable now at near-zero cost — the existing AI product imagery workflow can generate a consistent character from reference images and reuse it across the PDP, A+ blocks and Meta creative — but low leverage. A recognition asset pays back over years of repeated exposure, and the binding constraint here is retention at an 18–22.5% repeat rate, which a mascot does nothing for; at ~$2,700/mo there is no exposure volume for recognition to compound against, and no version of it will be measurable. Held at 4: **do this only as a side effect of imagery work already happening**, never as a standalone project, and never in place of the founder persona above, which is the credible face for a small natural body-care brand and the one the peer-register and 'how I' framing rules depend on. A mascot cannot make a claim the formulation does not support, and cartoon framing must not imply an efficacy or safety result.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 8 of 16)*

## Give the reader a numbered list of concrete symptoms and a scoring threshold — 'if six or more of these sound like you, the diagnosis is clear' — so they diagnose themselves rather than being told they have the problem.

**Why it works:** A conclusion the reader reaches by tallying their own behaviour is believed far more than the same conclusion asserted by the seller, and cannot be argued with. The checklist also forces active engagement item by item, and converts an abstract problem into a set of specific recognisable situations — so the reader arrives at the offer having already conceded the premise.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated rather than taught: a twelve-item 'leisure phobia' checklist with a stated six-item threshold, plus a shorter self-question stack ('Does your current level of success equal your potential?'). No performance data, no comparison against a straight assertion of the same problem.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It is a concrete artifact this catalogue can build honestly, and the raw material already exists in the review corpus and voice-of-customer file: 'six signs your natural deodorant quit on you' or 'four signs you're still in the transition, not failing at it' — the second aimed squarely at the week-two churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate, and belonging in the post-purchase flow rather than acquisition. Held at 5 because it overlaps the 'how to know if…' problem-aware stem in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, the suspicion-confirmation rule above, and the problem-articulation rule directly below; the additive part is the scored, tallied checklist with a stated threshold, which none of those own. Every symptom must be something a real reviewer said, and none may imply a health condition.
