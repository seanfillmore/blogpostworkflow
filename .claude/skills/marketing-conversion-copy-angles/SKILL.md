# Conversion Copy Angles

## Write three distinct copy angles for every offer — a human-driven narrative told from your own perspective, an evidence-driven version built on data and proof, and a direct-response version that names the problem, attaches real numbers and pitches — and run them against each other on your pages.

**Why it works:** The three angles persuade by different routes — identification, proof and urgency — and a market buys on one of them more readily than the others. Writing all three covers the routes instead of betting the whole page on the one the writer happens to find natural, and because each is a full draft of the same offer rather than a reworded headline, the comparison is between genuinely different arguments.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion; references the author's own prior videos on the same three angles. No conversion data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable generation frame, age irrelevant, and it costs nothing but three drafting passes — no production, no new claims, no spend. Executable today on the giveaway entry page, the deodorant PDP and the 3-2-2 flexible ad's two primary texts. It is orthogonal to the rest of this skill: awareness-level and creative-job rules choose *what* to say, the matrix and headline transforms below vary *how a line is stated*, and this one fixes three whole-asset registers to draft in parallel. It also indexes cleanly onto rules already here — the human-narrative angle is the spoken peer register plus the personal-experience phrasing below, the evidence angle is the specificity and proof rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, and the direct-response angle is the call-out, contrast and negative-framing rules below. Held at 7 because at ~54 orders/month the three drafts will never be significance-tested against each other: read any difference as directional and pick the one you can write with real proof behind it, which for a founder-run brand with no lab will usually be the human narrative. Every number in the evidence and direct-response versions must be a defensible figure under the body-care claim rules, and **scope the human-narrative angle away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy.

*Source: orenmeetsworld — "The only funnels guide you'll ever need (Marketing 2.0)" (-8YiaBpl3DU)*

## Hold the claim fixed and generate headline variants by running it through a checklist of verbalization transforms — measure its size, measure its speed, compare it, metaphorize it, sensitize it, demonstrate it, dramatize it, state it as a paradox, remove a limitation from it, state it as a question, offer information about how to accomplish it, tie authority to it, put it before-and-after, name the problem it solves, turn it into a case history, or warn about the pitfall of not acting.

**Why it works:** The claim decides whether the ad can work at all; the verbalization decides whether the claim gets read. Because the transforms are mechanical, they turn 'write another headline' from a blank-page problem into a checklist, and each transform binds a different secondary image to the same underlying claim — so you get variety of expression without drifting onto a different, weaker claim.

**Evidence offered:** A list of roughly two dozen transforms, each with one or two real 1950s–60s headlines as examples ('I am 61 pounds lighter', 'Six times whiter washes!', 'Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery!', 'Who else wants a whiter wash, with no hard work?'). Illustrative only, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (claim construction), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. Directly useful this week: the 3-2-2 flexible ad at $30/day needs two meaningfully different headlines and two primary texts, and this generates them from one already-chosen claim — which also makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings, and is the headline-level complement to the concentrate-effort-on-concept-and-hook rule below. Same use for email subject lines and Amazon bullet leads. The claim/expression split is the additive part: this skill owns many individual transforms (authority attribution, before/after contrast, case history, the question opener, the pitfall warning) but nothing owned the discipline of fixing the claim first and varying only its expression. Note the division of labour with the two rules that bracket it: the three-angle rule above fixes which whole-asset register a draft is written in, the matrix rule directly below chooses *which* angle a line takes, and this one varies *how* a chosen claim is stated. Held at 6 because that overlap is substantial and because the register of the examples is a live hazard — 'melts away ugly fat' is exactly the phrasing that becomes an unsupportable efficacy claim in body care, so every output must clear the specificity rule above, the body-care claim rules, and the understatement rule below.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Generate ad variations by crossing a matrix instead of hunting for new concepts: every value element (What) × whose perspective experiences it (Who — the buyer, their partner, their coworkers) × the time horizon it lands over (When — today, this week, this year).

**Why it works:** Combinatorics. A small fixed set of offer facts produces dozens of distinct, non-repetitive, concrete claims when systematically crossed, so the copy can answer the prospect's question at the moment they think it, in their own words, instead of restating one abstract benefit in five slightly different ways. The matrix also removes the blank page: you are filling cells, not inventing concepts.

**Evidence offered:** Presented as the author's standing production method built on the value equation; Ogilvy's 'the customer isn't a moron, she's your wife' cited as supporting authority. Practitioner assertion, no performance data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable generative framework, age irrelevant, and it hits the exact bottleneck for a solo operator: the 3-2-2 flexible ad needs multiple primary texts now, and the standing experimental slot needs a supply of angles indefinitely. Cost is an hour with a spreadsheet. It is genuinely additive rather than a restatement of the value-equation audit in `marketing-offer-construction` — that one *scores* an offer, this one *manufactures copy lines* from an offer already fixed. The two axes with the most unused surface here get their own enumerated rules directly below: the Who axis becomes the status-conferrer list, the When axis becomes the past/present/future timeline. Also feeds email subject lines, PDP body sections and A+ blocks. Held short of 9 only because every cell still has to clear the body-care claim rules and the understatement rule before it ships, half the cells will be duplicates once written out, and at ~54 orders/month you will never learn which cell won — this is a generator for judged selection, not a test plan.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 8 of 16))*

## Inventory eight ad angles per offer by writing each of the four value drivers in both directions — the dream outcome AND the nightmare of going without, the likelihood of success AND the risk of doing nothing, the speed AND how slow their current trajectory is, the ease AND the work they'll keep spending without you.

**Why it works:** Toward-pleasure and away-from-pain are separate motivators, and a writer who works only the positive half of the value equation leaves half the available motivational surface unwritten. Mirroring each driver doubles the angle inventory from the same offer facts without inventing a single new claim.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, illustrated with weight-loss and business examples. No test data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable copy principle, age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. The nightmare half is largely unwritten in this catalogue today and the raw material is sitting in the review corpus: reapplying at noon, the ruined shirt, the rash from a baking-soda stick, the two weeks nobody warned you about. It supplies the four negative cells directly to the multiple primary texts a 3-2-2 flexible ad needs, and to Amazon bullets and PDP sections. It partially overlaps the negative-framing rule below, but that one is a stylistic instruction about naming a fear while this is a systematic eight-cell generation checklist — use this to decide *which* fear, that one to decide whether the asset opens on fear at all. Held at 7 because of that overlap and because the constraints bite hard: every 'nightmare' cell must be a defensible experiential fact rather than an implied health claim, and the eight cells are eight separate assets — the negative-framing rule prohibits stacking the positive and negative halves into one headline, where they dilute each other.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 9 of 16))*

## Write the benefit from the perspective of the people who confer status on the buyer — partner, kids, parents, colleagues, boss, friends, rivals — showing how each of them treats the buyer differently after the purchase, and how they treat them if nothing changes.

**Why it works:** Humans are status-driven and status is conferred by other people, not self-awarded. A benefit narrated through a third party's reaction therefore carries more motivational weight than the same benefit stated from the buyer's own point of view, and each new observer produces a fresh, non-repetitive angle from the same product fact.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus worked examples (a spouse who stops nagging, competitors noticing, kids noticing less stress). No data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable positioning and psychology principle, age irrelevant, free, and unusually concrete for this catalogue — the partner who stops noticing at the end of the day, the coworker sitting at close range, the kid who copies the routine, the gym friend who asks what you switched to. This is the enumerated Who axis of the matrix above, and it is genuinely additive: other skills cover 'a role the buyer gets to display' at a high level, but nothing lists third-party observers as an angle-generation checklist. Feeds Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, PDP body copy and email subject lines. Its close kin is the primal-desire rule below — that one names the desire (status, approval), this one names who hands it over — and the third-party voice is also what makes a claim readable as observation rather than self-praise. Held at 8 rather than higher because the 'if nothing changes' half slides easily into manufactured social shame, which is both ugly and off-brand for a small natural body-care company: keep the observer's reaction to something a real reviewer actually reported, and run the negative half past the understatement rule before shipping.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 9 of 16))*

## Run the same benefit or pain through the prospect's past, present and future — what their decisions have already cost them, what it costs them today, and what it will look like months from now — instead of writing only about the present moment.

**Why it works:** Buyers default to evaluating a decision in the here-and-now, where doing nothing looks free. Placing the same consequence on their own timeline makes the cost of indecision visible, and each timeline slot multiplies with each observer perspective to produce more distinct angles from one offer fact.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus worked weight-loss timeline examples (teased as a kid, the jeans today, another belt loop later). No data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle, age irrelevant, free, and it maps unusually cleanly onto this catalogue. The **past** slot is the single richest untapped vein here — every natural brand the buyer already tried and abandoned — and it is the raw material the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule below consume. The **present** slot is the reapply-at-noon reality. The **future** slot is the retention story: the transition period ends, and saying so belongs in the post-purchase emails aimed at the week-two churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate. This is the enumerated When axis of the matrix above. Directly usable in the giveaway campaign's ad copy, the non-buyer nurture flow and post-purchase sends. Held at 8 because the future slot must stay an honest description of a transition timeline rather than a promised result, and because two of the three slots feed rules already recorded below rather than standing alone.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 9 of 16))*

## Call your audience out by name in the copy — and generate the call-out from a menu of four verbal types: a label that names a group the buyer identifies with, a yes-question they can answer 'that's me' to, an if-then statement that states the qualifying condition, or a ridiculous result. Pick one specific enough to catch the right people and broad enough to catch many of them.

**Why it works:** The cocktail-party effect — attention is captured by a signal the listener recognises as being about them, so a line that names the reader's group, symptom or situation pierces feed noise before any claim is made. Naming the reader makes them stop and identify, which filters in the right buyer and filters out the wrong one before they cost you anything. Self-selection is a targeting mechanic, not just a stylistic choice, and the four types make it a batch job rather than a single lucky line.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion (Denney), plus the cocktail-party analogy, multiple worked example lines and the 2013 'I'm looking for 5 Chino Hills residents' ad as a case (Hormozi). No conversion data from either.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle, age-immune, executable solo at zero cost. Especially relevant for natural deodorant where the buyer is a self-identified group (people who switched off aluminium, people whose natural deodorant failed them), and the four types generate a batch in minutes: label — 'For people who quit aluminium and then quit the replacement'; yes-question — 'Do you reapply before 3pm?'; if-then — 'If you switched to natural deodorant and gave up in week two, this is for you'; ridiculous result — the one to use most sparingly, since an outsized claim in body care is a claims problem rather than a creative flourish. Match the type to the surface: label-type call-outs work in an Amazon title or bullet lead, while the question and if-then forms belong on Meta primary text, the PDP hero and email subject lines, which tolerate conversational copy. Its close kin is the agreement-opener rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`: this one names *who* the reader is, that one gets them to concede *what they are putting up with*. The disqualification rule directly below is the negative form of the same mechanic — it dismisses the wrong reader rather than naming the right one.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 8 of 16))*

## Disqualify the wrong reader up front — say plainly that if a stated condition is true of them they should stop reading right now, that this is not for everyone, that it will demand something of them.

**Why it works:** Naming who the offer is *not* for makes the reader run the qualification test themselves. Those who fail it leave, costing nothing later in refunds and complaints; those who pass have just privately argued themselves into being the right person, which is a stronger commitment than a claim handed to them. Inviting the wrong reader to leave also signals a seller who is not needy, which raises perceived value, and it reads as candour rather than salesmanship, so the claims that follow are evaluated rather than pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated rather than taught: 'if you know in your heart of hearts that you have already achieved everything you're capable of achieving — then please stop reading this report right now. There's nothing here that will help you.' And later: 'this program is definitely NOT for everyone', 'you'll need a pretty thick skin because I'm going to tear your beloved business to shreds', 'securing a place will NOT be cheap'. No test, no conversion, refund or take-rate figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), so the 2008 date is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, and it lands on live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject and opening lines, the PDP hero, and the non-buyer nurture flow. The honest form here is real rather than manufactured — natural deodorant genuinely is not for someone who wants sweat stopped, and the two-week transition genuinely defeats people who will not ride it out, so saying that plainly filters out the buyer most likely to conclude the product failed, which is the exact churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Held at 5 because it sits close to the call-out-by-name rule directly above (the if-then call-out type is the same sentence pointed the other way) and to the admit-flaws rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` (that one confesses a limitation of the product, this one dismisses a reader from buying at all, before any claim is asserted). Must stay honest rather than becoming false scarcity of attention on a $12 consumable, and **scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy. At ~54 orders/month no version of the opening line will ever be measurably better.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 1 of 6)*

## Audit every asset against five human needs — encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions, help them throw rocks at their enemies — and make sure at least one is doing real work, then work the missing ones in.

**Why it works:** Persuasion bonds form when a deep emotional need is met, not when an argument is well constructed. These five needs are what people reward others for meeting, and they recur behind every successful campaign and every strong relationship, so an asset that hits none of them is asking the reader to be convinced by logic alone — the weakest available lever. Because each lever is a different emotional job, running them as a checklist surfaces the ones a draft has silently skipped and stops a writer defaulting to the same one or two angles they are comfortable with.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus a challenge ('try to find a truly successful ad campaign that does not use one or more of these five'), a worked teardown of the Pelmanism sales letter showing three of the five stacked in the opening paragraphs with an unverified '$9,000 almost instantly' figure, and a self-demonstration in which the author re-reads his own three opening paragraphs and labels which lever each sentence performs, arguing they worked precisely because they never read as technique. Illustrative only; no controlled comparison, no split test, no performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), so the 2005 date is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, and it works as a coverage check across surfaces already live: PDP body copy, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks, the post-purchase and non-buyer nurture emails, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign. Its real value is as a generator that widens the angle set beyond the two this catalogue already leans on — natural deodorant copy defaults to fear (aluminium) and dream (confidence), while 'justify their failures' and 'confirm their suspicions' are wide open and map exactly onto a buyer whose last natural deodorant quit by noon. Use it as the index into the rest of this skill: **dreams** are the primal-desire headline rule below and the positive half of the eight-cell value-driver mirror above, **fears** are negative framing plus the negative half of that same mirror and the fear-handling rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, **failures** are the prior-attempt walkthrough and the blame-absolution rule beside it, and **suspicions** and **enemies** are the two sections directly below. This audit checks emotional coverage; the three-angle rule at the top of this skill checks register coverage, and the three-demands audit further down checks whether proof and mechanism arrive where the reader asks for them — run them on the same draft. Held at 7 because three of the five levers are already owned elsewhere, it is a checklist rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is the author grading his own essay, and at ~54 orders/month nothing it produces will ever be measurably better than what it replaced. Every 'suspicion' confirmed and every 'enemy' named must be true and defensible under the existing body-care claim rules.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Lean into the taboo — say the thing competitors are too polite to say.

**Why it works:** Naming the uncomfortable reality both stops the scroll and signals that you actually understand the buyer's lived experience, creating identification at a deeper level than benefit copy.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, reinforced in the demo where she argues Dr. Squatch is 'playing it very clean and very safe' and should go more visceral on gunky pits and pit stains.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle and unusually well-matched to the category: deodorant, body odor, and oral care are inherently taboo-adjacent, and the competitors named in this very video (Native, Every Man Jack, Harry's) are the ones playing it safe. Free to execute on PDP, Amazon, and email copy. Its close kin is the suspicion-confirmation rule directly below: taboo says the uncomfortable thing nobody says, suspicion-confirmation says the thing the buyer already half-believes but has never seen stated. Use taboo when you want a scroll-stop, suspicion when you want credibility.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Confirm the suspicion the reader already half-holds — state plainly the thing they suspect but have not seen said out loud.

**Why it works:** A suspicion the reader is already carrying costs nothing to install and can't be argued with, because they got there themselves. Confirming it makes the reader feel astute rather than sold to — the 'I knew it' surge — and they credit the confirmer with the good feeling. It also converts a private hunch into a shared belief, which is the fastest route to trust.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with historical and cult examples (Hitler confirming German suspicions about the cause of their troubles; cults confirming that prospects' families are sabotaging them), plus the Pelmanism letter confirming that an answer to the reader's dilemma exists. No commercial data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Genuinely additive: nothing else here owns 'say the thing the buyer already suspects'. Natural deodorant has a dense supply of true, already-held suspicions in the review corpus and voice-of-customer file — that most 'natural' deodorants are the same base with a different scent, that the transition period is real and nobody warns you, that baking soda is what burns. Stating one plainly on the PDP or as a Meta primary-text opener costs nothing and buys credibility a claim cannot. Held at 6 because it is close kin to the taboo rule above, the source's examples are demagogic rather than commercial, and in body care a 'suspicion' about a competitor's formulation is a disparagement risk unless it is a defensible fact — **confirm suspicions about the category and the experience, not about named competitors.**

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Name a shared enemy and help the reader throw rocks at it — but make the enemy a condition, never a competitor.

**Why it works:** Everyone you are selling to is fighting something — a person, a group, an illness, a setback, a rival philosophy. Someone who joins that fight stops being a vendor and becomes a partner, which is a materially different relationship from a satisfied-customer one. Refusing to name the enemy leaves that bond available to whoever will.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with a hedge that the author knows how ugly it sounds ('rest assured that people have enemies. All people'), plus the aphorism that everyone you meet is engaged in a great struggle. No examples from advertising, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free. The honest enemy for this catalogue is not a competitor but a condition: aluminium antiperspirants, baking-soda irritation, the ruined-shirt afternoon, the two weeks nobody warns you about. Framing the brand as the thing that fights alongside the buyer against that is available on the PDP hero, the Meta primary text going live now, and the founder-voice emails, and it is one of the few angles that suits a small brand better than it suits Native or Dr. Squatch. Held at 6 because `marketing-product-image-stack` already owns the us-vs-them comparison frame and `marketing-offer-construction` owns the differentiator audit — the additive part is the emotional register (join the fight) rather than the comparison table — and because the enemy must stay a category condition: disparaging another brand's formulation in body care is a claims and legal problem, not a creative choice. Write these lines in the first person plural (see the pronoun rule below), which is what keeps 'we're up against X' from reading as an attack. The contrast rule directly below is the argumentative counterpart: same refusal to name a competitor, aimed at the product class rather than at a condition.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Eliminate the alternatives with sharp explicit contrast — and aim it at the product class the buyer already owns, not at a named competitor. Generate on four axes: before/after, without your solution/with it, you/competitors, now/later.

**Why it works:** When a prospect cannot easily see the difference between your product and the thing already in their bathroom cabinet, the decision stalls and the default wins. A stated contrast lets them reach the superiority conclusion themselves — which they believe far more than a claim handed to them — and gives the deciding brain two concrete things to choose between rather than one thing to evaluate in the abstract.

**Evidence offered:** Two Billy Mays contrast lines (Grater Plater vs food processor and garlic press; Quick Chop vs a knife), plus an unsourced appeal to 'experts in Neuromarketing.' No figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, editorial. The additive part is specific and useful: the contrast target is the product class the buyer already owns — a conventional aluminium antiperspirant, or the natural stick that quit by noon — rather than a named brand, which is exactly the constraint this catalogue operates under, since disparaging Native's or Dr. Squatch's formulation is a claims and legal risk. The four-axis list is a cheap generator for PDP sections, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three recorded rules: the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, the us-vs-them graphic capped at three or four attributes in `marketing-product-image-stack`, and the shared-enemy-as-a-condition rule directly above. This rule chooses the axis; the rhythm rule directly below governs how the two halves are arranged on the page. Anything it generates must still be a defensible formulation fact.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Deliver that contrast in an alternating 'bad… good… bad… good' rhythm — name what the product class the buyer already owns does badly, immediately follow with what yours does instead, and choose weak-sounding words for that side and powerful ones for yours.

**Why it works:** Concentration is not just criticism: pointing out a weakness and then, in the same breath, proving your product delivers the same want without it makes the reader draw the superiority conclusion themselves. Alternating the two keeps each contrast adjacent so the comparison is unavoidable, and deliberate word selection widens the felt gap without adding a new claim.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with one illustrative structure ('competitor's product does this badly… mine however does this') and a compressed single-phrase example, 'SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY'. No test data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. The additive part is narrow but real: the contrast rule directly above supplies four axes and the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` supplies a one-line form — neither owns the alternating rhythm or the contrastive-vocabulary instruction. Natural fit for a PDP section or A+ block where 'baking soda burns / ours does not' is the whole argument. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three existing rules and because the hard constraint here inverts the source: the target must stay the product class the buyer already owns, never a named competitor, and every 'bad' half must be a defensible formulation fact rather than a chosen adjective. The word-selection half also has to survive the understatement rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — pick the accurate word, not the loudest one.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Write to a primal desire (status, sex, belonging, safety, approval) and treat the product as the mechanism, not the headline.

**Why it works:** People buy the end state, not the feature; the product is only the route to the desire, so the desire belongs in the headline and the feature belongs in the supporting line.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion; framed as one of the recurring patterns across all her winning creative, video and static.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable positioning principle, free to apply. This is the 'encourage their dreams' lever of the five-needs audit above, and the reader-centred half of the strip-yourself-out audit below; the status-conferrer rule above is its operational companion, naming who actually hands the status over. Scored slightly lower because natural body care sits closest to 'safety' and 'approval' and the risk of overreach into unsupportable claims is real — but reframing 'aluminum-free' as a safety/confidence outcome is straightforward PDP and Amazon work. The format-specific hard version of this rule — keeping the product out of the opening line entirely — is the product-out-of-the-hook rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`, and applies only to organic-styled social copy, not to Amazon or the PDP.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Strip yourself out of the draft — audit a finished piece for sentences about your wants, your offer mechanics, your story or what you think.

**Why it works:** Attention is legible. Copy organised around the seller's intentions reads as someone talking past the reader, and no amount of solid argument or polish repairs that. Copy organised entirely around the reader's needs creates a natural desire to reciprocate, and the seller's own goals then get expressed and met incidentally rather than having to be pushed. Pushing produces resistance; meeting a need almost never does.

**Evidence offered:** The observation that the one-sentence framework contains no reference to the persuader at all, plus a thought experiment (a conversation where the argument is perfect but you are staring at the wall behind the person). Assertion and analogy, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy discipline), age irrelevant, free, and runnable as a literal draft-review pass: highlight every sentence in a PDP section, Amazon bullet set or email that is about the brand's story, the offer mechanics or what the founder thinks, and check whether the reader gets anything from it. That is a real catch for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which reliably produces brand-voice throat-clearing before it gets to the reader. Capped at 5 because it overlaps the primal-desire rule above and the product-out-of-the-hook rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — so the additive part is only the whole-draft audit. Note the tension with the personal-experience rule below and with the human-narrative angle at the top of this skill: a first-person 'here's what I do' line is about the reader's problem even though it uses 'I', so this audit targets *self-interest*, not the first person. It also needs one scope limit: **an Amazon title and bullet set must state what the product is immediately, so the audit governs proportion and emphasis, not the removal of product facts.**

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Use negative framing — name what the audience is afraid of rather than only what they'll gain.

**Why it works:** Fear and avoidance language cuts through where benefit language blends in; naming the feared outcome proves you understand the stakes.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable copy principle, zero cost. Fits the category (aluminum, ruined shirts, natural deodorant that quits by noon) and is testable on Amazon bullets and PDP copy without any ad spend or traffic. Treat as the alternative opening to transformation-first, not a companion to it — running both in one headline dilutes each. Note also that this names a *feared future* outcome; the prior-attempt walkthrough below names an *already-experienced* failure, which is the version to reach for once the buyer has tried and been let down. Naming the fear is only half the job: the fear-handling rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` governs how you put it down again, and 'don't worry' does not count.

## Never publicise that many people do the thing you don't want — state the desired behaviour as the norm instead.

**Why it works:** One of the best predictors of an individual's behaviour is what the people around them are doing. Announcing that an undesirable behaviour is widespread licenses it, because the reader concludes 'everyone else is doing it, so I can too'. The same social-proof mechanism runs in reverse when the stated norm is the behaviour you actually want.

**Evidence offered:** The Cialdini-sourced IRS anecdote: the IRS publicly announced that too many people were cheating on taxes and raised penalties, and tax cheating then rose rather than fell. Plus the 'urinal rule' illustration. Secondhand anecdote, no figures, no test.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It lands on a live temptation: the transition-period copy on the PDP, in the A+ block and in post-purchase emails is exactly where a solo operator drafting with an LLM will write 'most people give up in week two' — which normalises quitting at the precise churn point driving the 18–22.5% repeat rate. The corrected form states the persistence norm ('most people are through it by around day ten') and is fine to ship if it is true. Hold the tension with the fear-handling and admit-flaws rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`: naming the rough patch honestly is required, but stating a quit rate as a norm is not the same thing and is what this prohibits. Capped at 5 because it sits close to the suspicion-confirmation and believability rules, the evidence is a single secondhand anecdote, and no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better at this volume.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 5 of 6)*

## Walk the buyer through what they have already tried and how it went — then associate the parts they liked with your solution and the parts they disliked with the specific reason it failed and why yours is different.

**Why it works:** Prior failed attempts are where the buyer's frustration already lives, so surfacing them widens the felt gap between where they are and where they want to be, which is the motivation to act. Splitting the prior attempt into 'the part that was right' and 'the part that broke' lets you inherit the credibility of the category while attributing the failure to a named cause your product removes — so you are not asking them to abandon the belief that got them here, only the product that let them down.

**Evidence offered:** Described as the 'pain cycle' step of his CLOSER framework, refined over 4,000+ consults: 'what have you done before, how'd that work out for you' repeated to keep expanding the gap. Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable persuasion principle, age irrelevant, and it has an unusually exact form here: the natural-deodorant buyer has almost always tried another natural deodorant that quit by noon or burned with baking soda. Copy that says 'you were right to get off aluminium — the problem was X, and here is what we do instead' does both jobs at once and is free to write on the PDP, in Amazon bullets and in the non-buyer nurture flow. In a saturated category this is also the natural vehicle for the named mechanism the sophistication rule demands. It is the fully-worked version of the past slot in the timeline rule above. Held at 6 because it partially overlaps negative framing above and transformation-first ordering in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` — treat it as the body-copy expansion of whichever opening you chose, not a third competing headline — and because attributing a competitor's failure to a specific cause in body care must stay to defensible formulation facts, not invented ones. The blame-absolution rule directly below is its front end: this one blames the *previous product*, that one absolves the *buyer*, and it fixes placement at the very top of the piece. The believability rule two entries down is its mirror image: this one names the *competitor's* flaw, that one names *yours*, and a body section that does both reads as honest rather than as a hit piece.

## Absolve the buyer of blame for their last failure in the opening lines — attribute it to a specific, truthful external cause outside their control ('it may not be your fault, it may be your metabolism').

**Why it works:** A reader who has already failed at this category is carrying private self-blame, and self-blame is both a barrier to hearing anything else and the reason they expect to fail again. Naming an external, factual cause hands them a scapegoat, which is itself something of value delivered before any claim is made, so the reason to expect another failure is removed and a bond forms with whoever removed it. Placing it in the first lines is what buys attention for everything after it. The truth constraint separates this from manipulation: depression genuinely can be a chemical imbalance, obesity genuinely can be metabolic, so the scapegoat is a real explanation rather than a flattering lie.

**Evidence offered:** Two US TV direct-response openings quoted or paraphrased (antidepressant: 'it may be the result of a chemical imbalance in your brain'; weight loss: 'it may not be your fault, it may be your metabolism'), the Pelmanism letter's 'you've done everything that you can... but there's still something holding you back', and a landscaper anecdote in which attributing a client's ugly yard to drought and bad soil 'increased significantly' the number of clients he secured. Teardowns and anecdote; no figures, no performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. This is the 'justify their failures' lever of the five-needs audit above, and the fit is unusually exact because the true scapegoat already exists: the natural-deodorant buyer's private belief is almost always self-blaming — 'I sweat more than most people', 'nothing works on me' — and the review corpus surfaces those lines verbatim, while the real causes are that baking soda irritates a large share of skin and nearly every competitor ships without warning that the transition takes two weeks. 'You didn't fail at going aluminium-free — the baking soda burned, and that is a formulation problem, not you' is literally defensible and is the exact opening the non-buyer nurture flow and the transition-period PDP section need. It lands on acquisition surfaces (PDP hero, Amazon bullets, Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway) and, more valuably, the post-purchase transition emails, where a week-two buyer concluding 'this failed because of me' is the exact churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Capped at 7 because it partially overlaps the prior-attempt walkthrough above; the additive part is narrow but real — that walkthrough blames the previous product, this absolves the buyer, and it fixes placement at the top rather than in the body. Every scapegoat named must clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before it ships.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 1 of 2)*

## Pitch exactly three differentiating pillars, each one or two sentences, each explained by an analogy to something the buyer already understands — and keep the pitch about their destination, not the product's internals.

**Why it works:** A long monologue about product detail loses the sale because the buyer cares about their own problem, not your mechanism. Capping it at three pillars keeps the argument holdable, and an analogy transfers understanding by mapping the unfamiliar onto something familiar, so a mechanism claim becomes believable without a technical explanation the buyer has to work through.

**Evidence offered:** Worked examples — SEO explained as a paycheck versus an investment account that compounds, and a mortgage-broker lead script built on exactly three pillars (exclusive, timely, qualified). Practitioner assertion from his own scripts, no conversion data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable copy-construction principle, age irrelevant, and free to apply to PDP body copy, Amazon A+ blocks and the non-buyer nurture emails. The three-pillar cap fits how much a shopper actually reads and lines up with the existing 'cap the us-vs-them graphic at three or four attributes you win on' rule, so it reinforces rather than contradicts. The analogy device is the additive part — nothing else in the copy skills covers explaining a mechanism by comparison, and natural deodorant needs exactly that for the transition period and for why an aluminium-free formula works differently. Held at 6 because analogies in body care slide easily into implied physiological claims, so each one has to be paired with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and kept descriptive, and at ~54 orders/month the choice of three pillars is judged rather than tested.

## Write the way people actually talk — 'just you and me' copy addressed to an old friend, informal, tolerant of the odd grammar mistake, in the voice of someone who actually uses the product rather than the voice of a brand.

**Why it works:** Informality signals a peer rather than a seller, so the reader drops the ad-avoidance posture and reads the piece as a recommendation. Copy written in a spoken register also carries the reader forward line to line, where formal marketing prose gives them a clean place to stop.

**Evidence offered:** Carlton's own retrospective quotes from Volume 1 of his Collected Letters ('the letter is written the way people talk', 'talks just like a real golfer'), plus an LLM-written yapper script as an illustration of the same register in a modern format. Practitioner assertion, no conversion data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable craft rule about register, age irrelevant, and free. Genuinely additive: this skill owns specificity, taboo framing, hook construction and attribution, but nothing else owns the *register* the copy is written in, and a solo operator drafting with an LLM will default to polished brand prose that reads as advertising. Natural homes are the founder-voice email sends, the Meta primary text going live now, and PDP body copy — all surfaces where a peer voice is credible for a small natural body-care brand in a way it is not for Native. Read a draft aloud; if you would not say it to a friend, rewrite it. **Scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy at thumbnail speed rather than conversational prose. Held at 6 because it is a craft rule rather than a revenue mechanism and will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month. It is the register the human-narrative angle at the top of this skill is written in, the voice the believability rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` is spoken in, the understatement rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` governs its intensity, the personal-experience rule directly below governs the *grammar of the claim* spoken in that voice, the pronoun rule below is its sentence-level companion, the persona-consistency rule further below is what keeps that voice the same from surface to surface, and it is the precondition for the long-form native format in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`.

## Write claims as personal experience rather than prescription — 'how I' rather than 'how to', 'my favourite way' rather than 'the best way'.

**Why it works:** A prescriptive claim invites the reader to check your authority and to argue with it. An experiential claim is not disputable — nobody can tell you what you did not do — and it reads as sharing rather than preaching, which avoids the arrogance that makes an unknown brand's advice bounce off a reader who has no reason to grant it standing.

**Evidence offered:** Three worked before/after rewrites (oatmeal, a 7-figure agency, lead generation). Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable voice principle, age irrelevant, free, and unusually well-matched to a founder-run natural body-care brand with no lab, no clinical studies and no authority to lend a prescription. 'Here's the routine I use after switching off antiperspirant' is both truer and safer than 'this is the best way to detox your pits.' Runnable today on Meta primary text for the live $30/day giveaway, founder-voice emails, PDP body copy and social captions. It is distinct from the spoken peer register directly above — that one governs how the sentence *sounds*, this one governs the grammar of the *claim* inside it — and from the strip-yourself-out audit above, which targets self-interested copy rather than the first person as such. Two hard limits: experiential phrasing does not launder an efficacy claim ('I stopped sweating through shirts' is still a performance claim and must clear the body-care claim rules), and it must be **scoped away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable product claims rather than a personal anecdote. Note also the division with the pronoun rule directly below: adversarial and problem-solving lines stay 'we', while the experience being reported stays 'I'.

## Write the adversarial and problem-solving lines in the first person plural — 'we're about to take aim' rather than 'I'm about to take aim'.

**Why it works:** Pronoun choice assigns sides. 'I' puts the writer on one side of the transaction and the reader on the other; 'we' places both on the same side of the problem, so the same sentence reads as shared cause rather than a pitch — which is what makes the shared-adversary lever land instead of sounding like a competitor attack.

**Evidence offered:** The author points at two of his own sentences and notes the deliberate 'we' in each, stating it 'helps assure readers that I'm on their side.' Pure self-annotation, no comparison against an 'I' version and no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable craft rule about register, age irrelevant, free, and it takes seconds to apply. Genuinely additive in a narrow direction: the spoken peer register above owns *how* the copy sounds and the personal-experience rule above owns the grammar of a reported claim, but nothing owns the pronoun as an alliance signal, which is what keeps a competitor-contrast or shared-enemy sentence from reading as a hit piece for a small brand with no authority to attack Native or Dr. Squatch. Natural homes are founder-voice emails, the non-buyer nurture flow, and Meta primary text on the live campaign. Capped at 5 because it is a one-word craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it substantially overlaps the peer-register rule it sits beside, and it must be scoped away from Amazon titles and bullets, which need scannable claim copy rather than conversational solidarity.

*Source: Blair Warren — "The One-Sentence Persuasion Course: 27 Words to Make the World Do Your Bidding" (special report, part 2 of 2)*

## Hold one persona constant across every surface — same voice, same reason-for-formulating story, same signature framing — because consistency is itself a sales asset.

**Why it works:** Repeated exposure produces familiarity, familiarity produces trust, and a pitch never stands on its own: the odds of a sale rise sharply when some relationship exists before the pitch arrives. Signature elements — the voice, the recurring story, the same way of framing the problem — are what let scattered exposures accumulate into one recognisable person rather than several forgettable ones. The same pitch from a familiar figure outsells the identical pitch from an equally skilled unknown.

**Evidence offered:** The Pitchmen Grater Plater split test: Mays and Sullivan pitched the same product, same clothes, same lines, same scenes and camera angles, and Mays's version brought in 40% more revenue over a week of TV testing. One test, self-reported through a TV show, no spend or sample detail.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning and trust), age irrelevant, free, and it is the rare item in this catalogue with a real controlled comparison behind it. The honest solo-operator form: the founder is the only persona available, so hold the same voice, the same reason-for-formulating-baking-soda-free story and the same signature framing constant across the Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, the founder-voice emails and the PDP — rather than writing each surface in a different register. This is also what keeps the 'I' of the personal-experience rule above referring to the same person everywhere, and it bounds the three-angle rule at the top of this skill: three angles may vary the route of persuasion, but not who is speaking. Held at 5 because it substantially overlaps the spoken peer register above and the authority-attribution lever in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` (the additive part is only that consistency *across surfaces* compounds), and because the evidence is a celebrity effect built over years of national TV exposure, which a sub-1,000-subscriber list and ~54 orders/month cannot reproduce. **Scope it away from Amazon titles and bullets**, which need scannable claim copy rather than a persona.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 2 of 3)*

## Give the reader a numbered list of concrete symptoms and a scoring threshold — 'if six or more of these sound like you, the diagnosis is clear' — so they diagnose themselves rather than being told they have the problem.

**Why it works:** A conclusion the reader reaches by tallying their own behaviour is believed far more than the same conclusion asserted by the seller, and cannot be argued with. The checklist also forces active engagement item by item, and converts an abstract problem into a set of specific recognisable situations — so the reader arrives at the offer having already conceded the premise.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated rather than taught: a twelve-item 'leisure phobia' checklist with a stated six-item threshold, plus a shorter self-question stack ('Does your current level of success equal your potential?'). No performance data, no comparison against a straight assertion of the same problem.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It is a concrete artifact this catalogue can build honestly, and the raw material already exists in the review corpus and voice-of-customer file: 'six signs your natural deodorant quit on you' or 'four signs you're still in the transition, not failing at it' — the second aimed squarely at the week-two churn event behind the 18–22.5% repeat rate, and belonging in the post-purchase flow rather than acquisition. Held at 5 because it overlaps the 'how to know if…' problem-aware stem in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, the suspicion-confirmation rule above, the yes-question call-out type above, and the problem-articulation rule directly below; the additive part is the scored, tallied checklist with a stated threshold, which none of those own. Every symptom must be something a real reviewer said, and none may imply a health condition.
