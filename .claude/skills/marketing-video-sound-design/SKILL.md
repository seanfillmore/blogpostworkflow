# Video Sound Design

## Run a deliberate sound-design pass on every generated or B-roll clip rather than shipping it with only voiceover: derive the effect list from two buckets — 'what do you see' (diegetic: footsteps, a cup rattling, a lid clicking) and 'what do you feel' (emotional: a suspenseful riser, a heartbeat, a deep impact) — then generate each one by plainly describing it to a text-to-sound model, and lay music under it too.

**Why it works:** Generated video is visually plausible but acoustically dead, and that silence is a large part of what reads as synthetic. The diegetic layer supplies the physical cues a viewer unconsciously expects so the clip reads as filmed rather than rendered; the emotional layer tells the viewer how to feel at that moment. Splitting the list into two named buckets means you enumerate both instead of only the obvious ones.

**Evidence offered:** Before/after playback of the same hook with and without sound design, plus the literal prompts used ('cups and silverware rattle on a table as an earthquake starts to rumble', 'a subtle but suspenseful riser'), and the tool path demonstrated for generating effects.

**Fit here (8/10):** Organic short-form and any AI-assisted ad are live surfaces produced end to end by one person, and sound design has no home in the skill set at all — the nearest skills cover voiceover casting and edit timing, not SFX. A cheap, repeatable quality lift on every video posted, and it applies identically at 16 orders a month or at $83K a month.

## In talking-head edits with on-screen graphics, map each graphic event type to one fixed sound, all over a low cinematic music bed: a click when text appears, a highlighter or marker sound when a line or graph draws, a whoosh on each transition.

**Why it works:** This extends the 'what do you see' bucket above from in-world diegetic sounds to UI and motion-graphic sounds. A one-to-one link between graphic events and sounds gives the viewer an audio cue each time new information appears. That pulls attention onto the overlay and keeps the pacing active in a sparse visual style. It also makes the sound pass a repeatable recipe rather than a fresh decision on every cut, and the animations feel tactile and premium.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus the demonstrated final edit.

**Fit here (5/10):** RSC already escalates founder yaps to 'graphic yaps': text, ingredient callouts and comparison graphics over the talking head. A standing event-to-sound mapping makes those sound-ready in minutes, and one person can apply it in CapCut or have Claude apply it. Runnable today. It is a modest craft lever, not a revenue driver on its own.

## Before the sound pass, put a folder of about 20 of the target style's best-performing videos in front of the AI editor, and have it derive from those references which sound effects go where and what the music should sound like, instead of choosing sounds by hand.

**Why it works:** Proven videos in a style encode its audio conventions: what triggers a sound, how dense the effects are, and the mood of the music bed. An agent that pattern-matches against a corpus reproduces those conventions more consistently than ad-hoc choices. The see/feel method above derives effects from the shot, while this method derives them from a style corpus. The two combine: the corpus sets the conventions, and the shot supplies the specific moments. Because the corpus persists, each later edit is faster.

**Evidence offered:** A single demonstration. The finished Arthur Brooks edit is played on screen, and the creator says the next run will take about half the time. No performance data.

**Fit here (5/10):** The founder films and edits his own organic short-form, and a reference corpus is something one person can assemble at a laptop. He can save 15–20 top-performing videos in the formats he copies, such as founder yaps and natural-deodorant explainers. Feeding them to the editor keeps the sound pass consistent across every reel and every organic clip later run as paid creative. Merit is moderate because audio polish is a secondary lever next to hook and angle, and the only proof is one course-seller demo.

## When generating a sound effect, tick the 'choose specific duration' option and set a longer duration than you need, because the model returns higher-quality audio when given room.

**Why it works:** Left on auto, the model produces clipped, truncated artifacts. An explicit and generous duration gives it space to render attack and decay, and the excess is trimmed in the edit anyway.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from repeated use ('I find it gives me higher quality sound effects').

**Fit here (6/10):** A small, concrete, setting-level tip that can run today inside a workflow he can start this week. It belongs to the fast-decay platform-mechanics class: the named control is part of a specific 2026 tool UI, so it should be re-checked rather than treated as durable, which is what caps it.

## Place each sound effect on the timeline against the exact moment the viewer sees or feels it, and pull its volume down well below where it imports, because generated SFX arrive far louder than needed.

**Why it works:** Sound effects exist to enhance the picture and the narration, not to compete with them. At import level they pull attention off the message, which is the opposite of their job. The same applies to the UI clicks, whooshes and music bed in graphic-heavy talking-head edits: keep them under the voice.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in the before/after playback; stated as standard practice.

**Fit here (7/10):** Runnable today in the basic editor already in use. It keeps the narration on top, and the narration carries the actual selling argument, which matters more for a product video than for an entertainment piece.
