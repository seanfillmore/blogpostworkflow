# Scarcity and Urgency Framing

## Make the promotion, price, or bonus the thing that expires — never the product itself.

**Why it works:** Claiming you won't sell the product after the deadline is a transparent lie for an always-available offer, but the promotion genuinely does end, so pointing at the promotion produces the same fear of missing out while keeping the claim true.

**Evidence offered:** Practitioner framing — 'I'm not sure how long we'll run this, we change them every four weeks' — presented as the honest substitute for fake product scarcity. Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** The single most transferable urgency mechanic for an always-in-stock consumable: RSC cannot claim deodorant becomes unavailable, so this is the only honest form of urgency available *on the standing catalog*. It attaches to structures already in place — a named bundle, a buy-X-get-Y multipack, a free lip balm with a refill, the subscribe-and-save rate. Zero cost, solo-executable on Shopify and in email, squarely in the Offer/AOV stage. Not higher because `marketing-offer-construction` warns against reflexive discounting: the safe version is an expiring *bonus or bundle*, not a recurring percentage-off that trains the list to wait. Note the boundary with the next section: this one expires the *offer* because nothing about the product is limited; the next one applies when something about the product genuinely is.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 8 of 11)*

## Mine the operation for limits that already exist — batch size, packing capacity, a design or scent that will not be repeated — and advertise those instead of inventing scarcity.

**Why it works:** Scarcity raises perceived value and compresses decision time, and using a limit that genuinely exists makes the claim true, defensible and repeatable instead of a bluff the buyer can spot. The limit is already in the business; the only work is stating it out loud.

**Evidence offered:** Examples: 'I can only handle five people per week', 'we only printed one batch of shirts and will never reprint this design', 'the most convenient class times fill up fast'. Practitioner assertion, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** A solo operator making 12 SKUs by hand has genuine, currently-unadvertised limits: batch sizes, a seasonal scent that will not be repeated, the number of giveaway prizes, how many orders can be packed in a week. Stating those on the PDP, on the giveaway entry page and in Meta primary text costs nothing and needs no ad budget or attribution. Distinct from the section above — that one expires the promotion because the product is unlimited; this one is about discovering the product-side limits that already exist and were simply never said. Held at 7 because the limits must be real and enforced (if a 'final batch' scent reappears, the claim is spent), and at ~54 orders/month the lift is judged, never measured.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 3 of 16)*

## Where nothing can honestly expire, attach the bonus to the act of ordering instead of to a calendar.

**Why it works:** Some surfaces cannot hold a deadline at all — an Amazon listing is evergreen and policy-constrained, an always-on PDP has no countdown, and a permanently-running ad that claims an expiry is simply lying. Conditional phrasing solves this by tying the extra value to the action rather than the date: 'order now and the lip balm ships with it.' The reader feels they must act to get everything on the table, and the seller never asserts an end date that will not arrive. This is the fallback when the sections above have nothing to point at — no live promotion, no genuine operational limit — not a replacement for a real promotion window.

**Evidence offered:** Observation only: Schefren notes that because the commercial ran continuously Billy 'couldn't have a time limit or an expiration date, but the way he described it made you feel that you needed to order now'. No data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Fills the real hole in the rest of this skill, which prescribes only honoured deadlines and seasonal re-wraps while the two surfaces carrying most revenue cannot hold a deadline — Amazon (~$1,800/mo) and the always-on Shopify PDP. The honest translation is conditional bonus phrasing on the PDP and in cart/post-purchase copy ('add the refill now and the lip balm ships with it'), with a hard guardrail: **the bonus must actually be attached to that order, never merely implied to be expiring.** Capped at 5 because the source's own version leans on manufactured feeling — exactly the drift the 'deadline must be genuinely honored' rule below prohibits — and at ~54 orders/month the effect will be judged, never measured.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 3 of 3)*

## Give every promotion an explicit, believable reason why and a real end date — then re-wrap the same core promotion with a new seasonal name and fresh dates.

**Why it works:** A stated reason answers 'why is this being offered and why should I act now', converting a bare price cut into a time-bounded event. The seasonal wrapper supplies a legitimate start and finish for something you actually run year-round, so you get a real deadline plus novelty without changing the offer economics. Without a reason-why the discount reads as the normal price and buyers learn to wait for the next one.

**Evidence offered:** A list of recurring seasonal wrappers (New Year, Valentines, Grand Opening, Anniversary, Back to School) with the instruction to display dates everywhere in copy and on landing pages. Practitioner assertion.

**Fit here (7/10):** The cheapest solution to a real solo-operator problem — what to email a small list about every month without inventing new products or deepening discounts — and directly corrective for the reflex to send a bare percent-off code. Re-uses bundles and multipacks already in the catalog, costs nothing, needs no ad budget or attribution, and every send lands on the retention surface where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided. Not higher because **the deadline must be genuinely honored or it erodes list trust**, and at ~54 orders/month the lift is judged, not measured.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 8 of 11)*

## Put a deadline on the opening too: announce a fixed on-sale date, say plainly it isn't buyable yet, and fill the gap with free value.

**Why it works:** Most of this skill closes windows; this one opens them. Withholding availability while continuing to deliver something useful converts waiting time into engaged attention rather than dead time — the reader keeps opening emails because more free material is promised — and the fixed date concentrates demand into a single moment instead of a trickle of individual decisions. It is an honest form of urgency that needs no discount and no manufactured scarcity: the thing genuinely cannot be bought yet.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated rather than taught: 'you can NOT sign up for Guided Profit System now. Not yet... between now and Tuesday, September 23 2008, keep your eyes glued to your inbox. There are more reports, videos and blog postings... all free. Plus, as we get closer to the release date, I'll provide more details and personally answer your questions.' No open rates, no conversion figures, no comparison against launching immediately.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable offer-sequencing principle, free and solo-executable, landing on the one owned surface where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided. The honest RSC form is a dated launch for a new scent, a seasonal bundle or the next giveaway round, with two or three value emails in between — the whisper material and the escalating cadence below are what fill that runway, the reason-why wrapper supplies the name, and the closing send carries the window once it opens. Capped at 5 because at a sub-1,000 subscriber list and ~54 orders/month the concentration effect will never be measurable, because a runway of 'coming soon' emails on a $12 consumable overshoots if it runs more than a few sends, and because **the mechanism collapses if the stated opening date is not honoured** — same trust rule as the closing deadline.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 6 of 6)*

## Start the runway with a whisper phase: tease that something exists without revealing it, and post the work being made.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is wanting to know what happens next; naming that something exists and refusing to explain it plants an open loop the audience carries until the reveal. Visible labour — drafts, versions, late nights — manufactures perceived value before any claim is made, because the longer something appears to have taken, the more the audience values it. Begin as far out as you can: the runway is the asset.

**Evidence offered:** His own book launch: posting draft numbers, photos of printed drafts, hand-drawn framework versions, videos of editing sessions. Demonstration, no lift figures.

**Fit here (7/10):** Free, on-brand and solo-runnable: a maker of small-batch soap, deodorant and oral care has genuine behind-the-scenes material — formulation batches, pour days, label revisions, cure racks — and 'show your work' is what a handmade natural brand should be posting anyway. Applies immediately to the soap giveaway and to any new SKU or seasonal scent, and it is what makes the dated-opening section above honest rather than a string of empty 'coming soon' emails. Held at 7 because organic reach on a sub-1,000 following is thin, so most of the whisper value will come through email and the $30/day Meta campaign rather than free posting, and the effect will be judged, never measured, at ~54 orders/month.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 14 of 16)*

## Escalate a launch on a fixed cadence: whisper, then tease, then shout.

**Why it works:** Rising frequency converts accumulated curiosity into a concentrated buying window, and each phase does one job — curiosity, then value, then action — so the audience is never asked to act before it wants to know. The published schedule: whisper every 4–6 weeks until 60 days out, then every 2–3 weeks until 30 days out; tease (reveal the product, publish the date, show the value elements) weekly until 14 days out, then twice weekly until 3 days out; then shout (specific calls to action, bonuses, scarcity) at least twice a day, hourly on launch day.

**Evidence offered:** His own book launch executed on this schedule; presented as the general method for launching anything, including activating affiliates. No comparison against a flat cadence.

**Fit here (7/10):** Concretely actionable for the soap giveaway now being promoted with the $30/day Meta campaign, and reusable for every future SKU or seasonal drop — it turns ad-hoc announcements into a written posting and emailing schedule the solo operator can execute without re-deciding each week. Slots between the sections above and below: it supplies the runway shape for a dated opening, uses whisper material as its first phase, names the honest limits and bonuses as shout content, and ends on the closing reminder. Held at 7 and **the shout frequency must be scaled down hard** — twice-daily emails, let alone hourly, will burn a sub-1,000 list on a $12 consumable; the workable RSC version is roughly one shout email a day for the last three days plus a last-hours send. The 60/30/14/3-day skeleton transfers; the raw send counts do not.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 14 of 16)*

## Run to a stated deadline and send a closing reminder at the end rather than letting a promotion fade out.

**Why it works:** Purchase decisions are deferred until deferral is no longer possible; the deadline is what converts intent into action. Fear of turning away sales by imposing a cutoff is unfounded, because the people who never bought under a deadline were never going to buy without one.

**Evidence offered:** The claim that 50–60% of a promotion's sales land in the final hours of the final day. Sourced from launch-model information products, not physical goods.

**Fit here (7/10):** Actionable as a specific artifact — every promo email sequence gets a last-day/last-hours send, and it is the final beat of the escalating cadence above. Free, no ad budget, on the owned email surface that carries the 18–22.5% repeat rate, and redemption is measurable via discount code even with attribution broken, so it survives the pre-tracking stage. Held at 7 because **the 50–60% figure does not transfer**: it comes from launch-model info products, not a $50.46-AOV consumable. Take the closing send as the practice and ignore the specific split.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 8 of 11)*
