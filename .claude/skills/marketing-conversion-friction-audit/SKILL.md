# Conversion Friction Audit

## Before rewriting anything, instrument the store and ask people directly — session recordings and an on-page question for non-buyers, a post-purchase survey for buyers — and keep probing until the specific constraint on order count or AOV is named.

**Why it works:** Without observation you are optimizing whatever you happen to believe, and you cannot double down on a winner or kill a loser before it drains the bank. Analytics show where people abandon but never why. Watching real sessions surfaces the specific confusion, missing information or friction that no amount of internal reasoning produces; asking the people who *did* buy which information closed them names the deciding factor directly, so the fix targets a known constraint instead of a guessed one. Both are qualitative evidence, which does not require statistical volume to be informative. The sharpest version of the non-buyer half is the free-offer decline question below — a generic exit survey collects shrugs, while a specific decliner asked a specific question names a defect.

**Evidence offered:** Schefren part 2: named as Silver Bullet #2, then detailed with tool name-drops (Woopra for real-time analytics, ClickTale for session recording, 4q.iperceptions for on-page survey) and the claim that today's reports are graphically robust enough that 'a sixth-grader could understand them' — no account data, no before/after, no instrumentation procedure. Schefren part 5: process instruction only — monitor each step, note abandonment points, scour forums and blogs, and 'better yet, conduct a survey at the end of the buying process' — no example survey, no findings, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** The named tools are platform-mechanics class and 17 years stale — ClickTale is now Contentsquare, 4Q is defunct — so treat the stack as dead and the practice as durable. The practice is free and solo-executable today: Microsoft Clarity or similar for recordings, a one-question exit survey on the Shopify PDP, and a one-question post-purchase survey appended to the existing thank-you page or the first post-purchase email. It lands on the ~$875/mo Shopify side where CRO work is live and the tracking gate is open. Its real value is that qualitative feedback is readable at ~54 orders/month where a quantitative funnel report is not: twenty session recordings show a broken variant picker or an unanswered transition-period question even though no conversion-rate delta would ever reach significance. Genuinely additive on the buyer-survey half — marketing-problem-solution-inventory sources objections from public complaints and competitor reviews and marketing-review-mining shapes the review request, but nothing asks buyers what actually closed them, and that answer is the input the rest of this skill is currently guessing at. It also feeds Step 1 of marketing-performance-pattern-analysis, which demands the binding constraint be named before anything is rewritten. Held at 6 (the survey half nearer 5) because Amazon — the larger ~$1,800/mo channel — cannot be instrumented or surveyed this way at all, it produces diagnosis rather than revenue, and the sections below already tell you what to look for once you are looking.

## When someone turns down even a *free* offer, ask why and ask "what would I have to do to make it worth it for you to continue?" — a declined free thing proves the blocker is dream outcome, belief, or hidden time/effort/sacrifice cost, not price.

**Why it works:** Price is only one term of the value equation, so a free thing that still gets declined is direct proof that the hidden costs are the real expense. The decliner is the only person who can name which of the other three terms is broken — they do not want the outcome, they do not believe it will work for them, or the effort/wait/risk of trying it exceeds zero dollars. Asking converts a no into a specific fixable defect, and the answer doubles as ammunition for the next prospect, since the objection you just heard is the one the page is failing to pre-empt.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, framed as the author's own diagnostic habit; no data, no worked case.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle class, zero cost, runnable today by the solo operator — reply to every email decline, and question the people who saw a giveaway or sample offer and did not enter. Unusually well matched: natural deodorant carries a real hidden cost (the transition/detox period, the reapplication habit) that plausibly caps the 18–22.5% repeat rate, and this question is the cheapest way to confirm that rather than assume it. Distinct from the generic buyer/non-buyer surveys above — this is one specific question aimed at one specific decliner, which is why it returns a named defect instead of a shrug — and distinct from the declined-cross-sell claim elsewhere in the library. Whatever it surfaces should be routed straight into the four-term audit below and, if the answer is the transition period, into the published-timeline tactic. Not higher because it only reaches people who engaged enough to be reachable (email list, DMs), never the anonymous Amazon shopper on the larger ~$1,800/mo channel, and it produces diagnosis rather than revenue.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 4 of 16))*

## Audit an offer against the four-term value equation — increase dream outcome and perceived likelihood of achievement while decreasing time delay and effort/sacrifice — rather than treating the offer as a single price decision.

**Why it works:** Buyers purchase when perceived value exceeds perceived price. Value is not one number but four movable terms, so a seller who can only move price is forced into a race to the bottom, while a seller who moves the other three terms raises value without cutting margin.

**Evidence offered:** Whiteboard derivation of the equation plus the Hormozi six-pack-pill thought experiment; a claim that clients have cut CPA by up to 90% 'by simply figuring this stuff out,' with no figures or named cases.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle class (offer construction), so age is irrelevant. Its value here is as a diagnostic checklist for the CRO and Offer/AOV stages of the gated sequence: it names four places to look on a PDP or Amazon listing with zero spend and no traffic requirement. Not higher because two of the four terms are already partly served by existing skills (dream outcome by primal-desire copy, risk by the guarantee tactic in offer construction) — the additive value is the audit structure and the time-delay term nobody covers.

*Source: Ecommerce Alley — "How To Apply Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers To Ecommerce" (Nxddux6n2-c)*

## Actively collect reviews and testimonials — email or call customers, incentivize if needed — and display them on every surface, because reviews are the cheapest proof that the product delivers the dream outcome.

**Why it works:** Perceived likelihood of achievement is what converts a wanted outcome into a purchase; third-party accounts of the outcome raise that certainty in a way seller claims cannot.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, framed as the first and most obvious lever; no numbers.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable persuasion principle, no decay, and it does not depend on scale — the instruction is to solicit and surface whatever exists, which works at dozens of reviews. Distinct from the existing review-mining skill, which covers extracting language from reviews, not generating or placing them. Held at 6 because 'incentivize them' is an Amazon TOS violation on the channel producing ~$1,800/mo, so only the Shopify post-purchase request and the on-page placement half is safely usable.

*Source: Ecommerce Alley — "How To Apply Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers To Ecommerce" (Nxddux6n2-c)*

## Publish the typical timeline and process of results and of product usage — visual documentation for anything with a learning curve — directly on the product page.

**Why it works:** When a product has a ramp-up, buyers silently discount their odds of success. Stating 'here is exactly what happens, in what order, over how long' both raises perceived likelihood of achievement and shortens the felt time delay to the outcome.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with a general 'products with educational curves' framing and craft/setup-time examples; no data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle class and an unusually exact match: natural deodorant has a named, well-documented learning curve — the transition period — which is the single biggest reason a first-time buyer concludes the product failed. A dated week-by-week timeline on the PDP and as an Amazon A+ block costs nothing, needs no traffic, and attacks the 18–22.5% repeat rate that is the binding constraint. No existing skill owns published-timeline documentation; the awareness skill only covers the 'how to start' headline stem, not the artifact.

*Source: Ecommerce Alley — "How To Apply Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers To Ecommerce" (Nxddux6n2-c)*

## Offer express checkout options (Shop Pay and similar) and allow guest checkout instead of forcing account creation.

**Why it works:** Form-filling and forced registration are pure sacrifice with no value to the buyer; removing keystrokes at the moment of highest intent reduces abandonment.

**Evidence offered:** Personal preference plus assertion; no conversion data.

**Fit here (7/10):** This is platform-mechanics class, so normally suspect — but the specific mechanics named (Shop Pay, guest checkout toggles) are still live Shopify settings in 2024-era and later, so the 2024 publish date does not undermine it. Squarely inside the CRO stage of the gated sequence: a one-time settings change by the solo operator, zero cost, no ad budget, and it operates on the Shopify side where revenue is weakest at ~$875/mo. Capped at 7 because at roughly 54 orders/month the lift will never be measurable, so it is a do-it-and-move-on item, not a test.

*Source: Ecommerce Alley — "How To Apply Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers To Ecommerce" (Nxddux6n2-c)*

## Spell out returns, exchanges, and cancellation terms plainly on the product page — e.g. in a toggle — instead of making buyers hunt for them.

**Why it works:** Unstated exit terms are assumed to be bad. Buyers silently price in the worst case, so making the process visible and simple removes an objection they will never voice.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus the observation that clients bury these terms; recommends a PDP accordion as the implementation.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle, no decay, and implementable as a Shopify PDP accordion in an afternoon at zero cost — CRO-stage work that needs no traffic or budget. Scored mid because on Amazon (~$1,800/mo, the larger channel) the return policy is platform-controlled and already prominent, so the gain is confined to the ~$875/mo Shopify side, and 'cancellation' only bites if a subscription option is actually offered.

## Remove decision fatigue — cut the number of variants, sizes, and purchase options rather than adding more, because too many choices stall the buyer.

**Why it works:** Every additional option is cognitive effort the buyer must spend before deciding, which raises the sacrifice term; fewer, clearer options make the choice trivially easy.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with a subscription-box example (month-to-month plus 3/6/12-pay plus one-time, times three box sizes); no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle, no decay, and concretely actionable across a 12-SKU catalog: collapse scent variants into a default, pick one subscription cadence instead of several, and lead each collection with one recommended pack. Zero cost, solo-executable, CRO-stage. It also acts as a needed counterweight to the bundle-heavy offer tactics already adopted — those add SKUs, this constrains how many are shown at once. Not 8 because with ~54 orders/month the effect is unmeasurable and must be applied by judgment.

*Source: Ecommerce Alley — "How To Apply Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers To Ecommerce" (Nxddux6n2-c)*

## Reduce information fatigue — but decide length by the three jobs copy has to finish, not by a word target: build desire to its greatest strength, make the buyer feel comfortable and complimented owning the product, and make him believe what you said.

**Why it works:** Excess information is effort. A page that requires reading everything to reach a decision imposes sacrifice, so trimming to what the decision actually needs raises perceived value. But 'shorter' is the wrong instruction on its own, because desire, identification and belief are separate mental dimensions: a page that has finished one and not the others is under-length no matter how many words it has, and a page repeating work already done on one dimension is over-length no matter how short it is. So the audit is per-section, not per-page — run each block of a PDP or A+ stack against the three questions (does this build desire with a concrete scene, does it serve identification, does it serve belief) and collapse anything that serves none of the three.

**Evidence offered:** Ecommerce Alley: assertion posed as a self-audit question, no data. Schwartz: assertion with the three-dimension framework laid out, no worked example and no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable CRO/copy-structure class, zero cost, purely editorial, solo-executable. The three-job test is what the trimming instruction was missing — it sits in real tension with several adopted tactics that add page content (results timeline, guarantee, returns toggle, proof stack), and the test resolves that tension by showing each of those is load-bearing on belief, so the useful form is 'order by decision-relevance and collapse the rest,' not 'delete.' Held at 6 because it is a length heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism, it overlaps the one-job-per-asset and headline-hierarchy rules already in marketing-product-image-stack (though those govern single frames rather than page length), and at ~54 orders/month page length will be judged rather than measured.

## An improvement the buyer never perceives creates no value — so spend on making the wait and the effort *feel* smaller, not only on actually shrinking them.

**Why it works:** Value exists only in the prospect's mind, so a cheap perceptual intervention can beat an expensive operational one. The London Underground's dotted arrival map cost a few million and raised rider satisfaction more than making the trains faster would have for billions, because what hurt riders was not the wait but the uncertainty of the wait.

**Evidence offered:** The London Underground dot-matrix map case, presented as the canonical illustration. Third-party example, no figures from the author's own accounts.

**Fit here (8/10):** Unusually well matched. RSC cannot shorten the natural-deodorant transition period, but it can publish a dated week-by-week map of what happens and when — exactly the dot-matrix move, where the wait stays the same and the uncertainty disappears. Zero cost, solo-executable on the PDP, the Amazon A+ block, and post-purchase emails, and it attacks the 18–22.5% repeat rate. It is also the 'why' behind the results-timeline tactic and generalizes it: **any real reduction in effort or wait must be stated on the page, or it earns nothing.**

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 5 of 11)*

## Treat every subpage as an entry page for cold traffic — credibility, proof and the offer must repeat on the deep pages people actually land on, not sit once on the homepage or a reviews page nobody visits.

**Why it works:** Organic and paid visitors land directly on deep pages with zero brand context, and nobody navigates down from the homepage, so any trust element living only on the homepage is invisible to most buyers. Proof only works where the decision is made. A buyer who never clicks through still absorbs credibility from seeing a badge or headline sitting next to the offer.

**Evidence offered:** Live demonstration: a law firm's Trustpilot stars and award badges appear on the homepage but vanish on the commercial-contracts subpage that would actually rank in Google; plus service pages with client logos embedded inline, and the assertion that dedicated case-study pages are among the least-visited on any site.

**Fit here (8/10):** An exact match for this business — an Amazon listing and a Shopify PDP are both pure cold-traffic entry points with no homepage visit preceding them, and Amazon is the larger channel at ~$1,800/mo. It also names the failure mode behind "a post with traffic and no buy path is a bug": the review quotes, third-party proof, transition-period guarantee and 'what this is' framing must each appear on **every** PDP, every commercial page, and in the A+ content, rather than once on the homepage or buried in a policy page. Zero cost, purely editorial, no traffic or attribution required. It sharpens the proof-placement rules elsewhere in this skill by naming *which* pages need the proof, which nothing previously owned.

## Inventory the credibility assets you actually have, then place at least one at every decision point — above the fold and immediately beside the button you expect people to click — and host the comparison and third-party studies on your own page so the buyer never has to leave to verify you.

**Why it works:** Nobody wants to be first to take an action, so everywhere you ask for trust you must supply a reason to trust at the moment of the ask. Credibility markers are an instant shortcut requiring no reading — but a marker the visitor has to hunt for is never counted, and proof only removes an objection if it is physically present when that objection fires. The same logic extends to comparison: buyers trust each other more than they trust you, so they will open another tab to check you out and price-shop similar products, and every departure is a chance to be sold something else. Putting the credible comparison, third-party studies and endorsements on your own page lets the prospect satisfy their need to have made a prudent decision inside your funnel — and buyers buy the product that is easiest to decide to buy. The test is not whether proof is present but whether it is **sufficient to end the comparison shopping**.

**Evidence offered:** Exposure Ninja: a client example where NHS, Deloitte and BBC logos were buried on an About page and moved into the hero; an accident-helpline teardown (Trustpilot in the hero, trust score beside the form, testimonials underneath); an ecommerce PDP with an above-the-fold review widget, secure-checkout logos and a '500k sold' badge; and an email popup reading '42,000 other people get our tips'. Teardowns and assertions, no conversion figures. Schefren: assertion, supported by the general Web 2.0 argument that prospects now ask each other for advice and 'shop' you looking for similar products at a lower price; no conversion figures.

**Fit here (7/10):** Purely editorial and zero cost on the Shopify PDP and the Amazon A+ block: move the star rating and one verbatim quote adjacent to add-to-cart, put payment and secure-checkout badges above the fold, add proof to the email capture popup, and add an on-page comparison block plus third-party testing and certification — natural deodorant is a category where the buyer *will* open another tab and compare against Native and Dr. Squatch, so retaining that session is real. Held at 7 because **the volume-based variants do not transfer** — RSC has no enterprise logos or press coverage, and displaying a small review count or list size actively undercuts credibility. The marker set has to be chosen honestly from what exists (third-party testing, cruelty-free and made-in-USA certification, small-batch) rather than padded to imitate the examples. Amazon also controls star placement on the larger channel. The comparison half is the narrower addition (worth ~5 on its own): it overlaps the every-subpage-is-an-entry-page rule above and the us-vs-them attribute cap in marketing-product-image-stack, and any competitor comparison must stay to defensible formulation facts under the body-care claim rules.
