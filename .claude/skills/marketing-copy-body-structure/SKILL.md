# Copy Body and Structure

> Split 2026-09-07 out of `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`, which still covers the opening line itself and format selection. Some "above"/"below" cross-references point back across that boundary.

## The hook only has to earn attention and stay loosely congruent — it must not become the ad's theme. Once it lands, pivot the body onto the reasons the market actually buys.

**Why it works:** Attention-grabbing premises — shock, disgust, novelty — are chosen because they stop the scroll, not because they are the purchase driver. If the body keeps returning to the hook's premise, the ad spends nearly all its word count arguing a pain the buyer does not buy on, and never makes the case that would actually convert. A good hook therefore produces a losing ad unless the theme is deliberately handed over to the market's real motives after line one.

**Evidence offered:** Two fully written versions of the same script off an identical hook, with the specific bracketed passage where V1 goes off the rails; assertion plus worked demonstration, no performance data.

**Fit here (9/10):** Durable copy principle, age-independent, and the highest-value entry here because it is a concrete audit the solo operator can run on the primary text he is writing this week — for the live $30/day Meta campaign driving soap-giveaway entries, and equally for PDP copy and email. The category's most tempting hooks are exactly the golden-thread traps: aluminium, parabens, 'what's in your deodorant'. The actual buying motives are odour control that lasts the day, no armpit irritation, and not having to re-apply. So a 'what's in your antiperspirant' opener is legitimate as a hook and fatal as a theme. Practical test on any draft: strike the hook, then ask whether the remaining body argues odour, irritation and re-application, or whether it is still relitigating ingredients. This is what the one-dominant-idea rule below was always missing — that rule says carry one idea through the piece, this one says the idea must be the market's motive rather than the hook's premise. It also sets the content of the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below: the symptom, root cause, mechanism and result steps belong to the buying motive, not to whatever stopped the scroll — and it likewise decides *which* scene the feeling-before-selling rule below is allowed to depict. Do not overcorrect into abandoning the premise entirely — the callback rule directly below is the fix for that.

## Keep the piece congruent by paying the hook's premise back once, late, as a small bonus benefit — not as the closing argument.

**Why it works:** A reader pulled in by a specific promise feels cheated if the piece never returns to it, so pure abandonment reads as bait-and-switch. But the callback costs one short paragraph and can be framed as an extra ('and here's a nice little bonus…'), so congruence is preserved without ceding the body's word count to a pain the market does not buy on.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in the V2 script, where the germ/handle premise reappears as a single bonus paragraph after the real selling points; assertion only otherwise.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable copy principle, free, runnable today by one person. It is the operational half of the golden-thread rule above and the thing that stops the overcorrection. Direct form for this catalogue: open on 'what's actually in your antiperspirant', spend the body on odour that lasts the day and skin that stops burning by day four, then close with one line — 'and yes, there's no aluminium in it either' — before the ask. Placement is after the mechanism and objection-bridge steps and before the unhedged imperative below, so the callback never displaces the CTA. Slightly below the parent claim because it is a refinement of the decision rather than the decision itself, and because at ~54 orders/month the difference between a callback and no callback will be judged at draft review, never measured.

## Never ship LLM-generated copy as-is — run a named audit for the golden thread, because next-token generation structurally turns your hook into the whole theme.

**Why it works:** An LLM builds each line off the one before it, optimising for local continuity with what was just written. Given a hook, the most probable continuation elaborates that hook — so the model will keep circling the attention premise instead of pivoting to the buying motive. The defect is systematic rather than occasional, which means the remedy is a fixed QA pass on every generated draft rather than hoping a better prompt avoids it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion grounded in how LLMs generate text; no test data.

**Fit here (8/10):** A solo operator with no copywriter is exactly who drafts ad copy in an LLM, and this names a specific, checkable defect in that output rather than generic 'edit the AI'. Applies today to the Meta primary text for the giveaway campaign, and to PDP, email and video-script drafts. Make it a literal checklist run on every draft before it ships: (1) does line one still withhold the resolution while naming its subject, or has the model summarised the ad; (2) strike the hook and check the body argues the market's motive, not the hook's premise; (3) does the premise get exactly one late callback rather than zero or five; (4) has the model reached for superlatives, per the understatement rule below; (5) is the register a spoken peer, per `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`; (6) does the draft still reach the objection bridge and an unhedged close, or has the model stopped at the mechanism, per the six-step skeleton below; (7) does the topic-bearing sentence sit at the very top, per the speed-to-value rule above, or has the model buried it behind two lines of setup; (8) read the hook alone and count the interpretations, per the isolation-read rule above, then run the clarity-rewrite prompt on it; (9) does the piece depict a scene before it offers the fix, or has the model named the feeling and asked the reader to self-diagnose ('worried about body odour?'), per the feeling-before-selling rule below — this is the single most common LLM tell in a problem-statement opener. This is the *back-end* control; the upstream-lock rule directly below is the front-end one, the brain-dump rule after it supplies the substance, the structure-and-corpus rule decides what the model was standing on in the first place, and the engine-training rule further below is what makes the audit catch less over time — on any piece long enough to be expensive you want all of them. Not a duplicate: `marketing-ai-product-imagery` holds AI QA passes for imagery (hallucination audit, text proofreading) but nothing about copy structure, and `marketing-competitor-messaging-teardown` only covers briefing an LLM off a surviving ad. The class is a durable principle about a tool behaviour, not platform mechanics, so the recency of the source is not doing the scoring work. Capped at 8 only because it is a QA discipline rather than a revenue mechanism.

## Lock the strategic decisions — awareness level, tension, opening — and confirm or redirect them before a single line of copy is written, then draft the story section by section with approval at each section rather than generating the whole piece at once.

**Why it works:** The stated failure mode is skipping straight to writing. When a native ad flops it is almost always mis-targeted — wrong awareness level, wrong tension, wrong opening — not a bad format or a bad product, and no amount of line editing rescues a piece aimed at the wrong reader. Deciding those upstream makes the target explicit and correctable while correction is free. Approving section by section then keeps a long generated piece from drifting off its own premise, because each section is checked against the locked decisions before the next one is built on top of it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, presented as 'the single biggest reason native ads don't work for people', plus the prompt sequence itself (Prompt 5 four decisions, Prompt 6 story architect).

**Fit here (7/10):** Runnable today by one person with an LLM and no budget, and it is the preventive complement to the LLM draft audit directly above: the audit catches drift after the piece exists, this catches it while it is one paragraph old. Real value at $30/day, where a mis-targeted 1,400-word asset burns a week of the operator's only scarce resource — time. Concrete form here: before drafting, write down four lines — awareness level (natural deodorant is a stage 4/5 market, so the level is usually product- or most-aware, per `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`), the tension (the shirt that still smells after the wash; the previous natural deodorant that quit by noon — written as a depicted scene rather than a named feeling, per the feeling-before-selling rule below), the opening (mid-action and in second person, per the rules above and below), and the buying motive the body will argue (odour duration, irritation, re-application — *not* the hook's premise, per the golden-thread rule above). Confirm or redirect those four before generating anything. Then have the model write one section, check it against the four lines, and only then ask for the next. The sectional check is where the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below become useful as the section list, the brain-dump rule directly below is where the section's substance comes from, and the structure-and-corpus rule after it is where the section list comes from when the format is one this skill does not already skeletonise. Held at 7 because it is a process discipline rather than a revenue mechanism, and because on a 200-word Meta primary text the ceremony costs more than it saves — reserve it for pieces long enough to drift, which for this catalogue means the long native story and long founder-voice emails.

## Before generating anything, voice-dictate a raw brain dump answering 'what do I know that most people don't know, and what do I believe that most people don't believe' about this topic — then make that dictation the substance the model writes from.

**Why it works:** The differentiated take has to come from the operator; a model can sequence and phrase it but cannot supply it, so a brief with no substance in it returns the average internet opinion on the topic. Dictating rather than typing gets more of the real, specific material out, because speech has a much lower editing threshold than a blank document — you say the thing you actually believe before you talk yourself out of it. Feeding that in is what makes the output yours rather than generic.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in the workflow (Whisper Flow or the phone mic), followed by a 'substance sheet' document of the core claims made.

**Fit here (8/10):** The founder formulates the products, so he is the only source of the knowledge that makes a deodorant script non-generic — why baking soda burns some people, what a magnesium formula does differently, why a natural stick that worked fine for a month suddenly stops. This is the step that gets that knowledge into a script instead of leaving an LLM to average the internet, and it costs two minutes of talking into a phone. Runnable today on the next Reel, the giveaway campaign's primary text and any founder-voice email. Adjacent to the structure-and-corpus rule directly below but distinct: that supplies the proven skeleton and the persisted pitch material, this is a specific contrarian-belief elicitation captured by voice before drafting — and its output is exactly what should be appended to that persistent project rather than dictated again next session. Two constraints. The substance sheet is the single likeliest entry point for an unsupportable mechanism or disease claim, because dictation is unfiltered by design: anything on it that reads as efficacy or condition language has to clear `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` and the claim-calibration and specificity rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` before it reaches a draft. And a contrarian belief is a body argument, not automatically the hook — the golden-thread rule above still decides what the piece is themed on, so the most surprising line on the sheet is a candidate opener, not the theme. Held at 8 rather than 9 because it is an input discipline judged at draft review, never measured at ~54 orders/month.

## Don't brief the model cold — look up the canonical published structure for the format you are writing, keep your real pitch material in a persistent LLM project, and have it draft against that structure, then re-edit.

**Why it works:** The format's proven skeleton supplies the sequence and your own pitch corpus supplies the substance, so the model is assembling known-good structure over known-true material rather than inventing either. A cold brief forces the model to invent both at once, which is where generic structure and fabricated specifics come from; a persisted project means every subsequent piece starts from the same true material instead of being re-explained from memory each session.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion — 'Google video salesletter structure and you will see web pages that break down the exact flow', then paste into a ChatGPT project with your pitches.

**Fit here (6/10):** Runnable today by one person at zero cost, and it supplies the upstream step the two LLM guardrails above assume but never name: where the structure comes from, and what the model is standing on. Concrete form here: keep one persistent project holding the real material — the founder's reason for formulating baking-soda-free, the dictated substance sheets from the brain-dump rule above, the voice-of-customer file from `marketing-review-mining`, the current PDP and Amazon copy, the transition-period explanation, the objection inventory from `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` — and add to it rather than re-pasting fragments per session. Then, for the format at hand, load the published flow for that format and have the model draft against it. Boundary that keeps this from duplicating the rest of the skill: for short direct-response pieces you do not need to go looking, because the structure is already here — the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below *are* the flow to draft against, and they are better tuned to this category than a generic web breakdown. Go find the canonical flow only for formats this skill does not skeletonise, which for the assets on the board means an advertorial pre-sell page for the Meta giveaway campaign, a long product-page rewrite, or a nurture sequence. For short-form video there is no published flow worth finding — use the transcript-modelling rule directly below instead, which reads the sequence off whatever is currently working in the category. Two constraints: whatever comes back still runs the LLM draft audit above, because a good skeleton does not stop the model re-theming the body around the hook; and the corpus must be real, since the whole point is that the model stops inventing specifics — a persisted file of invented customer stories just makes the fabrication reusable, per the truth constraint on the long-form native entry below. Held at 6 because it is a workflow rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and because at ~54 orders/month the difference between a corpus-fed draft and a cold-briefed one is judged at draft review, never measured.

## Before scripting, read the full transcript of the highest-performing video you can find on the same topic, turn on section markers to see how its blocks were sequenced, and write your own version block by block — modelling rhythm, flow and the order the points move in, never the wording.

**Why it works:** Studying one concrete transcript mapped onto your own topic teaches sequencing faster than an abstract framework does, because you can see exactly which job each block was doing and then ask the transposing question — 'what would the how-block be for me?'. Section markers make the block boundaries visible, which turns a wall of talking into an ordered list of jobs you can refill with your own material.

**Evidence offered:** Creator states he has done this thousands of times and calls it 'the fastest way to get better at scripting'; live demonstration reading his own top transcript.

**Fit here (7/10):** The founder is the scriptwriter and publishes his own short-form, so pulling a high-view 'switching to natural deodorant' Reel, transcribing it, marking the blocks and rebuilding the order for his own product is a today task at zero cost. Distinct from the seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton below: those prescribe a fixed structure that this skill vouches for, while this is a method for reverse-engineering the structure of whatever is currently working in *this* category on *this* platform — which is why it is also the short-form substitute for the 'look up the canonical published structure' half of the rule directly above, where no published flow exists. Three constraints. Model the sequence, never the wording — copying phrasing is both a differentiation failure and a plagiarism risk, and the substance refilling each block should come off the dictated brain dump above. A competitor's block order may lean on efficacy or condition claims this catalogue cannot make, so the borrowed order still has to survive `lib/seo-copy-health-gate.js` and the claim-calibration rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`. And a borrowed sequence is not a borrowed theme: the blocks still have to argue odour duration, irritation and re-application per the golden-thread rule above. Pairs with `marketing-competitor-messaging-teardown`, which covers reading a competitor's messaging; this covers reading their *order*.

## Build your hook and script generation engine on a corpus of deep-analyzed top-performing videos — yours plus competitors' — accept that it will not one-shot a script better than you write on day one, and improve it by taking each draft to 80–90%, hand-editing it, feeding the edited version back, and repeating twenty to thirty times until it converges on your voice.

**Why it works:** An LLM has no reinforcement on your specific voice or on your specific audience's response data, so cold it can only produce the average of everything written on the topic. Grounding it in performance-ranked transcripts supplies the second thing; re-feeding your own hand-approved scripts supplies the first. Next-token generation cannot match a competent human writer on the first pass, but every corrected draft you return is training material for the pattern you actually want, so the gap closes with iterations rather than with cleverer prompting — which also means judging the system on draft one is the mistake that makes people abandon it.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstration of the built engine's first-generation output, which the creator himself judges as 'a good starting point I could iterate off of' rather than usable as-is; assertion from experience that 'on day one this will not be able to one-shot scripts better than what you could write', with the value framed as owning a machine you can train.

**Fit here (7/10):** The founder is the only writer, so the whole loop is his to run and it is free. Its real contribution is setting the correct expectation on the one judgement he cannot outsource — whether a line sounds like a real person talking about their armpits — and telling him to keep going rather than concluding after two disappointing generations that LLM drafting does not work here. Practical form: the persistent project from the structure-and-corpus rule above already holds the pitch material, so add two folders to it — performance-ranked transcripts, and every hand-edited final script — and append to both rather than starting fresh. Complements rather than duplicates the two LLM rules above: the draft audit is a single-pass defect check on the output, the structure-and-corpus rule is about what the model stands on for one piece, and this is the persistence rule about accumulating corrections across many drafts. Two limits. RSC's posting history is thin, so the performance-ranked half of the corpus is a slow build — seed it with competitor transcripts per the transcript-modelling rule above and re-rank as your own posts accumulate. And convergence on voice does not retire the audit: the golden-thread defect is structural to next-token generation, so a well-trained engine still re-themes the body around whatever hook you dropped in.

## Induce the emotion with a depicted scene before you offer the solution — never sell directly to the emotion by naming it ('worried about body odour?').

**Why it works:** Naming an emotion asks the viewer to self-diagnose and hands them an easy 'no' — the question is answerable and dismissable in one beat. A concrete depicted scene induces the feeling instead of requesting it, and only a viewer already in that state is receptive to a fix. Feeling first, selling second: the order is what makes the offer land, because a solution presented to someone who has not yet felt the problem reads as a product claim rather than as relief.

**Evidence offered:** Analogy to shelter-dog commercials; assertion that 'nobody stops' for the direct-question version.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable structural principle, age-independent, and runnable today by one person with a phone. The equivalent for this catalogue is filming or narrating the specific moment — the hug at the door, the car ride with the windows up, the re-application in a work bathroom before a 4pm meeting — rather than opening 'worried about body odour?' or 'tired of deodorant that quits by noon?'. Applies to his own short-form scripts first, then email openers and PDP copy. Distinct from the mid-action opening rule in `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats`: that governs *where* to start, this governs the *order of emotion and offer across the whole piece*, which is a body-structure decision — you can open mid-action and still ruin it by naming the feeling in line three and pitching in line four. Operationally it is the content rule for beats one through three of the seven-beat sequence below (micro-situation, symptom, inner reaction are all depicted, never labelled) and for step one of the six-step skeleton (the problem is a scene, not a question), and it is checklist item 9 of the LLM draft audit above, since a named-emotion rhetorical question is the model's default problem-statement opener. Two constraints from elsewhere here: the golden-thread rule above decides *which* scene is legal — a scene about a paraben rash is still relitigating ingredients rather than odour, irritation and re-application — and the scenes themselves come verbatim from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` rather than being invented, which is also what supplies the successive fresh settings the one-dominant-idea rule below wants. Held at 7 because it is a drafting-and-review judgement that will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

## Sequence the body through seven ordered beats — micro-situation, symptom, inner reaction, failed explanation, pattern recognition, root cause, mechanism — rather than opening on the problem callout or the mechanism.

**Why it works:** Each beat earns a successively larger concession. The exact moment of the problem produces self-recognition; the symptom names what he already knows; the inner reaction proves you are inside his head; naming the explanation he currently holds and then replacing it creates doubt in his own model; pattern recognition makes the doubt general rather than a one-off; and only once that doubt exists does a mechanism read as necessary rather than as another product claim. Leading with the mechanism inverts this — it asks a reader with an intact explanation to care about a fix for a cause he does not yet believe in.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only — 'one of the BEST frameworks we've tested lately', with no numbers, no example ad, and no named account.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable principle class (copy structure), so age is irrelevant and the source's date is moot. This is a template a solo operator can execute today with no budget: it is exactly what the Meta primary text pointing at the soap giveaway needs, and it re-runs into PDP copy, Amazon bullets and the pre-purchase nurture emails. Natural deodorant has an unusually strong micro-situation beat available (the arm-raise at 2pm; the shirt pulled out of the wash still smelling) and an unusually strong failed-explanation beat (the buyer blames his own sweat or decides 'natural doesn't work for me' rather than the antiperspirant transition or a bacteria mechanism) — draw both verbatim from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` rather than inventing them. Not a duplicate: the individual beats echo recorded claims — the prior-attempt walkthrough and blame-absolution rules in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` sit inside beats four and five — but no existing skill records an ordered beat sequence for the *body* of an ad, which is precisely the outline the 90%-effort rule below assumes you already have. The beat list also doubles as the section list for the sectional-approval rule above, and is the in-house flow the structure-and-corpus rule says to draft against instead of going looking for a generic one. Note the sequence's own limit: it is an emotional on-ramp that stops at the mechanism, so it says nothing about proving your mechanism is the better one, about the result to expect, about the objection bridge, or about the close — the six-step skeleton directly below covers that back half, and the clean composite is beats one through seven as the front of the piece and steps three through six as the back. One caution held it back from a 9: `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` instructs cutting the root-cause education block in a saturated category, and natural deodorant is stage 4/5, so the root-cause beat must stay one belief-shifting line, not a paragraph of education. Four boundaries from this skill: beat one is the body, not the hook — the hook is the two to three lines above it, and line one still has to name its subject and withhold the resolution; beats one through three must be *depicted* rather than labelled, per the feeling-before-selling rule directly above, since 'you feel self-conscious' is the failed version of the inner-reaction beat; the beats must be about the market's buying motive rather than the hook's premise, per the golden-thread rule above; and the finished draft still has to pass the understatement filter below.

## Run the whole ad on a six-step skeleton: state the problem or want, show the mechanism that solves it, prove why your mechanism is better, state the result they can expect, bridge the skepticism by handling the main objection, then call to action.

**Why it works:** The order matches the sequence of questions a cold reader silently asks — what is this about, how does it work, why this one, what do I get, yes but what about X, what do I do now. Answering them in that order means no unanswered question is left blocking the click when the CTA arrives; skipping the superiority proof or the objection bridge leaves the reader convinced the problem is real and still unconvinced that *this* is the fix.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from claimed experience scaling brands past 8 figures — no data shown.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure), no decay concern, and directly usable this week: one person can write to a six-line skeleton in an hour, which is exactly what the live $30/day Meta campaign needs for both the soap-giveaway primary text and the deodorant statics, and it re-runs into PDP body copy, Amazon bullets and the pre-purchase emails. It extends rather than restates the seven-beat sequence above, which stops at the mechanism: the beats are the emotional on-ramp, this supplies the three closing steps the beats never had. Together they are also the house structure the structure-and-corpus rule above says to draft against for short pieces, so no lookup is needed there. Concrete form for this catalogue: problem (the shirt that still smells after the wash) → mechanism (odour is bacteria, not sweat, so the job is the bacteria) → why ours is better (baking-soda-free, so it works through the transition without the day-four burn) → result (a full workday without checking) → objection bridge (the main one here is 'I tried natural deodorant and it didn't work', answered with the transition-period explanation) → ask. Four constraints from this skill bind every step: step one is a depicted scene, never a named-emotion question, per the feeling-before-selling rule above — 'the shirt that still smells after the wash', not 'worried about body odour?'; the superiority proof and the result step are exactly where an LLM reaches for superlatives and unsupportable efficacy language, so they must clear the understatement rule and the claim-calibration and specificity rules in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` — and the same applies to any quantified comparative smuggled in from the contrast rule above; the objection to bridge is the market's real one, drawn from the review corpus per `marketing-review-mining` and from the objection inventory in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, not the hook's premise, per the golden-thread rule above; and step six is the unhedged imperative below, not 'you can learn more here'. Step one is the body's opening, not the hook — line one still has to withhold the resolution. Held at 8 rather than higher because it is a skeleton rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month the six-step version will be judged at draft review against a four-step one, never measured.

## Put roughly 90% of your copy effort into the concept and the hook — the body is a formulaic outline you can model off an existing ad.

**Why it works:** The body of a direct-response ad follows a repeatable structure (story → pain → discovery → proof → CTA) that is largely interchangeable across brands, so time spent rewriting it produces almost no differentiation. The concept and hook are the only parts that decide whether anyone reads the body at all, so effort concentrated there is the only effort that changes the outcome.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, with the supporting claim that his four-step LLM modeling process produces 'a good ad' 99% of the time in 1–2 minutes plus ~10 minutes of tweaks. No spend, CTR, or conversion figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable effort-allocation principle, age irrelevant. Directly useful now that a $30/day hand-run Meta campaign is live: one operator with finite hours building a 3-2-2 flexible ad needs two meaningfully different primary texts, and this says the two should differ in **concept and hook** while the body structure stays fixed — which is what makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings. Note what 'the hook' means in that instruction: two to three lines, per the hook-budget rule above, so the two variants should differ in topic framing *and* in the contrast they set up, not just in the first sentence. On video it means both deliverables, spoken and overlay. The seven-beat sequence and the six-step skeleton directly above are the fixed outlines this rule assumes: hold the beats and steps constant, vary the concept and the opener. Note the boundary the source's own LLM workflow ignores: cheap body copy is not unaudited body copy — the LLM draft audit above still has to run, because the model will quietly re-theme the fixed body around whichever new hook you dropped in and will default the problem step to a named-emotion question, and the corpus the model draws the body's specifics from still has to be your real pitch material, per the structure-and-corpus rule above. The verbalization-transform checklist in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles` is how you produce the two headline variants without drifting off the claim, and the angle-count rule below is the companion for the opposite job — reusing one asset across surfaces of different lengths. It also transfers to email subject lines and Amazon A+ block leads. Capped at 6 because it is a prioritization rule rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month and $30/day the payoff of concentrating effort will be judged, never measured.

## Hold attention past the hook with three scaffolds — lists, steps and stories — and state the number of items up front.

**Why it works:** Curiosity is the durable retention driver, and each scaffold embeds an unresolved question in the reader's mind for the length of the piece rather than only in line one. A numbered list creates an explicit open question ('what are the other five?'); an ordered step sequence does the same with a stated reward at the end; a story creates the implicit version ('what happened next?'). Naming the count up front tells the audience exactly what they are staying for, which is why it raises completion rather than spoiling it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus example headlines for each device, with the distinction drawn that steps carry an explicit reward while lists are more flexible. No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle and genuinely additive: the open-loop rule at the top of this skill says a hook must leave a question hanging but supplies nothing that keeps it hanging through the body, which is where a solo operator's drafts collapse into a flat paragraph. These are the scaffolds, and they map onto assets the business already needs — a '5 reasons your natural deodorant stopped working' email, a numbered switch-period routine post, a 3-step first-use sequence for the post-purchase flow that drives the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Steps are the right scaffold when there is a real end state to promise (getting through the transition period); lists when the material is a set of independent reasons; story when the raw material is a single real customer account from `marketing-review-mining` — and the story scaffold is where the seven-beat sequence above supplies the internal order, with the feeling-before-selling rule deciding that the story opens on a depicted moment rather than a stated feeling. Note that a stated count also does clarity work for the hook spec above: 'five reasons' names the subject shape as well as opening the loop. Two constraints from elsewhere in this skill: the stated count must be honest, and the one-dominant-idea rule below still applies — five list items should be five fresh settings for one promise, not five different promises. Capped at 6 because it is a structure rule judged at draft review and unmeasurable on a sub-1,000 list.

## Carry one dominant idea through the whole piece — and make that idea the market's buying motive, not the hook's premise — intensifying it by re-presenting it in successive fresh settings rather than repeating the same phrasing.

**Why it works:** A vague desire has no purchase-forcing power; concrete images give it weight, and the reader leaves with one dominant image no matter how many you present, so each additional legitimate setting reinforces rather than repeats, building emotional weight and making the price feel smaller. Repeating the same phraseology bores a sophisticated reader, so a fresh perspective is the only way to say the same thing twice; a perspective that is not genuinely fresh adds reading cost with no return. Which idea gets carried is decided by the golden-thread rule above — a piece can be perfectly unified around the wrong idea and still lose.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion framed as the opening of the 'thirteen ways to strengthen desire' section, with a stated stopping rule ('as long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire... use it'). No test data, no worked examples.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure/craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Nothing else here governs how a long PDP body, an Amazon A+ sequence or a multi-block email holds one idea across several sections. Clean form for this catalogue: one promise — you stop checking your shirt — carried through four distinct settings (the 3pm meeting, the twelve-hour shift, the gym bag on Friday, the hug at the door), each drawn verbatim from the review corpus rather than invented. Those settings are depicted scenes, not labelled feelings, per the feeling-before-selling rule above — which is the same reason a fresh 'setting' has to be a place and a moment rather than a new adjective for the same emotion. Note that the promise chosen there is a buying motive, not an ingredient premise, which is the pairing this rule now requires. The stopping rule is the load-bearing half for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which will happily generate ten near-identical restatements. The price-salience argument is directly relevant to a $12 stick sitting next to Native. Held at 6 because it overlaps the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof` and transformation-first ordering above, it must be bounded by the three-job length test in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, and it will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

## Insert explicit momentum phrases into transition sentences — 'let me explain', 'here is what you'll find', 'all I ask from you is this', 'for example —' — so interest never flags between sections.

**Why it works:** Transitions are where a reader's attention has the cleanest place to stop, because one idea has closed and the next has not opened. A short invitation to continue removes that exit by carrying the reader across the seam, and because it costs a few words it does not slow the piece down.

**Evidence offered:** A list of four example phrases with the framing that they are 'invitations to read on'. Assertion only.

**Fit here (4/10):** Durable-principle class (long-copy craft), age irrelevant and free, but the ceiling is low. Half the source's momentum material is already owned by the open-loop hook rule above and the curiosity-loop rule below, so only the transition-phrase half is additive, and it is a device for long continuous prose. The revenue-carrying surfaces are scannable — Amazon bullets and A+ blocks read in fragments, the PDP ordered by decision-relevance. The one honest home is a long founder-voice email or a transition-period education send, where the phrases are also what carries the reader across the seams between the seven beats and the six steps above — including the seam from the hook's two to three lines into the body, the seam from the depicted scene into the offer, the seam where the hook's premise is handed over to the buying motive, and the seam into the objection bridge ('yes, but you've tried natural deodorant before —'). Adopted because it is a distinct mechanism nothing else owns, but scored 4 because its application is narrow and unmeasurable at a sub-1,000 subscriber list.

## When the same piece has to fit a shorter placement, cut angles — keep the opening callout and the CTA identical. Length is angle count, nothing else.

**Why it works:** The hook and the close are the two elements that decide whether the piece is noticed and whether it is acted on; everything between them is compressible content. So a short version is not a trimmed long version — it is the same callout and the same ask with fewer angles in the middle. Trimming the hook to save room removes the only thing earning the read, and softening the ask removes the only thing converting it.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable formatting principle and practically the repurposing rule a solo operator is missing: one angle has to appear as Meta primary text on the live $30/day giveaway campaign, as a PDP block, as an Amazon bullet and as an email, and without a rule for what gets cut the four versions drift into four different promises. Concretely: write the long email version first with four angles, then cut to two for the PDP block and one for the bullet, holding the opening callout and the ask word-for-word across all four. Note what 'the callout' includes: the topic-bearing sentence and the contrast line both survive, since they are the two jobs the hook exists to do — a single-line surface is the rare case where they have to be compressed into one sentence rather than cut to one. When the long version is built on the seven beats and the six steps above, the elements that survive compression are micro-situation, failed explanation, mechanism and the ask — those are the spine, the rest are the angles you cut. Keeping the micro-situation in the spine is deliberate: compressing the scene into a named-emotion question is the cheapest-looking cut and the one that kills the short version, per the feeling-before-selling rule above. The superiority proof and the objection bridge are the first two steps to compress to a single clause each; the late premise callback is an angle, not spine, and is the first thing to go on the shortest surface, since a one-line bullet cannot read as bait-and-switch. It is the complement of the 90%-effort rule above — that one says where to spend effort when writing a new piece, this one says what is safe to remove when moving an existing piece to a smaller surface. The unhedged-imperative rule below is what 'keep the CTA identical' means in practice, with the scope limit that Amazon A+ content is policy-constrained on cart-directing language, so there the ask is the one element that legitimately changes. Modest score because it is largely a refinement of what this skill already implies, and unmeasurable at ~54 orders/month.

## Close with an unhedged imperative — 'order now', 'enter now' — repeated through the back half of the piece rather than softened into 'you can learn more here'.

**Why it works:** Once the pain has been surfaced, the product explained, the alternatives contrasted and the risk reversed, the only step left is telling the reader exactly what to do. A hedged ask leaves a persuaded reader with no instruction to act on and lets the momentum you just built dissipate; the direct form also signals that the seller believes in the product enough to ask for the order plainly.

**Evidence offered:** Two phrases pulled from Billy Mays' pitches plus the Zig Ziglar line 'timid salespeople have skinny kids'. Assertion only, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Narrowly additive: `marketing-email-design-production` owns CTA placement and bridging the ask to what was just read, and this skill owns register and pronouns, but nothing owned the ask being an *unhedged imperative repeated after the argument lands* — which is the exact failure mode of a solo operator drafting with an LLM, since brand prose defaults to 'you can learn more here'. Applies to the Shopify PDP CTA, the post-purchase and reorder emails where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign ('enter now' rather than 'buy now'). It is step six of the six-step skeleton above, arriving after the objection bridge — and after the late premise callback where one is used — and the element the angle-count rule tells you to hold constant when the same piece is cut down for a shorter placement. Capped at 5 because it is a one-line craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it is close kin to CTA rules already recorded, it must be scoped away from Amazon A+ content where cart-directing language is policy-constrained, and at ~54 orders/month no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better. The risk-reversal half of the source's pairing is **not** additive — the guarantee rule in `marketing-offer-construction` and the urgency rules in `marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing` already cover it, and bare 'order now' pressure with no expiring thing behind it is the empty version this catalogue has already ruled out.
