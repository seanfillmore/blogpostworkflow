# Awareness Level Messaging

## Match every message to a specific market awareness level, which bridges persona and goal to the copy framework.

**Why it works:** What a stranger needs to hear depends on whether they know they have a problem, know solutions exist, or know your product — mismatched messaging talks past them regardless of format quality.

**Evidence offered:** Presented as the fourth strategic prerequisite; the AI gap analysis in the demo is structured around awareness-level and market-sophistication coverage.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle class, age irrelevant. Useful for deciding what a PDP above-the-fold says versus what an Amazon listing says versus what a post-purchase email says — and retention is the binding constraint, so knowing that repeat buyers are most-aware (and need reorder/ritual messaging, not education) is directly actionable at zero cost.

*Source: Dara Denney — "AI Static Ads Masterclass (FULL GUIDE)" (5C5VhqW9HCc)*

## Rank the candidate desires on urgency, staying power and scope, then check the winner is a pain felt today.

Before choosing an awareness level, choose *which existing desire* the message channels. A product plausibly touches several, but only one can predominate — score each on three dimensions and pick the winner:

- **Urgency / intensity** — how badly it is felt right now. Constant arthritic pain outpulls a minor headache.
- **Staying power** — how quickly it satiates and how often it returns. Raw hunger outpulls a gourmet craving.
- **Scope** — how many people share it. A gas-saving accessory outpulls a repair-prevention one.

Then run one categorical gate over the winner, which is faster than the three-axis score and catches a failure the score only implies: **can the buyer feel or see the result today?** If the benefit is prevention of something that might happen later, there is no felt urgency and no demonstrable result, so there is nothing to act on now — that claim belongs below the fold as supporting reassurance, never in the lead. Selling the removal of a present pain beats selling progress toward a future goal.

One exception on the entry point: if the pain is real but the buyer has not consciously registered it, name it *at* them before offering the solution — "are you tired of knives that just don't cut?" — so the felt problem exists by the time the offer arrives. That is one confronting line, not a root-cause lecture (see the stopping rule below).

A second exception, on the prevention side: **a prevention claim does carry urgency when the imagined sufferer is someone the buyer cares about rather than the buyer himself.** Decay-preventing toothpaste sold once the ads focused the decay on the children rather than the parent; life insurance sells on the same structure. People will not act on a harm they might personally meet at some indefinite future point, but they will readily picture that harm landing on a child or spouse, and the protective motive is felt *now* even though the harm is not. So a prevention benefit that is inert pointed at the buyer can lead when pointed at a dependent. Here that is a parent choosing an aluminium-free stick for a teenager, or natural oral care for a child — and the usable version is "what you put on your kid's skin" as ingredient transparency, never an implied health warning.

Scored against this catalogue: mid-day odour anxiety is high on all three and is felt the same day, so it is the default lead. The transition-period fear is urgent and high-scope but self-limiting in staying power (it dies once the buyer is through it), so it belongs in onboarding and post-purchase rather than the hero. Aluminium-health worry is broad but low in urgency and is a textbook prevention claim — nothing the buyer can feel or see today — so it is a below-the-fold reassurance, not a headline, *unless* it is redirected at a dependent per the exception above. Plastic-free/eco is narrow in scope among people who will actually pay.

**Why it works:** A copywriter cannot manufacture desire, only channel existing desire onto a product. Choosing the weakest of the available desires caps the ceiling before a word is written, and splitting the message across several desires means none is felt strongly enough to overcome skepticism and price. The felt-today gate is the same logic sharpened: an impulse purchase requires a problem the buyer wants solved right now, and a preventative benefit gives them no reason to buy this week rather than next year. The dependent exception is the one loophole in that logic — the protective feeling is present-tense even when the harm is not.

**Evidence offered:** Schwartz: assertion with three illustrative desire pairs (arthritis vs headache, hunger vs gourmet craving, gas saving vs future repair bills), plus two asserted examples for the dependent exception (decay-preventing toothpaste aimed at children, life insurance) with no figures and no test. Schefren: assertion plus one worked headline ("are you tired of knives that just don't cut?") and an unattributed appeal to "breakthroughs in NeuroMarketing" — no test data, no cited study.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (demand selection and positioning), so the 1966 date is irrelevant. Free, editorial, and it answers a question the other copy rules here do not own: they tell you how to write to a chosen desire, not how to choose between candidate desires. That ranking directly informs the PDP hero, Amazon bullets and the Meta primary text now going live at $30/day. Held at 7 because it is a selection heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism, and at ~54 orders/month the choice will be made by judgement and never measured. The felt-today gate and the confront-the-unaware-buyer move are worth about a 5 on their own — they largely restate a conclusion the three-axis ranking already reached for this catalogue, and the neuromarketing justification is asserted with no source — but they are kept here because a pass/fail test is faster to apply than a three-way score and because "can they see the result today?" is the exact question that keeps aluminium-free out of the hero slot. The dependent-directed prevention exception is likewise worth about a 5 on its own: it is unclear the 12 SKUs address that buyer, the shared-center rule below argues against splintering a demographic slice, and any health-flavoured claim aimed at children has to clear the body-care claim rules in the conversion copy angles skill — but it is kept because it names the single configuration in which a prevention benefit can legitimately lead, and it should be run as an email subject line or Meta primary text before it touches a durable asset, per the last rule in this file.

## Check the second axis: diagnose which of five sophistication stages the category is in, and take the move that stage prescribes.

Awareness is about the individual reader; sophistication is about how much the *category* has already heard. A mature category has burned through plain benefit claims and moved on to improved-mechanism and identification angles — so a newer brand cannot lift the messaging of an incumbent, because the incumbent is playing a different game with a different level of earned attention.

The ladder, and the prescribed move at each rung:

- **Stage one — nobody has made the claim yet.** Be simple and direct: state the plain benefit.
- **Stage two — the claim is working for someone else.** Copy it, but enlarge it and outbid the competitor.
- **Stage three — the claims are no longer believed.** Lead with a new named mechanism explaining how the old promise is finally achievable.
- **Stage four — everyone has a mechanism.** Elaborate yours: easier, quicker, surer, fewer limitations.
- **Stage five — the market no longer believes advertising at all.** Abandon promise and mechanism as the lead and lead with identification — let the reader recognise himself.

The same axis sets **mechanism depth**, a three-position dial rather than an on/off switch:

- **Name it and stop** when the prospect already knows how products like yours work.
- **Describe it** when they don't — the detail they lack is what makes the promise believable.
- **Feature it as the lead** when the market is exhausted and a genuinely new mechanism is the only remaining reason to choose you. (Which is also why price competition in a tired market is suicidal — a matched promise at a lower price gives no reason at all.)

**Why it works:** Each stage burns out the device that worked in the previous one — repetition stops registering, exaggeration crosses out of believability, mechanisms proliferate until a new one no longer feels significant. The only reliable way to make an old promise fresh is to change the level at which you are competing, and eventually to stop competing on the promise altogether. On depth: detail the reader already holds bores them and costs reading time; detail they lack is what buys belief.

**Evidence offered:** Thin on the practitioner side — Denney reads a market-sophistication section out of an AI-generated audit ("this is where the market is now — improved mechanism and identification") and repeatedly notes that Dr. Squatch is "playing a much different game than a newer brand." Schwartz supplies the illustrative weight-loss progression across the stages: the direct claim "I am 61 pounds lighter... never hungry a minute", then the mechanism headline "floats fat right out of your body" with the full claim demoted to a subhead, plus the three named mechanism-depth stages with reasoning. Observation and historical illustration, no data on either side.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, and it owns an axis the awareness-level sections only gesture at. It matters unusually here because natural deodorant is saturated: "aluminum-free" is table stakes, and Native, Dr. Squatch and every private label already name a mechanism (baking-soda-free, magnesium, mineral salt, prebiotics), which puts the category at stage four or five rather than three. The practical read: the baking-soda-free mechanism is closer to table stakes than to a breakthrough, so *feature* the mechanism on deodorant SKUs (elaborated — surer, fewer limitations, survives the transition), *name it only* on the commodity items with nothing distinctive to name, and expect identification copy to outrun yet another mechanism claim. Free, editorial, no traffic or budget. Held at 6 because both sources assert the ladder rather than testing it, because its second half is partly a *constraint* on competitor teardowns (don't copy incumbent messaging) rather than a mechanism of its own, and because stage diagnosis at ~54 orders/month is a judgement call with no way to check it. The sections immediately below supply what this one leaves open: what the mechanism copy's job is, where to find the differentiator, what register to write it in, when to stop — and, on the other branch, where an honest identity angle comes from.

## Write the mechanism as a compare-and-contrast statement, not as an education block.

The job of mechanism copy in a mature category is *differentiation*, not education. So collapse the problem-mechanism and the solution-mechanism into one move: "the real problem with other products is that they don't do X — ours does." One sentence, framed against the alternatives rather than against the buyer's ignorance.

- **Wrong shape** → a section explaining why the problem happens, followed by a section explaining how your formula addresses it.
- **Right shape** → "Most natural deodorants rely on baking soda, which is what burns — ours doesn't; it uses X instead."

**Why it works:** When the prospect already accepts the problem exists, the only remaining job of mechanism copy is to give a reason to choose you. Naming the specific thing competing products fail to do converts an abstract formulation detail into that reason, and does the work of both mechanism halves in a single sentence instead of two sections.

**Evidence offered:** Worked illustration only: a $500 robot vacuum where the buyer needs no convincing the floor is dirty, so the mechanism reduces to superior suction or a UV light that kills bacteria most competitors lack. Plus a turmeric example and a hair-loss example. No performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, and it fills a specific hole — the sophistication rule above demands a named mechanism but never says what that mechanism copy is *for*. This says: differentiation. It also lines up with assets Real Skin Care already builds; the us-vs-them product graphic capped at three or four attributes sourced from competitor one-star reviews is the visual version of this move, and this is the sentence-level version for PDP body copy and Amazon bullets. Free, editorial, no traffic or budget required. Held at 7 because the differentiating attribute must be a defensible formulation fact rather than an invented one in a body-care category, and because it partly overlaps the prior-attempt walkthrough already in the conversion copy angles skill — the additive part is that the *mechanism itself* is the comparison, not a separate education block preceding it.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #7: Unique Mechanisms have changed" (social post)*

## Find the differentiator in one of four predictable places.

A named mechanism has to be anchored to something physically true about the product. In a commoditised category the true differences cluster, so run this as a checklist against your own formulation rather than treating "find a mechanism" as a creative act:

- **Delivery format** — pill vs liquid vs liposomal in the turmeric example; stick vs cream vs bar here.
- **A genuinely novel complementary ingredient** — the baking-soda-free formulation's replacement active is exactly this slot.
- **Concentration of the active** — 99% curcumin/curcuminoid in the worked example.
- **Intervening at an earlier stage of the problem** — most hair-loss treatments target the Z stage of the cycle, but hair loss really starts at Y.

**Why it works:** Enumerating the slots turns mechanism-finding into something you can run against a spec sheet. The earlier-stage variant works differently from the other three: it reframes competitors as arriving *too late* rather than as simply worse, which is a harder claim to rebut.

**Evidence offered:** Turmeric worked example listing pill vs liquid vs liposomal delivery, a complementary ingredient, and 99% curcumin/curcuminoid concentration. Plus a hair-loss P.S. example: most treatments target the Z stage of the hair-loss cycle, but hair loss really starts at Y. Illustrative only, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, and it supplies the missing generator for the sophistication rule above, which demands a named mechanism in a saturated category without saying where to find one. Every slot has an honest form here: the baking-soda-free formulation is the complementary-ingredient swap, stick vs cream vs bar is the delivery axis, and the earlier-stage angle is unusually exact — competing natural deodorants are all judged on whether they work *once you have switched*, while Real Skin Care can position around surviving the transition period itself, which is both the known churn point and the binding retention constraint. Note that an earlier-stage angle must still clear the felt-today gate above: frame it as removing a pain the buyer is living through now (the two weeks that make people quit), not as preventing a problem they might meet later. Free, editorial, feeds PDP copy, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks. Held at 7 because every candidate differentiator must be a real, defensible formulation fact, and the specificity and body-care claim rules in the conversion copy angles skill have to gate anything this generates.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #7: Unique Mechanisms have changed" (social post)*

## Cut the root-cause education block, and stop the moment the differentiator has landed.

Don't write the long "here's the REAL hidden cause of your problem" section — and once you have named what competing products fail to do and what yours does instead, stop the paragraph there.

- **Don't** explain the hidden physiological cause as if the reader has never encountered it. Buyers have seen thousands of ads and now get their category education from LLMs, short-form video and podcasts, so they arrive already holding a working explanation. Telling them the real cause is something exotic contradicts what they already believe and reads as a sales device.
- **Don't** continue past the differentiator into background science or category explanation. Every sentence after it explains something the reader already accepts — reading cost with no persuasive return, killing momentum toward the sale.
- **Do** treat the compare-and-contrast sentence above as both the start and the end of the mechanism block.

**Why it works:** The long-form formats that used to carry root-cause education no longer feel native even to the older demographics that used to sit through them, and the market's baseline knowledge has risen to meet the claim. Past the differentiator you are "wasting breath": the reader has the reason to choose you and is now being asked to keep reading for nothing.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus two observations — that "Grandma Sue" is now on TikTok so long-form VSLs no longer feel native to her either, and that in the GLP-1 category, where awareness is near-total, literally nobody spends time on mechanism copy. Self-reported scaling numbers on his own GLP-1 brand ($20M→$32M→$50M over 2.5 years) but no copy test comparing a long unique-mechanism passage against a short one. The stopping rule is demonstrated by negation on the vacuum example: he sketches the mold-and-bacteria mechanism, then explicitly refuses to continue into bloodstream, organs, inflammation, immune response and chronic inflammation ("BORING ALERT. Nobody cares"), and equally refuses 300–1,000 words explaining UV technology as a category.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning and copy-editing discipline), so age is irrelevant — the era cues here support the rule rather than dating it. It lands on a real temptation: the sophistication rule above tells Real Skin Care that natural deodorant demands a named mechanism, and a solo operator drafting PDP or A+ copy with an LLM will produce exactly the long apocrine-gland-and-bacteria explanation this says to cut. Buyers in this category already know what aluminium is, already know baking soda irritates some people, already know there is a transition period. Free, purely editorial; applies to PDP body copy, Amazon A+ blocks (where slot count and scroll depth are both constrained across a 12-SKU catalogue) and the non-buyer nurture emails. Held at 6 because it is a "stop doing" rule that protects hours and page space rather than a revenue mechanism, because at ~54 orders/month the effect of shortening a mechanism block will never be measurable, and because the stopping half substantially overlaps the information-fatigue rule already in the conversion friction audit skill — the additive part is narrow but real: that rule governs page length in general, this governs where a mechanism explanation specifically terminates, which is the exact block an LLM will happily overrun. Note that this rule sets *where the mechanism stops*; the rule immediately below sets *what register it runs in* while it lasts, and the depth dial in the sophistication section sets how much of it to supply at all.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #7: Unique Mechanisms have changed" (social post)*

## Keep the mechanism block loaded with promise — it is selling copy, not documentation.

Mechanism copy is not scientific discourse. Never let it go dull or merely factual: carry the promised outcome through the explanation, so the reader is still wanting something while the reason-why is building credibility.

**Why it works:** A neutral technical explanation gives the reader nothing to want, so they stop reading before the reason-why has done its persuasive work. Carrying the promise through the explanation keeps desire alive while belief is being assembled.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as "the first rule of mechanism copy". Assertion only, no example.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. Genuinely additive next to the stopping rule above: that one governs where mechanism copy *ends*, this governs its *register* while it runs. The failure mode is live — a solo operator drafting with an LLM will produce a neutral apocrine-gland-and-bacteria paragraph, which is exactly what this and the stopping rule together prohibit. Capped at 5 because it is a craft note inside a rule already recorded, and in body care "load it with promise" must be gated hard by the specificity and claim rules in the conversion copy angles skill or it becomes an unsupportable efficacy claim.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 3 of 3)*

## Give the product two reasons to buy: the physical need it fills, and a role the buyer gets to display.

The sophistication ladder says a saturated category needs a named mechanism *or* an identity. This is the generator for the identity half. Alongside the physical desire there is a distinct longing to act out and be acknowledged in a chosen role, and a product can serve it three ways:

- **Help the buyer master the role** — the tool that makes them the person who does this well.
- **Speed up that mastery** — the shortcut into the role.
- **Stand as a symbol that the role has been achieved** — the object that says it is already true.

The honest roles here: the person who got off aluminium, who reads ingredient panels, who buys from a small maker rather than a conglomerate.

**Why it works:** Character roles are never tested against reality, so a buyer accepts an assigned identity far more readily than a performance claim. And when competing products are functionally identical and price is neutral, the role difference is the thing that decides the choice.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with cultural illustrations: American food choices signalling youthfulness or cosmopolitanism, a book-plus-speed-reading-course-plus-bookshelf triad, a Ferrari selling on role rather than performance, the Ford → Buick → Cadillac status ladder. No test data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning psychology), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. It fills an explicitly open slot — three sections above generate the mechanism and nothing generated the identity — and natural body care has real, honest identity content to work with. Applies to the PDP hero, Amazon A+ blocks and the founder-voice Meta primary text at $30/day. Held at 6 because deodorant is invisible in use, so the display half is much weaker here than for a car; because it partially overlaps the primal-desire rule already recorded elsewhere; and because any identity claim in body care must stay a positioning statement rather than drift into an implied product claim.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 2 of 3)*

## Find the identification material in the physical product itself.

Don't invent the identity angle — audit the object. Strong primary images already exist in three places:

- **Appearance** — the cardboard tube versus a plastic twist-up.
- **Components and structure** — the short ingredient list, the baking-soda-free formulation.
- **Technical background and provenance** — small-batch, made in the USA, the founder's reason for formulating it.

**Why it works:** Image-building does not have to be manufactured; the object has attributes that already carry meaning to a buyer. Auditing appearance, construction and provenance surfaces the images the market will already accept, which is the only raw material an identity angle can legitimately be built from.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as a three-part breakdown with the assertion that each has image potential. No worked example, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class, age irrelevant, free, and it converts "find an identity angle" from a creative act into a checklist run against a spec sheet — the same service the four-places differentiator rule performs for mechanism copy. Capped at 5 because it is a generator feeding the role rule above rather than an independent mechanism, and everything it produces must clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before it ships.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 2 of 3)*

## Write down the image the market already holds, then intensify or bridge it — never contradict it.

Before drafting anything, name the primary image the prospect already assigns to this product category. Then:

- **Favourable image** → intensify it.
- **Negative or neutral image** → lower its intensity and use it as a believability bridge into a larger, more attractive image. Never discard it and assert the opposite.

Here the accepted image is blunt: *natural deodorant doesn't work, it burns, you have to reapply, there's a horrible transition.* The tempting draft — "finally, a natural deodorant that just works!" — denies it outright and gets disbelieved. The bridge move concedes the image, lowers its intensity by naming its real cause (baking soda; the transition period), and carries the reader from there.

**Why it works:** The characteristics people already assign to a category are, from the advertiser's side, facts as hard as rock — they come from the product's structure, history and the role similar products play in the buyer's life, and they cannot be argued away. Copy that denies them is disbelieved wholesale. Copy that starts from the accepted image and folds it into a larger one keeps the reader agreeing at every step. Start on his side and he will follow you almost anywhere.

**Evidence offered:** The Marlboro case — the existing virility image deliberately intensified through virile men, virile occupations and the tattoo. Plus the general assertion that discarding an unfavourable image in favour of an attractive one "doesn't work". Observational, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), so the 1966 source is irrelevant. The fit is unusually exact, and it gives a free, editorial diagnosis step that should run before any PDP hero, Amazon bullet set or Meta primary text is written. Held at 6 because the bridge move is close kin to the prior-attempt walkthrough and blame-absolution opener already in marketing-conversion-copy-angles — those govern the *buyer's* failure, this governs the *category's* reputation, and its additive part is the explicit prior step of writing down what the market already believes.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 2 of 3)*

## Aim at the shared center of the market, not at demographic slices.

Before choosing an awareness level, choose *who the message is for* — and the answer is almost always the shared center: the symptom cluster and life situation nearly every buyer in the category has in common. Put roughly 90% of the messaging effort there rather than building a separate avatar profile for each demographic slice.

- **The shared center** → what everyone in the category is living through. A 52-year-old gut-supplement buyer is dealing with menopause, lower energy, and the same bloating/sleep/mood cluster whether she is married or single, working or retired. Here: wanting off aluminium, a two-week transition to survive, and a previous natural deodorant that quit by noon or burned with baking soda.
- **The demographic slice** → marital status, children, ethnicity, employment. Reaches a fraction of the market and costs weeks of research to produce.

**Why it works:** The sub-segments people carve out of a market have far more in common than not, and that overlap is where the volume sits. Copy aimed at the shared center reaches nearly everyone; copy aimed at a slice reaches a fraction, for much more work.

**Evidence offered:** Worked illustration only: a 52-year-old female gut-supplement buyer enumerated across marital status, children, ethnicity and employment, with the shared attributes and shared symptom list listed out. No test, no account data, no comparison against a segmented approach.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning and audience scoping), so age is irrelevant. It is an unusually good fit because natural deodorant has an exceptionally tight shared center: essentially every buyer wants off aluminium, has a transition to survive, and has had a previous natural deodorant quit by noon or burn with baking soda — that cluster is already enumerated by marketing-problem-solution-inventory and the voice-of-customer agent. A solo operator at ~$2,700/mo with 12 SKUs, one $30/day campaign and a sub-1,000 list has no business generating segmented avatar briefs; the shared center is close to the whole market they can reach. Free, editorial, applies to PDP hero copy, Amazon bullets, A+ blocks and the Meta primary text going live now. Reconcile it with the one-narrow-persona-per-asset rule in marketing-product-image-stack this way: a single asset still speaks to one narrowly-stated person, but that person is drawn from the shared center's attributes (the buyer whose last natural deodorant quit by noon) rather than from a demographic segment. There are exactly two sanctioned narrowings: a split by *motive* rather than demographics (the splinter rule immediately below), and a profile *derived from retention data* rather than guessed (the rule after it). (The dependent-directed prevention exception in the desire-ranking rule is the one place a demographic-flavoured framing is tolerated, and only as a cheap-surface test.) Held at 7 because the evidence is a single hypothetical.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #6: Avatar research is overrated" (social post)*

## As a small advertiser, pick a splinter of the market defined by motive — then widen only if it works.

Every mass market contains sub-markets driven by *different motives for the same outcome*: women losing weight for appearance versus for health. A small company should deliberately take one of those splinters rather than the whole market, and let that focus alter every element of the campaign. Go for the whole market later, only if the splinter succeeds.

This does not contradict the shared-center rule above — it qualifies it. The forbidden split is demographic (marital status, children, employment); the sanctioned split is by motive, which is a difference in what the buyer is actually trying to achieve. Here the honest splinter is the baking-soda-irritation / sensitive-skin buyer: a motive-defined slice that Native and Dr. Squatch address only generically, and where a genuinely differentiated formulation already exists. Note that this splinter also passes the felt-today gate — irritation is a pain the buyer had this morning — which is part of why it outranks the health-motivated splinter as a lead.

**Why it works:** A small advertiser competing for the whole market fights incumbents head-on across all of it. Focusing the appeal on one motive pitches the message exactly at a slice the incumbents cover only in passing, so a small budget buys a sharper, less-contested claim.

**Evidence offered:** One worked illustration (appearance-motivated versus health-motivated weight-loss buyers) plus the assertion that a successful splinter can later be widened. No figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning and audience scoping), age irrelevant, free. Applies to the PDP hero, Amazon title and bullets, and the $30/day Meta primary text at zero cost. Held at 6 because it partly restates the differentiator audit in marketing-offer-construction, because a splinter that is too narrow shrinks an already-tiny addressable pool at ~54 orders/month, and because there is no way to verify the splinter beat the broad appeal — which argues for running it on the cheap reversible surfaces first, per the last rule in this file. Where the choice of splinter is otherwise a coin-flip, prefer the one the retention data already points at, per the rule immediately below.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Derive the narrowed profile from your lowest-churning customers — even if they are a minority of revenue today.

The splinter rule above picks a slice by judgement. This one picks it from the order history, which is a better source. Find what your best-retained, best-outcome customers have in common, then re-point the call-outs at only that profile — accepting that it may currently be a small share of revenue.

- **The read** → sort the last year of orders by whether the buyer came back. Look for what the repeaters share: which SKU they bought first, which scent, whether they were switching off an antiperspirant versus buying a gift, whether they bought a bundle or a single stick, which channel they came from.
- **The move** → make that shared attribute the call-out in the Meta primary text, the PDP hero and the Amazon bullets, rather than writing to the whole catalogue's buyer.
- **The nerve it takes** → the profile that retains best is often not the profile that bills most today. Re-pointing at it deliberately trades some near-term breadth for customers who stay.

**Why it works:** Customers the product actually fits get more value from it, accumulate more goodwill, stay longer and refer. Pointing acquisition at that profile also makes the messaging more specific, which lowers acquisition cost — so the same spend buys both cheaper customers and better-retaining ones.

**Evidence offered:** One portfolio case: a PR company whose lowest-churn customers were all in one niche — companies raising investor funding — which was 15% of revenue at the time. Re-targeting advertising at that niche broke a multi-year revenue plateau, cut ad costs, and started producing referrals "like clockwork." Single company anecdote, no figures beyond the 15%.

**Fit here (8/10):** Durable-principle class, and it is aimed squarely at the binding constraint — retention, with repeat buyers already 45–52% of revenue and an 18–22.5% repeat rate. A solo operator can run this in an afternoon against Shopify order history at zero cost, and the output is a call-out that can go straight into the live $30/day Meta campaign, the PDP hero and the Amazon bullets. Distinct from the motive-splinter rule above rather than a duplicate of it: that one picks a slice by judgement about competitor coverage, this one *derives* the slice from who actually stayed — so where the two disagree, this one wins. Feed the result forward into the how-to stems and the shared-center attributes: the profile it produces should still be stated as a symptom-and-situation description ("the buyer whose last natural deodorant burned") rather than a demographic. Held at 8 rather than higher because at ~54 orders/month the retention read is directional at best — a handful of repeat buyers is not a profile, so treat the pattern as a hypothesis and write it into a subject line or primary text before rebuilding the listing around it, per the last rule in this file.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 11 of 16))*

## When a segment does need naming, spend one situational opening line on it — and nothing else.

Segment specificity that actually registers is carried by a single recognisable situational sentence, not by a psychographic dossier. Write that line, keep the body copy, mechanism and offer identical, and move on. "I'm still in great shape even though I'm in my 50s, which is why I don't understand what's going on in my gut" is the whole segment treatment.

- An ad is one to four minutes of reading, so one line is the entire budget the segment framing has.
- An LLM can draft that line in seconds — it was trained on the same forums the avatar research would have mined.

**Why it works:** The reader recognises a situation, not a demographic profile. One sentence buys all the recognition available, and the research step that used to precede it adds nothing the reader can perceive.

**Evidence offered:** Two hypothetical opening lines written live for "fit dad in his 50s" and "out-of-shape retiree", offered as proof that the whole job takes seconds rather than four weeks. Assertion plus a self-authored demonstration; no performance comparison against a researched version.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class in its core claim (segment framing is one sentence, not a dossier), though the "LLMs already know it" half is a fast-moving capability claim worth re-checking rather than trusting. Genuinely usable and free here: the honest situational lines for this catalogue are already sitting in the review corpus — "I work twelve-hour shifts", "baking soda burns my skin", "I sweat more than most people" — and each can open an email subject line, a Meta primary text or a PDP section while everything below it stays fixed. This is also the natural home for the confronting line that names an unregistered pain, for the dependent-directed prevention framing ("what you put on your kid's skin"), and for the retention-derived call-out from the rule above — all as one line, with the body unchanged. Capped at 5 because it substantially overlaps the re-entry rule below (hold the proven angle fixed and change only the entry point); the additive part is only that the varied entry point can be a *situation* as well as an awareness level. Also worth stating plainly: at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list, no segment line here will ever be measurably better than another, so this is a cheap way to widen coverage, not a test — and any situational line must reflect something a real reviewer said rather than an invented persona detail.

## Judge an angle by its headroom, not only by how fast it converts out of the gate.

The awareness levels are not interchangeable slots — they trade off against each other. Narrow, lower-funnel messaging (competitor comparison, spec numbers, value math) converts fastest early because it picks off the already-convinced buyers who are cheapest to convert; that pool is small and finite, so the angle runs dry. Broader problem-aware or unaware messaging converts more slowly at first but has far more audience left to reach, so it keeps running and scales longest.

- **Narrow / product-aware** → harvests fast, exhausts fast. Right for retargeting, reorder emails, comparison sections, and anywhere the reader already knows the category.
- **Broad / problem-aware or unaware** → slower to prove out, far more headroom. Right for the PDP hero, the top-of-funnel content piece, and the subject line that has to keep working month after month.

**Why it works:** Early conversion rate is partly a measure of how pre-sold the audience already was, not how good the message is. An angle that only speaks to people who have already decided has nowhere left to go once that segment is served; an angle pitched upstream still has an audience the day after.

**Evidence offered:** One test recounted: variant A (value-based, competitor comparison, numbers-heavy) converted faster initially, while variant B (negative framing, trigger word, question format, broader appeal) ran and won longest. Single account anecdote, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (awareness/audience-size logic), age irrelevant. It supplies the tradeoff the awareness mapping above leaves implicit — that the product-aware angle harvests fast and runs dry while the problem-aware angle sustains. It is also the counterweight to the narrowing rules: a motive-defined splinter or a retention-derived profile is a deliberate trade of headroom for sharpness and better-retaining customers, which is why the splinter is meant to be widened once it works. Usable today at zero cost as a judgement rule for what a PDP hero, an Amazon A+ block, or an email subject line should lead with, and it fits the natural-deodorant reality where "aluminum-free" is table stakes and the problem-aware angle has far more people left in it. Capped at 5 because the evidence is a single unquantified test and the notion of an angle "running out" is a paid-scaling artifact that does not transfer literally to an evergreen product page — on owned surfaces it argues for placement (broad above the fold, narrow further down) rather than for retiring anything.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How to Analyze Facebook Ads Data the Right Way (The 2025 Guide)" (CCsty8R0UaA)*

## When the reader already knows the product but doesn't want it yet, pick one of six jobs for the headline.

Product-aware is the hardest slot to write for, because nothing needs explaining and the reader has already declined once. At that level the headline has exactly six available jobs — choose one deliberately rather than writing "a better headline":

- **Reinforce the desire** — make him want the outcome more than he currently does.
- **Sharpen his image of how the product satisfies it** — make the satisfaction concrete and picturable rather than abstract.
- **Extend where and when it satisfies** — new occasions, new conditions, new hours of the day it holds up.
- **Introduce new proof or documentation** — give him a reason to believe the claim he has already heard.
- **Announce a new mechanism** — a different way the old promise is finally delivered.
- **Change the image of the mechanism** — reposition the mechanism so the product escapes comparison with everything else claiming the same thing.

**Why it works:** At this awareness level the reader needs no education about the problem or the category, so the only remaining variables are how vividly he pictures the satisfaction, how widely he believes it applies, how much he believes it at all, and whether he sees your version as comparable to the others. Naming the six moves converts an open-ended creative task into a choice among six concrete jobs — and guarantees two drafts differ in *job* rather than in wording.

**Evidence offered:** Enumerated list of six tasks presented as the options facing a headline at this awareness level. Assertion only, no examples worked through, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (positioning), so the 1966 date is irrelevant. Close match for this catalogue's actual reader: virtually every natural-deodorant prospect already knows aluminium-free sticks exist and most have tried one that quit by noon, so they are product-aware and unconvinced. The sections above say match the message to that level but never say what a message at that level can *do* — this supplies six named jobs, which is the generator needed for two meaningfully different primary texts and two headlines inside the 3-2-2 flexible ad at $30/day, plus PDP hero variants and Amazon bullets. Honest reads for this catalogue: "extend where and when" is the twelve-hour-shift and gym angle; "change the image of the mechanism" is what stops baking-soda-free reading as table stakes; "sharpen the image" pulls from the appearance/construction/provenance audit above. Free, purely editorial. Held at 6 because two of the six (announce a new mechanism, change its image) are already largely owned by the sophistication ladder and the compare-and-contrast rule, "introduce new proof" is owned by marketing-review-mining, and at ~54 orders/month the choice among the six will be judged rather than measured — so run the variants as primary text or subject lines first, per the last rule in this file.

## Operationalize the framework with three how-to sentence stems, one per awareness stage.

This is the concrete execution layer for the awareness-level diagnosis above — instead of abstractly "matching awareness," write the literal headline:

- **"How to know if..."** → problem-aware. The reader suspects something is wrong but hasn't named it.
- **"How do I pick / how to choose..."** → solution-aware, in comparison mode. The reader accepts the category and is choosing between options.
- **"How to start..."** → most-aware. The reader has decided (or already bought) and needs onboarding, not persuasion.

**Why it works:** How-to phrasing signals education rather than selling, and each variant answers the specific question a buyer at that stage is actually asking, so the message meets them where they are.

**Evidence offered:** "How do I pick" was the top performer for six months for both a home decor and an apparel client; the rest is practitioner assertion, with an explicit admission that this framing makes bad YouTube content but keeps working in ads.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle class and the cleanest operationalization of awareness levels — it converts an abstract framework into three literal sentence stems. Executable solo at zero cost: "how to know if your deodorant is the problem" for top-of-funnel content and problem-aware PDP copy; "how to choose a natural deodorant that actually works" as a comparison section or Amazon A+ block; "how to start" for the transition-period onboarding email that directly serves the 18–22.5% repeat rate. The stems cover problem-, solution- and most-aware; for the product-aware-but-unconvinced reader use the six-jobs list immediately above instead. Fill each stem from the strongest-ranked desire — one that passes the felt-today gate — and from the shared center (or the retention-derived profile) rather than a demographic slice, and in a saturated category fill the body with the compare-and-contrast mechanism rather than the category claim — kept in a promise-carrying register, then stopped where the differentiator lands. The stems also map onto the headroom tradeoff: "how to know if" is the broad, long-running one; "how do I pick" is narrower and harvests comparison shoppers. And per the criteria-setting rule immediately below, the "how to know if" piece is where the standards the "how do I pick" piece is later judged against should be installed.

*Source: Dara Denney — "I Analyzed $1 Billion in Ad Spend (These 9 Hooks Win Every Time)" (I8tXqqfjIX4)*

## Set the evaluation criteria upstream, before the buyer starts comparing on scent and price.

Influence is highest at the earliest stage, before the prospect has framed their own need. So do the framing for them: in problem-aware content, define what a good solution in this category looks like, choosing criteria that project your strengths and minimise your weaknesses — rather than waiting until they are already lining products up on features and price.

- **Too late** → the buyer arrives at your comparison section holding criteria assembled from Native's and Dr. Squatch's messaging. You are now arguing against a frame instead of setting one.
- **On time** → a "how to choose a natural deodorant that actually works" education piece or A+ block that establishes baking-soda irritation and surviving the transition as the two things to judge on, published before the comparison starts.

**Why it works:** A buyer who has not yet defined what a good solution looks like will adopt the definition offered to them, and every later comparison is filtered against those criteria — whoever supplies them first shapes the shortlist. Framing the need early also lays the foundation for a longer-term relationship rather than a one-off price comparison.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion within the first buying-process step, with the added claim that framing the need also lays the foundation for a long-term relationship. No examples, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable positioning principle, free and purely editorial. Capped at 5 because it substantially overlaps three recorded rules — the "how do I pick" solution-aware stem and the market-image bridge above, and the us-vs-them attribute cap in marketing-product-image-stack. The additive part is narrow: those govern the comparison surface, this says the criteria should be set upstream in problem-aware content before comparison begins — which is also the practical reason the broad "how to know if" stem earns its headroom. Any criterion named must be a defensible formulation fact under the body-care claim rules, and at ~54 orders/month the effect will be judged rather than measured.

## Hold the proven angle fixed and change only the entry point to make a second asset.

Once an angle converts, don't rewrite it — swap only the hook and the bridge sentence so the reader enters at a different awareness level, then funnel into the identical body copy. Example: instead of the direct problem-aware open ("if your dog eats grass, do this"), open unaware ("our dogs can't talk to us when they feel off") and bridge into the same script.

**Why it works:** A hook that names the problem directly only catches people who already recognize the problem. A less-aware entry point catches people upstream and walks them into the same working argument, expanding the addressable audience without discarding the message that already converts.

**Evidence offered:** Side-by-side of the direct "if your dog eats grass, do this" hook versus the unaware "our dogs can't talk to us" hook leading into the identical script; both described as winners. Practitioner assertion, no numbers.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class, age irrelevant. This is the derivation move that sits on top of the diagnosis above: the earlier sections tell you which awareness level a surface serves; this one tells you how to generate a variant for a *different* surface without starting from scratch. It is also the practical remedy for a narrow angle with no headroom left — re-enter the same body from a broader hook rather than scrapping it. Free and solo-executable across an Amazon title vs. an A+ block vs. a PDP hero vs. an email subject line — one natural-deodorant angle, four entry points. Pair it with the how-to stems: keep the body, swap the stem ("how to know if..." → "how do I pick...") to slide the same argument up or down the awareness ladder; and where the entry point stays product-aware, vary the *job* instead, per the six-jobs list above. The entry point that varies can also be a *situation* rather than an awareness level — the one-line segment opener above is the same move on a different axis, and the unaware entry point is where the confronting "are you tired of..." line belongs. Note that only the entry point varies — the compare-and-contrast mechanism in the body stays fixed, because sophistication is a property of the category and doesn't change between surfaces. Note also that the re-entry move applies to an angle that has *already* shown signal; for an untested one, see the cheap-layer-first rule below. Capped at 6 because Real Skin Care cannot measure which variant wins — it can only reason about coverage, not test it.

## Lead with a "why is it important" hook that answers the question buyers think but never ask.

Take your single biggest value prop and answer the unasked "why does that actually matter to me?"

**Why it works:** It reads as education rather than selling, feels native rather than stuffy, and gives a fence-sitting buyer the permission-to-yes reason they were already looking for.

**Evidence offered:** Cited as a top performer for Pax over the last year (per their chief digital officer) and pressure-tested at the agency with "amazing results." No numbers given.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable principle class — an educational framing device, not a platform mechanic. Directly usable as an Amazon bullet lead-in, a PDP section header, and a post-purchase/education email subject line. Natural deodorant and oral care are exactly the categories where "why does aluminum-free matter" / "why does the transition period happen" is a real unasked objection, and answering it is zero-cost CRO-stage work — and this is the right home for a prevention-flavoured claim like aluminium-free, which the felt-today gate keeps out of the hero (unless it is redirected at a dependent, per the exception in the desire-ranking rule) but which still needs a reason attached somewhere below the fold. Pairs naturally with the how-to stems above: the how-to stem sets the awareness stage, the why-is-it-important body supplies the reason to act — and it is also the natural home for the named mechanism a saturated category demands, since "why it matters" forces you past the table-stakes claim, and the natural place to install an evaluation criterion before the buyer starts comparing. Keep that answer in compare-and-contrast form, keep the promise alive inside it, and end it at the differentiator; the "why is it important" frame is the one place most likely to slide back into a root-cause lecture. Because the body stays fixed, it is exactly the kind of proven copy worth re-entering from a different hook.

## Write a doubtful angle into the cheap reversible surface first; rebuild the expensive assets only after it shows signal.

Everything above tells you *which* angle a surface should carry. This tells you the order to commit them in. Never rebuild a landing page, advertorial, quiz funnel — or a PDP hero and a set of Amazon A+ modules — around a pain point nobody has yet responded to. Put the angle in an ad or a subject line first; carry it into the durable assets only once it pulls. A supplement brand tempted to build a whole brain-fog funnel should run brain-fog ads first.

- **Cheap and reversible** → Meta primary text on the live campaign, email subject lines to the list, a small copy tweak on the existing product page. Write the untested angle here — including an untested desire ranking, an untested identity angle, a dependent-directed prevention framing, an untested pick from the six product-aware jobs, an untested evaluation criterion, a motive splinter, or a call-out derived from a thin retention read.
- **Expensive and slow to unwind** → PDP hero rewrite, Amazon A+ blocks, listing images and title. These are earned, not guessed at.

**Why it works:** An ad is the cheapest and most reversible place to put an angle in front of a market; a lander, advertorial or funnel is expensive, slow to build and hard to unwind. Testing at the cheap layer means a failed angle costs a few creatives instead of a rebuild, and only angles that already show signal earn the expensive assets.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus a hypothetical (a supplement brand tempted to build a brain-fog funnel; test brain-fog ads first instead). No data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (test order — cheap and reversible before expensive and durable), age irrelevant. It maps onto a real temptation here: a solo operator with no designer can burn days rewriting a PDP hero and rebuilding Amazon A+ modules around an angle nobody has responded to, and Amazon copy changes are slow and awkward to unwind. The cheap layers that exist are Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign and email subject lines to the list — write the angle there first, and only carry a winner into the PDP hero, A+ blocks and listing images. One honest adjustment: the live campaign optimises for giveaway entries, not purchases, so an angle that wins on entry cost is evidence of attention, not of purchase intent — treat it as permission to write a PDP section, not to rewrite the listing. Held at 6 because at ~54 orders/month and a small list the ad-level read is directional, so the rule is really "don't commit the expensive asset on no signal" rather than a formal validation gate. In practice this pairs with the how-to stems and the re-entry move: draft the stem, run it as primary text or a subject line, and only then re-enter the proven body across the durable surfaces.
