# Competitor Messaging Teardown

## Research competitor ad libraries first and run a gap analysis on which awareness levels, personas, and formats are missing from your own rotation before producing anything.

**Why it works:** Competitors' persistent creative reveals what the market responds to; comparing their coverage to yours exposes the specific angles and formats you have no asset for, so production targets a known gap instead of guessing.

**Evidence offered:** Live demo scanning Native, Every Man Jack, Harry's, and Dr. Squatch; identifies Dr. Squatch's missing comparison and ingredient-infographic archetypes and notes Every Man Jack's top performers are all comparison/problem-solution.

**Fit here (6/10):** The underlying practice — reading competitor messaging and auditing your own coverage for gaps — is durable and free via the public Meta Ad Library, and the exact competitors demoed are Real Skin Care's category. Scored mid because the tool-driven automated version is a fast-decaying platform mechanic; the manual version is what transfers, and the output should feed Amazon/PDP copy rather than an ad account that isn't running yet.

### Rank what you find by run duration, then model the structure of the survivors.

The gap analysis above tells you what exists; run duration tells you which of it is worth copying. The Ad Library shows you a start date but never results, so longevity is the only performance signal it exposes: if a competitor has kept an ad running six months or longer, it is probably working, because nobody keeps paying to run a loser. Sort what you pulled for Native, Every Man Jack, Dr. Squatch and Harry's by how long each ad has been live, then work only from the survivors.

For each long-running ad, write down its structure rather than its words: the angle, whether it leads with a testimonial or a pain point, UGC versus polished production, how the headline is constructed, what offer type it carries. Then look across the survivors for convergence — when three competitors independently do the same thing (customer results in the first five seconds, long-form educational framing, testimonial-dominated creative), that recurring move is validated by real spend and belongs in your version. Model the pattern; do not copy the copy. The briefing procedure below is how you turn one of those survivors into an actual asset.

Because Real Skin Care is not running ads, route the surviving angles into the assets that are live: Amazon bullets and A+ blocks, PDP copy, and email subject lines.

**Why it works:** Longevity is the only quality proxy a public archive leaks. Modeling the recurring structure across several long-running competitor ads means you inherit choices the market has already paid to validate, instead of spending to rediscover them. Convergence across independent advertisers on the same structural move raises confidence that the move, not the brand, is doing the work.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion built on the transparency of the Ad Library, with the reasoning that you cannot see results but can trust duration; example signals listed (long-form educational ads persisting, testimonial creative dominating a category). No named brands or spend figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class — reading a public archive and inferring quality from persistence, not a platform feature — so age is irrelevant. Executable today at zero cost with no ad account, and it fills the hole in the gap analysis above, which sent you into the Ad Library without any way to rank what you found. Held at 6 because duration is a weak proxy at best (a well-funded brand can leave a mediocre evergreen ad up indefinitely), the source's real use case is producing ad creative this business is not running, and nothing here is measurable at ~54 orders/month.

### Turn a survivor into your own asset by briefing an LLM on its structure, then supplying only the hook.

This is the operational completion of the step above: you have a long-running Native, Every Man Jack, Harry's or Dr. Squatch ad and the instruction to model it, but modeling only becomes an asset when you brief it. Four steps:

1. **Hand the LLM the existing ad** — the full text of the survivor you selected by run duration.
2. **Ask it to analyze, not rewrite** — have it report the structure (section order, what each section does), the voice, and the word count. This is the same structural inventory the section above asks you to write down, done explicitly and reusably.
3. **Supply your own hook plus brand context** — the angle you are actually making, the RSC product facts, ingredients, and audience. The hook is the only genuinely new input required.
4. **Ask for a new piece matching that structure and length** — same skeleton, same pacing, your concept and your facts.

Two hard constraints on the output. First, the structure is what you are borrowing — never the phrasing. Lifting sentences from a competitor's live ad is a legal and credibility risk, and an LLM handed a source text will drift toward its wording unless you say otherwise; check the draft line by line against the original and rewrite anything that reads like an echo. Second, an LLM reproducing a competitor's structure will cheerfully reproduce a competitor's claim, including efficacy claims RSC cannot substantiate. Run every draft through the specificity and body-care claim rules before it goes anywhere near a listing.

Because no ad account is running, point the finished piece at live surfaces: Amazon A+ blocks, PDP body copy, and email campaigns.

**Why it works:** The winning ad's structure encodes ordering and pacing decisions someone else already paid to validate; handing that structure to an LLM as an explicit template, alongside your own concept and brand facts, means the model reproduces the proven skeleton rather than inventing an average one.

**Evidence offered:** Four numbered steps with the claim that it works "99% of the time" and takes 1–2 minutes plus ~10 minutes of edits. Practitioner assertion, no examples of output and no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Mostly durable — a briefing and modeling workflow, not a UI feature. Free, solo-executable, and it closes the gap left by the run-duration section, which says to model the pattern but never says how. Held at 6 because the output must be checked hard against the specificity and body-care claim rules, and because structure-modeling must stay strictly structural.

### Extend the same gap analysis to email, and pull the reference sends from a public archive instead of waiting on your inbox.

Same practice, different channel. Take the brands you already track in the ad library — Native, Every Man Jack, Dr. Squatch, Harry's — and get their emails in front of you two ways:

1. **Archive first (fast).** Search the brand on a public email archive such as Milled and pull three or four actual sends whose design and structure you admire. This is the quick path: you get on-demand access to their reorder, education, and win-back emails in minutes rather than waiting for a send to land.
2. **Subscribe too (slower, but shows sequence).** Join the lists anyway, because only your own inbox shows you the *order and timing* of a flow — welcome sequence pacing, how long until the first replenishment nudge, when win-back fires.

Keep five to ten emails you admire as explicit exemplars and hand the files to the generator as inspiration rather than describing the look in words. Read them the same way you read paid creative: which awareness level does each email target, which persona, which format (welcome, education, comparison, reorder/replenishment nudge, win-back)? The formats they send repeatedly are the ones that earn their place in a mature flow — the email-channel equivalent of the run-duration filter above — and the ones missing from your own sends are the gap to fill next. To turn a specific admired send into your own email, use the same structure-analyze-then-brief procedure above, with the same two constraints: borrow the skeleton, never the phrasing or the claims.

**Why it works:** Explicit exemplars give the generator a concrete visual and structural target to imitate rather than a generic average, and they surface the tonal choices a category leader has already validated. An archive removes the latency and coverage gaps of inbox-only collection — you can search any brand's real sends on demand. Email is also the surface where retention and reorder messaging lives, which is harder to see from the ad library alone.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion in both sources; no performance data. One demo pulls Liquid IV and Ring Pop sends off Milled and uploads them as inspo, showing the ungrounded output first and then the grounded one, asserting the grounded version is better.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class — competitive reference gathering, not a tool feature. Free and solo-executable: archives and subscriptions both cost nothing and reveal the retention and reorder messaging where Real Skin Care's binding constraint lives. The named archive is a small platform dependency, but the practice survives it. Scored 6 because it is a close cousin of the ad-library gap analysis above, so it belongs as an email-channel extension rather than a separate skill — and imitating a well-funded brand's design does not itself move revenue. Copy the *angle* and flow-format gaps, not just the visual polish.
