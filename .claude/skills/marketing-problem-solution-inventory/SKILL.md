# Problem / Solution Inventory

## List every problem the buyer encounters in the sequence they encounter it — immediately before, during, and after use — and for each, the four reasons they'd fail or quit: not worth it, won't work for me, too hard or confusing, takes too long.

**Why it works:** Purchases stall on unresolved friction the seller never thought to name. Walking the buyer's actual sequence in detail produces dozens of specific objections instead of a vague guess, and the four value drivers act as a generator, so each step yields four distinct problems rather than one.

**Evidence offered:** Presented as the foundational exercise the rest of the offer method depends on, to be done in 'insane detail'. Process instruction, no data.

**Fit here (8/10):** A zero-cost, no-traffic, solo-executable exercise that produces the raw input every other marketing skill here consumes. The natural-deodorant buyer's real sequence is concrete: choose a scent → first application → transition period → residue on shirts → running out mid-transition → deciding whether to reorder. Since retention is the binding constraint and the churn cause is a misunderstood transition period, an explicit before/during/after problem inventory is exactly what populates the PDP FAQ, the results timeline, the post-purchase sequence, and the next bundle. No other skill owns the generative inventory — they all assume you already have the list.

Note that the four quit-reasons here are the *negative* pole of the four value drivers, stated as objections. The eight-angle rule below runs both poles deliberately and turns each into a piece of copy; this section only needs the negative half, because that is what the problem list is made of.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 6 of 11)*

## Convert each listed problem into a solution mechanically — add 'how to' and reverse the problem into solution language — producing a checklist of exactly what the offer must deliver and what each page section must say.

**Why it works:** Reversing the problem statement guarantees the solution language answers the objection in the buyer's own terms, and doing it as a mechanical transform means you never write from a blank page: the problem list becomes the headline list.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as a mechanical transform applied to the inventory above; illustrative examples only.

**Fit here (7/10):** A free execution layer on the inventory, applying directly to PDP section headers, Amazon A+ blocks, and post-purchase subject lines — 'the transition period will make me smell worse' becomes 'how to get through the first two weeks without a mid-day panic'. Partially overlaps the how-to stems in `marketing-awareness-level-messaging`, but that skill picks the stem by awareness stage while this derives the sentence from a specific named objection. Held at 7 because **reversed problem statements drift into unsupportable claims in body care** — pair every output with the existing specificity rule before it ships.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 6 of 11)*

## Run the mirror inventory before writing: list every separate performance the product delivers, group those performances against the desires each one satisfies, and pick the single dominant performance attached to the strongest desire — that pairing is the headline.

**Why it works:** Every product is two products: the physical object (materials, construction) and the product in action (what it does for the buyer). The physical object does not sell. Writing without an explicit performance inventory means you lead with whichever feature happens to be top of mind rather than the one that maps onto the strongest desire — and it is the mapping step, not the listing step, that converts a feature into a claim.

**Evidence offered:** Process instruction only, stated as the first task in studying the product. No worked example, no data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable-principle class, age irrelevant, free, solo-executable, no traffic or attribution needed. This is the mirror image of the problem enumeration above: that exercise lists everything that goes wrong for the buyer, this lists everything the product does and forces each item to be matched to a desire. Together they are exactly the raw material the copy, PDP, A+ and email skills assume already exists. Across 12 SKUs the inventory is genuinely useful — a deodorant stick has half a dozen distinct performances (holds through a long shift, does not burn, leaves no residue on dark fabric, survives the transition, no aluminium, scent options) and only one belongs in the headline; the rest belong further down the page or in the objection answers below. Cross-check the two lists: a performance with no matching problem is a feature nobody asked for, and a problem with no matching performance is a gap the offer has to close with an artifact rather than copy. Held at 7 because it is an input-generating exercise rather than a revenue mechanism, and every performance still has to clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before shipping.

The physical half of the product is not discarded — it is placed. See the four-jobs test in the next section for where the ingredient panel, the tube, and the provenance facts earn a slot.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## The physical detail has exactly four jobs — justify the price, prove the claim is possible, assure the performance continues over time, and sharpen the mental picture of that performance. Keep a spec on the page only where it is doing one of the four.

**Why it works:** Spec detail is not persuasive on its own and reads as documentation, but each of these four jobs is a real objection the performance claim cannot answer by itself: why it costs this much, why it is believable, why it will keep working, and what it will actually feel like. Naming the four jobs gives a test for whether a given ingredient, material or construction fact earns its place, instead of dumping the whole panel because it is true.

**Evidence offered:** Enumerated four-item list stated as the use of the physical product. Assertion only, no example, no data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (copy structure), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It closes a real gap beside the performance inventory above: that rule says what the product does and which desire it maps to, but nothing says where the ingredient panel, the cardboard tube, the small-batch provenance and the made-in-USA fact belong or why. Each has an honest slot here — shea and arrowroot justify $12 against a supermarket stick, the baking-soda-free formulation proves the no-burn claim, the format assures it holds through a full shift, texture description sharpens the picture. Anything that does none of the four is documentation and comes off the page. It is also the constructive counterpart to the 'cut the root-cause education block' rule. Held at 6 because it is a placement test rather than a revenue mechanism, it partly overlaps the identification-material audit already recorded, and **every spec used as proof must clear the body-care claim rules**.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 1 of 3)*

## Close the loop in the other direction: for each performance, ask 'how does this make the buyer's life easier?' and reverse the answer into the pain it removes — that inverted pain, not the benefit, is where the copy starts.

**Why it works:** Every benefit statement contains an implied pain. Inverting the benefit surfaces that pain in the buyer's own terms, which is where the advertising has to start, because buyers respond to relief from something they feel rather than to an abstract improvement. It also catches performances the before/during/after walk-through missed: if a performance inverts to a pain that is not on the problem list, the list was incomplete.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with a single reusable prompt sentence. No worked output, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Runs the two inventories above in the direction they do not currently run — problems get inverted into solution language, and performances get matched to desires, but no step turns a performance back into the pain it removes. Concretely: 'doesn't burn' inverts to 'the last one left my skin raw by day three'; 'holds through a long shift' inverts to 'checking your shirt at 3pm'. That output feeds the PDP, the Amazon bullets and the giveaway primary text. Capped at 5 because it is one more mechanical transform inside an exercise already owned, and every inverted pain still has to clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before it ships.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "Billy Mays Marketing Magic" (blog post, part 1 of 3)*

## Generate eight angles per SKU, not one: write each of the four value drivers — dream outcome, perceived likelihood of achievement, speed, ease — and then its negative mirror — the nightmare, the risk of not acting, how slow the current trajectory is, the effort and sacrifice of going without.

**Why it works:** Buyers are moved both toward a reward and away from a loss. Covering both poles of each value term produces eight independent reasons to act instead of one benefit statement, and each pole is a separate asset you can lead with rather than a sentence buried inside a longer argument.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, illustrated with weight-loss and business examples.

**Fit here (8/10):** The cheapest volume multiplier in this file: one pass over the performance inventory above yields eight headline-grade angles per SKU with no new research, no spend and no traffic — exactly what a solo operator needs to keep Meta giveaway creative and PDP variants stocked across 12 SKUs. The four negative mirrors are almost certainly missing from current copy for natural deodorant, and they are the strong ones here: reapplying all day, the mid-afternoon shirt check, the stains on dark fabric, the natural brand that failed last time and cost two weeks of smelling wrong. This is adjacent to the four-term value equation in `marketing-conversion-friction-audit`, but that rule *evaluates* an offer; this one *generates* copy from it, including the four negatives that rule never mentions. It also completes the first section above, which uses only the negative pole and only as objections. Every negative angle still has to clear the specificity and body-care claim rules — 'the switch that failed you last time' is fair, disease and health-scare framing is not.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 9 of 16)*

## Write every benefit a second time from the point of view of the people who confer status — name them explicitly (partner, kids, parents, colleagues, boss, friends, rivals) and say how each of them treats the buyer differently after the purchase.

**Why it works:** Status is granted by other people, so a benefit described only from the buyer's own point of view leaves out most of what the buyer actually wants. Each named observer multiplies the number of concrete benefit stories available from the same single performance, and a third party noticing usually carries more emotional weight than the buyer's own perception of the same fact.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus worked examples (spouse won't nag about a risk-free purchase, competitors notice, golf buddies comment); presented as the 'Who' axis of a who/when expansion grid.

**Fit here (8/10):** Unusually apt for this catalogue. Deodorant, oral care and body care are almost entirely other-people products — the partner who notices, the person you sit next to on a shift or a flight, the kid who asks what you're using, the friend who switched after you did. Running the performance inventory above through a named observer list is a mechanical way to turn six performances into thirty angles for Meta giveaway creative and PDP copy, at zero research cost. Multiply it against the eight angles above and the grid is large enough to stop reusing the same three lines across 12 SKUs. The 'role the buyer gets to display' claim recorded elsewhere names this axis; this supplies the generative list. Two guards: observer benefits are the easiest place to overclaim in body care, so every one must clear the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, and shame framings ('your colleagues can smell you') are off-limits here — write what the observer notices positively, not what they recoil from.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 8 of 16)*

## Run each angle through the buyer's own timeline — past, present and future — so they see the consequence of buying and of not buying at each point, not only right now.

**Why it works:** People default to evaluating a decision in the present. Forcing them to visualise where their past decisions already led, and where the current trajectory ends, makes indecision feel costly today rather than neutral.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus weight-loss examples across past (teased as a kid), present (jeans won't button), future (another belt loop).

**Fit here (7/10):** Cheap for one person to apply as a third pass over the angle grid. The past leg is the strong and under-used one for natural deodorant: most buyers have already tried a natural brand and quit during the transition period, and copy that names that failure, absolves it ('it wasn't you — nobody told you week two is the worst of it') and re-frames it converts far better than a fresh benefit claim. Present is the mid-afternoon check; future is the third stick, when the transition is long over and the routine is unremarkable. Partially overlaps the now/later contrast axis in `marketing-conversion-copy-angles`, but the past leg and the consequences-of-indecision framing are new. Held at 7 because it is a third multiplier on an already large grid — use it to deepen the two or three angles that tested best, not to triple the output.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 9 of 16)*

## Commit to answering every enumerated obstacle with a concrete artifact, because a single unaddressed item is routinely the whole reason someone doesn't buy.

**Why it works:** Objections are not weighted averages — a buyer with one unresolved dealbreaker does not buy at 90%, they don't buy at all. Coverage of the full objection set therefore matters more than the polish of any one answer, and solving each obstacle with a real artifact (a guide, a template, an instruction) removes the veto instead of arguing with it.

**Evidence offered:** Asserted as the reason offers fail despite strong individual components; no figures.

**Fit here (8/10):** Natural deodorant has a short, enumerable objection list — transition period, 'it quit by noon', staining, baking-soda irritation, scent choice, melting in transit — and each can be answered with free on-page copy, an FAQ block, an Amazon A+ module, or a card in the box. CRO-stage work requiring no spend, traffic, or attribution, attacking both first-purchase conversion and the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Not 9 because `marketing-conversion-friction-audit` already owns the risk/returns/proof half, and this must be reconciled with the information-fatigue rule: **every objection gets an answer somewhere, collapsed by decision-relevance — not stacked above the fold.**

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Offers" (book, part 6 of 11)*

## Before writing, name the structural drawback the SKU carries — 'looks like work' or 'looks too narrow' — and redefine the product to remove it: simplify what looks complicated, and escalate what looks occasional by showing the many everyday situations it pays off in.

**Why it works:** Some resistance comes from the product's perceived category rather than from any specific doubt — it looks like effort, or it looks like it only matters now and then. Arguing against that impression fails, because the prospect has not articulated it and cannot be argued out of something they have not said. Restating what the product *is* — simple, or broadly useful — removes the roadblock before it is voiced.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as two of three named drawback categories with prescribed fixes. No examples worked through in this excerpt, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (objection removal), age irrelevant, free, editorial, and it lands on a real category problem: natural deodorant genuinely reads as harder work than an antiperspirant, so the 'looks like work' fix applies to the whole line. The escalation half maps onto the 12-SKU catalogue and the named-routine insert already prescribed in post-purchase onboarding — the same body bar shown paying off across gym, shift work and travel is broader appeal at zero SKU cost. Capped at 5 because both halves overlap the ease vector, the minimal-effort staging rule and the performance inventory above; the additive part is the diagnostic framing — run it as a one-line step before the inventories, naming which structural drawback each SKU has, so the problem list and the performance/desire mapping are built against the right roadblock.

*Source: Eugene M. Schwartz — "Breakthrough Advertising (reader's notes)" (book notes, part 2 of 3)*

## Source the objections from public complaints rather than from imagination — competitor one-star reviews, ad comments, and forum threads are where honest disqualifying beliefs live.

**Why it works:** People are candid in public forums and hostile comment threads in a way they never are in a survey or in a review of your own product. Those sources surface disqualifying beliefs a seller would never think to name, and each named belief becomes something the page has to answer.

**Evidence offered:** Asserted — 'people are very very honest and you can get some genuine feedback there' — presented as the external half of a signal-gathering setup. No worked output shown.

**Fit here (7/10):** Closes a real hole in the sections above, which tell you to walk the buyer's before/during/after sequence but never name a *source* for the objections. Competitor one-star natural-deodorant reviews on Amazon, and threads in the zero-waste and sensitive-skin communities, are a free high-density supply of the exact disqualifiers blocking first purchase and reorder here — 'burned my armpits', 'quit by noon', 'I gave up during the transition'. The same sources also rank the desires the performance inventory has to be matched against: the complaint that recurs most is usually the desire that belongs in the headline, and it is also the sharpest wording available for the inverted-pain transform and for the four negative mirrors above. They are also where the structural drawback shows up in plain language — 'too much faff' is the 'looks like work' roadblock stated by the buyer — and where the past leg of the timeline pass gets its wording, since 'I tried natural for three weeks and gave up' is the failed-switch story in the buyer's own voice.

And the 'write a better spin on each complaint' half must be paired with the specificity rule in `marketing-copy-credibility-and-proof`, or it drifts into unsupportable body-care claims.
