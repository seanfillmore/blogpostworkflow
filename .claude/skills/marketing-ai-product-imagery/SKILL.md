# Ai Product Imagery

## Before generating anything, run a setup prompt that points the model at your live product page URL and makes it research the product, inspect the real packaging photos, name one buyer and their specific frustration and desired outcome, collect only verified benefits/offer terms/reviews, fix a brand visual identity, and return a written six-frame creative plan with source links — generate nothing until that plan is approved.

**Why it works:** Six attractive images can still leave six unanswered questions. Forcing the research and a frame-by-frame plan first means each frame is assigned a distinct buying question to answer, and grounding the plan in the live page's own facts and packaging photos stops the model inventing product details before a single render is spent.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated end-to-end on a real brand (Winks) with the full prompt published; the creator states this plan-first step is 'the biggest improvement in this workflow'. No conversion data — he explicitly calls the outputs hypotheses, not tested winners.

**Fit here (8/10):** The solo operator *is* the designer and already generates listing imagery with AI, but nothing in this workflow previously forced a job-per-frame plan before rendering. With conversion rate the binding constraint across 12 SKUs on Shopify PDPs and Amazon listings, a step that makes every frame earn a distinct job is directly on the CRO path and runnable today at zero cost. Pairs with the reference-photo rule below rather than replacing it: the plan supplies *what each frame must say*, the phone photos supply *what the product actually looks like* — the page's own packaging photos are a research input, not a substitute for your own multi-angle shots. The brand visual identity this prompt fixes is what the hex-code rule below turns into an enforceable instruction. Which slot owns which job is still governed by marketing-product-image-stack; use that as the check on the model's proposed six frames.

*Source: Oliver Kenyon (@oliverkenyon, ConversionWise) — "How to Generate High-Converting Ecom Product Images with ChatGPT's Astra Update" (social post)*

## Run a concept step in an LLM before touching the image model: for each angle, ask for four candid moments where the pain or relief is visible, returned in a fixed format — SCENE (one sentence), SUBJECT (age range matching the buyer), EMOTIONAL READ (what a stranger feels in half a second), WHY IT STOPS THE SCROLL — expect most to be unusable, and push back with 'make these more scroll stopping, elevate the realistic pain this person experiences in their actual day'.

**Why it works:** Going straight to the image model gives it a generic request and returns generic output. A structured concept pass costs two minutes, produces twenty candidates for five angles, and forces the scene and emotional read to be decided in language before any pixels are generated.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from documented agency workflow; the named re-prompt line is reported as the one that reliably works.

**Fit here (8/10):** The plan-first setup prompt above covers product research and which buying question each listing frame answers — it does not generate candid *scenes*. This is how the solo operator turns one angle into four shootable-without-a-camera moments for Meta statics, PDP gallery frames and organic posts, at zero cost per concept. Durable workflow, runnable today. Sequence it after the angle is chosen and before any generation: concepts in language, then the base-layer prompt block below, then the believability selection pass. The fixed schema also makes the candidates comparable — you are picking between four written emotional reads, not four finished images you are already attached to. Anything the LLM writes here gets the reality audit below before it reaches the image model.

*Source: Lorenzo Pravata (@lorenzo_pravata) — "How to exploit GPT-2.5 Images for more winning ads" (social post)*

## Ground every generation in your own raw phone photos of the actual product: shoot several angles, several zoom distances, and deliberate close-ups of any fine detail (engraved text, buttons, labels), and re-upload that same set at the start of every fresh chat.

**Why it works:** The model has no ground truth for your specific item — absent your own references it averages the category and renders a competitor's size and construction, and a single flat photo leaves it guessing at shape, depth, and small typography. Multiple angles plus explicit detail shots anchor scale and physical detail to the thing you actually ship.

**Evidence offered:** Two worked demonstrations: three photos of an electric bike pump produced a primary render with correct brand text, display, hose and nozzle engravings; four photos of a diffuser produced a usable primary image on the first try. Counter-diagnosis: the generated diffuser kept coming out too large, which he attributes to the model 'referencing images online' where most competing diffusers are bigger than his. Practitioner demonstration, single products, no conversion data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Platform-mechanics class but published within weeks of the model's release, so no staleness discount. This is the single biggest unlock — and the precondition that makes every other tactic here safe — for a solo operator with 12 SKUs, no designer and no photographer, on the ~$1,800/mo Amazon channel where secondary image slots are the main conversion surface. Products are already in hand, the phone is free, and Gemini's cost is trivial against $2,700/mo revenue. Deodorant sticks and lip balms have exactly the fine detail (embossed labels, cap text, ingredient panels) the multi-angle rule protects, and a stick rendered at the wrong size or with the wrong cap is a listing-accuracy problem, not just an aesthetic one. Three mechanics below make this rule stick in practice: bind each upload to the element it supplies (per-reference addressing), save the shot set as a named reusable element so the re-upload step stops being a chore you skip, and — when one of those real photos is nearly good enough on its own — edit it surgically rather than regenerating it from scratch.

*Source: Chris Rawlings — "Nano Banana Pro for Product Photography (Step by Step 2026 Guide)" (12pQ0W2bCDE)*

## Put a scale reference in the reference photos — hold the product in your hand or set it next to money, a phone, or a laptop — because the model cannot infer size from a product shot against a blank wall.

**Why it works:** Physical scale is not recoverable from an isolated object photo, so the model invents it, which is what produces lifestyle images where the product is comically oversized relative to the furniture around it.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as a rule, then demonstrated in the negative: the first lifestyle render put the diffuser at obviously wrong scale next to a coaster and books, requiring a corrective prompt.

**Fit here (7/10):** Platform-mechanics class, freshly published, so no age penalty. Free and solo-executable, and it matters more than average here because a deodorant stick, a lip balm and a body bar are small objects whose size buyers routinely misjudge on Amazon — wrong-scale lifestyle renders read as fake and invite 'smaller than expected' returns. Not higher because it is a prerequisite step inside the reference-shooting tactic rather than an independent revenue mechanism.

*Source: Chris Rawlings — "Nano Banana Pro for Product Photography (Step by Step 2026 Guide)" (12pQ0W2bCDE)*

## Address each uploaded reference explicitly in the prompt — the @-mention syntax, or bracketed [img1]/[img2] labels — so one frame is composed from several references: this person, that product, that environment.

**Why it works:** Without per-reference addressing the model blends all the uploads and guesses which attribute to take from which image — which is exactly where garbled packaging comes from, because the label gets averaged with whatever else is on screen. Naming the reference against each noun in the sentence tells it which asset supplies the face, which supplies the packaging and which supplies the setting, so composites land first try instead of after five corrective rounds.

**Evidence offered:** Live demo: man from image 1 holding mug from image 2 standing in environment from image 3, 'followed those instructions perfectly'. Single walkthrough, no comparison against unaddressed uploads.

**Fit here (7/10):** Every lifestyle frame this brand needs is exactly this composite — real deodorant or lotion packaging, plus a person, plus a bathroom or gym setting. The grounding rule above says to upload your own phone photos; it never said how to bind multiple references to specific elements, and that gap is where the on-pack text turns to mush. Free, runnable today, and it is the control that makes multi-reference scenes safe to put on the ~$1,800/mo Amazon channel. Fast-decaying platform mechanic (the exact syntax will change), but current, and the underlying habit — one reference, one named job — survives the syntax. Pair it with the saved-element rule below so the packaging reference you address is a multi-angle set, not one flat shot, and with the style-reference restyle rule below, where 'image two' supplies treatment rather than content.

## Turn any reference you will reuse — a person, a place, or the product itself — into a saved named 'element' with several images from different angles attached, then call that element by name in future prompts.

**Why it works:** The element injects all of its angle references automatically every time it is called, so the model gets multi-angle data on the same subject instead of one flat shot or its own fading chat memory. That is what holds a face or a package visually identical across a whole batch of ads, and it removes the re-upload step at the start of every fresh chat — the step most likely to be skipped under time pressure, which is when consistency breaks.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated creating an 'Anthony in Office' character element; asserts multiple angles 'will help improve the consistency of that character'. Assertion plus one walkthrough, no side-by-side consistency test.

**Fit here (7/10):** Supports two things this catalogue already needs: product packaging that must look identical across every frame of a 6–7 slot listing (×12 SKUs, so ~80 images where drift is cumulative), and the recurring non-founder likeness marketing-product-image-stack already prescribes. It is the durable mechanism behind the 'carry the same reference set into every thread' rule above, and it makes the fresh-chat reset below cheap rather than a five-minute re-upload tax. It is also what a catalogue-wide batch pass (below) should read per SKU, so the agent works from your multi-angle phone photos rather than whatever image is currently live on the listing. One person, laptop only, no spend. Held at 7 because named-element features are vendor-specific plumbing; if the tool lacks them, the fallback is a saved folder of the same angle set you drag in every time.

## Use the image model for surgical edits to your own real photographs — remove an object from the frame, add a prop, colorize a black-and-white shot, open a subject's closed eyes — by naming only the change you want.

**Why it works:** Modern editing models rebuild only the named region and leave everything else pixel-identical, so an otherwise-good real photo gets salvaged or improved instead of reshot or replaced with a fully synthetic frame. Because the base is a real photo, the output keeps the believability a from-scratch render loses — and the packaging in frame is the *actual* packaging rather than the model's reconstruction of it.

**Evidence offered:** Multiple worked demos: phone and gimbal removed from a desk shot, a kitten added to the cleared spot, a black-and-white cat photo colorized, a blinking subject's eyes opened — before and after shown each time. Walkthroughs only, no performance data.

**Fit here (8/10):** The operator already shoots his own product and lifestyle photos on a phone, and this is how a cluttered bathroom-counter shot or a near-miss lifestyle frame becomes usable without a reshoot. Everything else here corrects the model's own near-miss *generation*; nothing covered editing a real photograph you already own. It also protects the hard rule that any claim must live on the real packaging: editing a real photo keeps the pack authentic where a from-scratch render risks a listing-accuracy problem on the ~$1,800/mo Amazon channel. Treat it as a preference order — edit a real photo where one exists, generate from references only where no real capture is possible — and it applies hardest to the main image and any frame where the label must be exact. Free, solo-executable today. The subject-lock rule immediately below is the same mechanic aimed at the background instead of a single object, and the coloured-rectangle and sketch rules below are how you say *where* the added prop goes. Still run the hallucination audit, proofread and export check on the edited file: 'leave everything else alone' is an instruction the model can quietly disobey.

## Change the environment or style around a subject while explicitly locking the subject — write the lock into the prompt ('keep the girl and the fish exactly the same, but change the image so she is waist deep in a pond'; 'keep the man exactly the same but make everything else look hand-drawn') — so one real photo yields many scenes.

**Why it works:** Naming what must *not* change constrains the model to re-render only the background, so the subject's identity, lighting and detail survive. That turns a single real capture into an unlimited set of contexts and styles, which is the cheap way to get genuinely distinct scenes rather than near-identical crops.

**Evidence offered:** Worked demos: overcast sky replaced with golden hour with the subject and fish untouched; the same subject relocated into a lily-pad pond; an office restyled as a child's drawing while the person stays photographic; a heavily specified neon studio background swapped behind the same person. Walkthroughs only, no test data.

**Fit here (8/10):** This is the production answer to a requirement two other skills already impose: Meta clusters near-identical creatives into one delivery entity, so only a new scene, subject, format or lighting condition counts as a new ad, and marketing-product-image-stack asks for a lifestyle frame per use context. One real photo of the deodorant in hand becomes a gym bag, a bathroom counter and a car console with no reshoot, and because the locked subject is a real capture the realism ceiling holds instead of the frame drifting glossy. Free, one person, today. It is the sharpest way to execute the 'change the location' and 'switch the light angle' moves in the six-move sequence below — write the lock explicitly rather than trusting the thread to hold the subject. Guardrails: the locked region is where the on-pack text lives, so re-read it in the hallucination audit after every swap; keep the new environment inside the realism ceiling; and if the swap keeps mangling the product, fall back to a fresh chat with the real photo re-uploaded.

*Source: Anthony Gallo (ContentCreator.com) — "AI Creator Course: AI image and video workflow lessons" (transcript, part 1 of 9)*

## Collect style references (Pinterest, competitor ads, the model's own research output), then deliberately close them and sketch your own visual concept from a blank page before touching a generation tool.

**Why it works:** References prime the visual vocabulary, but staying inside them means you only ever iterate on something that already exists, which produces derivative work. Closing the source and starting from blank forces an original concept; the collected references then serve as raw execution material for that concept rather than as its shape. Sarah Levinger applies the identical move to AI — let the model do the research, read the output, close it, open a blank doc.

**Evidence offered:** Both speakers describe it as their own practice — 'go on Pinterest, find various visual inspirations, then maybe close the laptop and think of really good visual ideas' / 'I force myself to read that output and then click X and open up a new blank doc'. Assertion only, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable creative-process principle with a concrete, named mechanism (collect, close, ideate blank), so it is not bare motivation. Cheap and entirely solo-runnable — this operator already produces his own statics, organic short-form and listing imagery, and the standing risk with AI tooling is that every frame comes out looking like the category average. Slots in between the approved creative plan above and the generation prompts below: the plan says what each frame must *say*, this step decides what it will *look like* before a reference or a model gets a vote. Scored below the plan-first and grounding tactics because the prescribed output is a better concept rather than a testable change to a surface, which makes it the weakest link to verify. **Scope boundary:** this is the rule for brand-led work — PDP gallery frames, organic, anything where being distinctive is the point. It does *not* govern performance statics; for those, use the converting-ad reference-board rule immediately below, which deliberately inherits from proven ads rather than starting blank.

**Why it works:** A generation pipeline reproduces the composition, framing and treatment of its references, so seeding it with proven-converting ads raises the hit rate before a word of the brief is written; an aesthetic mood board seeds aesthetics with no evidence of performance.

**Evidence offered:** Reported as one of two standing rules from the strategists who use the agency's static generator daily.

**Fit here (7/10):** Cheap and directly actionable — the operator already has access to competitor ad libraries, and the teardown skill ranks found ads by how long they have been running, which is exactly the filter this rule needs (long-running = converting; screenshot those, not the pretty ones). ## To hit a specific visual style, find a strong reference image, reverse-engineer that reference into a generation prompt, then mix that prompt with your own visual idea — this gets you roughly 80% of the way to the image you wanted.

**Why it works:** Describing an abstract idea to an image model cold leaves composition, lighting and treatment undefined, so the output drifts. A reference already encodes all of those decisions; converting it into prompt language transfers them wholesale, and layering your own subject and idea on top gives a controllable image that is original in concept while borrowing execution quality.

**Evidence offered:** Given as the summarised prompting workflow: 'figure out exactly what it is you're trying to visualize, find a really good reference, reverse engineer the reference in terms of a prompt — that'll give you 80% of the way there'. Assertion from practice, no output examples or test data.

**Fit here (8/10):** Directly operational for a solo operator already generating his own product and ad imagery, and it fills a real gap: everything else here controls how to get an *accurate product render*, nothing covered how to get a chosen *style* out of a model. Costs nothing but time and runs on today's tooling. Sequencing with the rules around it: fix the concept first (blank page for brand work, converting-ad board for paid), so the reference supplies execution rather than the idea; keep your own phone photos as the product ground truth (a style reference tells the model how to light and compose, never what your deodorant stick looks like); and treat the last 20% as the named-defect correction loop below, one fix per prompt. **Four routes, pick by what you want to inherit:** this one when you want to *borrow a look and keep your own composition*; the direct out-of-category recreation rule below when you want the whole proven layout intact; the single-element lift below when you want one piece of craft out of an in-category asset and nothing else; and the style-reference restyle rule immediately below when you want to apply a treatment to a scene you already have without describing it in words at all.

## Restyle an existing scene by uploading an image of the target visual style and prompting the model to change the style of image one to match image two — explicitly instructing it to restyle both the subject and the background.

**Why it works:** A style reference carries far more information about palette, line and rendering than a text description can, and naming subject AND background stops the model restyling only the figure and leaving an incongruent realistic set behind it.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated output: a desk photo restyled into the animation style of a cartoon screenshot. Single demo, no comparison and no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** A solo operator producing his own ad statics and organic video frames can get a distinct, non-stock visual treatment for a body-care scene from one reference he likes, and the 'restyle the background too' clause is a concrete defect fix worth carrying as standing prompt text. Distinct from the reverse-engineer-into-a-prompt route above, which converts the reference to text first; this applies the reference image directly, so address the two uploads explicitly per the per-reference rule above (image one = content, image two = treatment only). Held at 6 because a stylised frame is a secondary format for a trust-dependent skincare catalogue — never use it where the buyer needs to see the real product, and never on the Amazon main image. Free, runnable today.

## Put your brand's actual hex codes into the generation prompt ('change the background of this image to a well-lit wall with the color #XXXXXX') to pull a generated image onto brand.

**Why it works:** A hex code is an unambiguous instruction where 'our blue' is not, so the model reproduces the exact palette value rather than approximating a colour word. Doing it in the prompt means brand consistency is enforced at generation time instead of being corrected afterwards in a design tool the operator does not own.

**Evidence offered:** Worked demo: a generated headshot's background swapped to the hex code tied to one of his own product lines, producing an on-brand profile image. Single walkthrough.

**Fit here (7/10):** marketing-product-image-stack already requires fixing a brand visual identity once — palette, typography, lighting — and letting consistency come from that identity while every frame uses a different scene; the setup prompt at the top of this file produces exactly that palette. This is the concrete prompt mechanic that makes the rule executable in an AI pipeline, across Amazon infographic slots, PDP gallery frames and email hero images, where ~80 images across 12 SKUs otherwise drift colour by colour. Free, one person, today. Keep the hex clause in the standing prompt text (it survives the brevity rule) and pair it with the subject lock when you are only changing a backdrop. Scope: infographics, backdrops and brand surfaces — do not hex-force a native static's environment, where the realism ceiling and camera-roll look matter more than palette discipline.

## Screenshot an ad whose design you like from a company completely outside your industry, upload it as the reference, and prompt the model to recreate that exact ad while swapping in your product and rewriting the on-image text.

**Why it works:** Image-to-image recreation transfers the whole working layout — product placement, sale-text block, button position, type hierarchy — without you having to reverse-engineer it into words, which is where fidelity leaks. Restricting the source to out-of-category advertisers means you inherit a proven composition without cloning an ad your own buyers may recognise.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated with a lawn-care irrigation ad recreated around his own product box, including swapping the headline, sale percentage and button copy; notes the leftover competitor logo is one more prompt to remove. Single walkthrough, no performance data.

**Fit here (7/10):** Statics here are produced by one person with no designer, and the existing routes only covered reverse-engineering a reference into prose or assembling a converting-ad board. Direct recreation with per-element swaps is faster and more faithful, and the out-of-category guardrail keeps it clear of natural-deodorant competitors a shopper might have just scrolled past. Free, runnable today on the $30/day Meta surface. **Three hard conditions:** before export, confirm no competitor branding, logo, watermark or product silhouette survives anywhere in the frame — that is a legal exposure, not a polish item; address the references explicitly per the rule above so the swapped-in product comes from *your* photos rather than being redrawn from the source ad; and keep whole-layout recreation strictly out-of-category — for an in-category asset, lift one element instead, per the two rules immediately below. Scope: paid statics and offer frames, not brand-led PDP work, which is governed by the blank-page rule.

## When you admire one visual element inside someone else's asset, screenshot it and prompt the model to recreate only that element — naming explicitly what to strip out (their text, their effects, their background) — so you end up with a clean reusable base image rather than a copy of their asset.

**Why it works:** The element — a macro product shot, a particular lighting treatment — is what carried the stopping power, while the text and effects are what make the asset identifiably theirs. Isolating the element gives you the borrowed craft with none of the recognisable asset, and the stripped output becomes a base layer you can reuse across many frames instead of a one-off clone.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated once — he recreates a macro iPhone shot from a screenshot with 'no text, no flames or embers' and says it becomes an incredible base image for a future video. Single walkthrough, no performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** This is the narrower, in-category sibling of the out-of-category recreation rule above: lift one element — a macro shot of a deodorant stick, a soap-bar lighting treatment — and discard everything else, which is the only version safe to run against actual natural-deodorant competitors. One person at a laptop, no volume needed, free today. Sequencing: the stripped base feeds the two-pass headline/CTA layer, the subject-locked environment swaps and the six edit moves below, and is worth saving as a named element so the treatment stays consistent across SKUs. Two guardrails carry over unchanged — the product's own form still comes from your phone photos, not from their shot, and before export confirm no fragment of their text, logo, watermark or effects survived the strip. The boundary rule immediately below is what keeps this from sliding into reproduction.

## Hold a deliberate line between inspiration and copying: never upload a competitor's asset and ask the model to swap your face or product into it — build off one element, add your own twist, and keep re-prompting the first generation until it is distinctly yours.

**Why it works:** AI makes one-to-one reproduction trivially easy, so the restraint has to be imposed by the operator rather than by the tool. An asset that is recognisably someone else's buys no differentiation and carries reputational and legal exposure, while an element-derived asset with your own changes is both defensible and distinct.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, argued at length ('that is a degree of copying which is just unwarranted'). No cases or test data.

**Fit here (5/10):** A durable guardrail rather than a growth lever, recorded because it sits directly against the recreation rule above, which tells the operator to reproduce a whole ad layout — and that rule must not be misapplied to a named natural-deodorant competitor. The usable boundary: whole-layout reproduction is permitted only out-of-category; inside the category, lift one element and change it. Free, runnable today, no scale or people needed. Treat the first generation as a starting point, not an output: keep re-prompting with your own product, your own scene and your own copy until nothing in the frame would read as theirs to a buyer who saw both.

## Read any LLM-written image prompt before you run it and delete every attribute that contradicts reality — he struck 'blonde and curly hair' from a generated prompt because that is not his hair.

**Why it works:** A model writing the prompt invents plausible specifics it has no way to know. Every invented attribute that survives into the prompt becomes a defect in the output you then pay a second generation to correct, so the cheapest place to catch it is in the text — before any pixels exist.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated once in a Ryan Trahan-inspired generation, where the invented hair description was deleted before running; assertion otherwise. No comparison against running the prompt unedited.

**Fit here (6/10):** This moves the hallucination audit below upstream into the prompt, where it costs nothing instead of a render. It matters more than average for a formulated product: an LLM-written prompt will happily invent a pump bottle, a scent, a texture, an ingredient or a benefit claim the pack does not carry, and both the anti-fabrication rules and the image audit only catch that after the generation is spent. Apply it to every prompt a model writes for you — the numbered native-ad concepts, the six-frame creative plan, the reverse-engineered style prompt, the model's own edit suggestions, and any batch instruction an agent drafts for the whole catalogue — and read it against the physical product and the real label in hand. Free, purely editorial, solo-executable today.
