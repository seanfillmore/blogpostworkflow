# Performance Pattern Analysis

## Step 1 — Name the binding constraint before you critique copy, offers or creative, and cap the research phase rather than extending it: ask what is actually capping output right now; if the answer is operational (inventory, fulfilment, a broken flow), the correct next action is to fix that, not to rewrite an asset — and if the honest answer is "we don't know yet", the correct next action is to put an asset in front of buyers, not to run another week of avatar profiling.

**Why it works:** Effort spent on a non-binding input produces no output. If the system is capped by something operational, every hour of creative or offer work is wasted regardless of how good the work is, because the cap does not move. Naming the binding constraint first is what makes the subsequent effort allocation correct — and it is what stops the rest of this skill (tag, contrast, interrogate) from being run enthusiastically on a variable that cannot change the result.

The same logic applies to research, which is the activity most likely to masquerade as progress. Deep avatar work feels like work and generates artefacts, but nothing in it is validated: only copy placed in front of buyers with money behind it returns real information about whether an angle is right, so every additional week of profiling is another week of unvalidated guesses accumulating. Treat audience research as a capped input — enough to write, then ship — rather than an open-ended one, and be suspicious when building the research system is more appealing than the exposure of shipping.

**Evidence offered:** Single consulting-call anecdote where the founder asked four creative questions and the real answer was "get inventory". Assertion, no data. The research-cap half is weaker still: pure assertion delivered as a personality read ("most of the people who love AvatarMaxxing are actually just using it as a cope for not taking action"), with no data and no example of a research project that failed to pay off.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (diagnosis before action), age irrelevant, and it restates the standing operating reality in a usable procedural form: retention is the binding constraint here, not traffic or copy, so hours spent rewriting an Amazon bullet for the fourth time are hours not spent on the post-purchase and reorder flows where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided. Free, solo-executable, and it also names a real operational risk for a one-person catalog — a stockout on the deodorant SKU caps everything downstream and no marketing work fixes it. The research-cap corollary (5/10 on its own) lands on a live temptation: a Meta campaign is being stood up this week, and the alternative to writing two primary texts is another week of persona work — but `agents/voice-of-customer` already mines Reddit and Judge.me monthly into `data/context/voice-of-customer.md` and `personas.json` already exists, so the marginal return on more audience research is close to zero while the post-entry email sequence and the giveaway creative are unwritten. The section is held at 6 because it is a prioritisation rule rather than a revenue mechanism, it partly overlaps the allowable-CPL diagnostic ordering already in marketing-paid-campaign-structure, and the evidence on both halves is anecdote or assertion.

## Step 2 — Tag attributes before results arrive: a metrics row is not an analysis, so record each asset's qualitative properties (format, who delivered it, imagery and production quality, setting, messaging angle, awareness level) at the moment you produce it.

**Why it works:** Performance numbers tell you which asset won but carry no information about why, so acting on them alone means producing "more variants of B" without knowing which property of B did the work. Attributes recorded at production time turn a set of results into a set of testable explanations, and recurring attributes across winners become the thing you deliberately reproduce. They are also the only record that lets you later answer *how* a losing angle was executed (Step 4) rather than relying on memory.

**Evidence offered:** Walkthrough of a four-variant test slide, showing what the numeric readout omits, plus one case where the same creator was identified as the recurring driver across multiple winning ads and was re-hired on that basis. Practitioner demonstration, no controlled comparison.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (a feedback-loop discipline, not a platform mechanic), so age is irrelevant. Its native home — a Meta creative test — does not exist here, but the attribute-tagging half translates honestly onto the two surfaces a solo operator actually has: Klaviyo campaign history (tag each send by angle, format, and awareness level *before* comparing opens and clicks) and Amazon/PDP image changes (record which lever changed, which marketing-product-image-stack already asks for, so the frames can be re-run as proper A/B pairs once sessions support it). Capped at 5 because it is close kin to the decile contrast in Step 3 below — the additive part is only the explicit attribute taxonomy recorded ahead of results — and because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 subscriber list every conclusion is directional judgement, not a finding.

## Step 3 — Run a common factors analysis: put your top 10% and bottom 10% of comparable assets side by side — content pieces, ads, customers, sales call recordings — and name the discrepancies between them, because those discrepancies are the transferable skill.

**Why it works:** Doing volume produces data; the data only becomes skill when you close the feedback loop by contrasting winners against losers. The differences between the best and worst performers isolate the variable that is actually doing the work, so you can deliberately make more of what wins and less of what doesn't, instead of averaging across everything. The tags from Step 2 are what make the discrepancies nameable rather than vibes.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from personal practice — he says it is how he learns things nobody teaches, and applies it to content, ads, customer avatars, and sales scripts. No worked example, no numbers, no controlled comparison.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (a learning/feedback loop, not a platform mechanic), so age is irrelevant. Executable today at zero cost on two surfaces a solo operator actually has: Klaviyo campaign history (which sends earned opens and clicks vs. which died) and Shopify order history (what the repeat buyers who drove 45–52% of revenue have in common versus one-and-done buyers). Capped at 5 because the deciles are tiny here — ~54 orders/month, 12 SKUs, a small list, and no working attribution — so every conclusion is directional judgement rather than a finding, and the ad-and-content version of the exercise needs volume this business does not have. It also partly overlaps the top-customer identification already in marketing-retention-offers and the winner-dissection rule parked in marketing-paid-creative-testing; the additive part is the explicit bottom-decile contrast, which neither owns.

## Step 4 — Before retiring an angle, interrogate the execution: "we tried that" is an unproven verdict, not a dead one, until you can say how many ads or sends it got, how many separate attempts, in how many formats, what the actual hooks were, and whether the angle led in the hook or was buried inside a weak asset.

**Why it works:** A winning asset is a strong idea plus a competent execution, and execution has many independent failure points — hook, pacing, format, who says it, how it is written. A single weak execution therefore produces a losing result that says nothing about the angle itself, so the result gets misattributed to the idea and the idea gets permanently shelved. Forcing the interrogation converts a vague memory of failure into a specific, checkable claim: either the angle was genuinely tested across several executions and can be retired, or it was tested once and belongs back in the queue with a different hook or format. This is the same attribution problem as Steps 2 and 3 pointed backwards — the attribute tags recorded at production time are what let you answer these questions honestly instead of from memory.

**Evidence offered:** One anecdote: an angle the client said had never been a big winner became a second large winner within 21 days, cannibalising spend from his earlier ~$750k winner and projected to run ~$1M in spend. Single account, self-reported, no comparison against the client's earlier attempts.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (attribution of a result to the right variable), so age is irrelevant. It is unusually load-bearing at this scale for the opposite reason to the source's: at ~54 orders/month, a sub-1,000-person list and $30/day, nothing here has ever been tested hard enough to be declared dead, so the standing risk is retiring an angle after one flat email subject line or one static plate. The interrogation questions are free, take minutes, and apply to the surfaces that actually carry history — Klaviyo campaign results and past PDP/Amazon copy — as well as to the new Meta account. Held at 6 because it prevents a bad retirement decision rather than producing revenue, and because at this volume the honest answer to "how heavily was it tested" will almost always be "not at all", which makes the checklist short.

## Step 5 — Read an unusually high conversion rate as a narrow-funnel symptom, not a win: when you widen the top, expect the rate to fall while absolute orders rise, and optimise for total sales rather than the percentage.

**Stage:** scale — parked until the scale phase opens.

**Why it works:** A narrow funnel is a filter. Only prospects with the tenacity to self-qualify ever reach the offer, and very few people have that tenacity, so the ones who arrive convert at a flattering rate on a tiny base. Widening the lure admits more unqualified people *and* more qualified ones; the denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the percentage drops even as absolute sales climb. Reading the falling rate as damage is the failure mode — it leads to re-narrowing the funnel and shrinking the business to protect a vanity ratio. This is the same attribution discipline as the rest of this skill applied to a rate rather than an asset: name which variable moved (audience composition) before concluding anything about quality.

**Evidence offered:** IT support company client anecdote — more leads, lower sales conversion rate, treated as the expected and correct outcome. Anecdote, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** The underlying principle is durable and transfers cleanly off the phone: an unusually high conversion rate on a narrow funnel means only self-qualified people are reaching it, and widening the top should be expected to lower rate while raising absolute orders — so optimise total sales, not the percentage. That is a real guard against a live failure mode once paid traffic starts diluting a currently self-selected audience. What is missing is a readable signal: at ~54 orders/month a conversion-rate movement is noise, so the diagnostic cannot be acted on yet. Trigger: enough monthly orders that a rate shift is distinguishable from variance.
