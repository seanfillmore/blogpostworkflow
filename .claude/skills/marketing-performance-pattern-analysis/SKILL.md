# Performance Pattern Analysis

## Step 1 — Name the binding constraint before you critique copy, offers or creative, assume the visible pain is not where the constraint sits, split advertising problems from conversion problems before touching either, and cap the research phase rather than extending it: ask what is actually capping output right now; if the answer is operational (inventory, fulfilment, a broken flow), the correct next action is to fix that, not to rewrite an asset — and if the honest answer is "we don't know yet", the correct next action is to put an asset in front of buyers, not to run another week of avatar profiling.

**Why it works:** Throughput is set by the weakest link, so effort spent on a non-binding input produces literally zero additional output — the pizza kitchen doubles prep and the extra dough rots beside a ten-per-hour oven. If the system is capped by something operational, every hour of creative or offer work is wasted regardless of how good the work is, because the cap does not move. Naming the binding constraint first is what makes the subsequent effort allocation correct — and it is what stops the rest of this skill (tag, contrast, interrogate) from being run enthusiastically on a variable that cannot change the result. Only once the real constraint is removed does previously wasted work convert from expense into investment.

The hard part is that the pain of a constraint surfaces *downstream* of it — turned-away customers, idle capacity, thin revenue — so the place that hurts is almost never the place that is binding. Assume the obvious visible problem is a symptom and trace it back to the root; reframe the question "from who to what" rather than patching the presenting complaint. Handling symptoms one at a time is Whack-a-Mole: each patch spawns new symptoms because the underlying constraint is untouched.

When the pain sits somewhere in a paid funnel, one question splits the constraint in two before you touch anything: do the leads you are getting have the problem you solve *and* the money to spend on it? If no, it is an advertising/traffic problem — you are buying the wrong audience, and no page rewrite fixes that. If yes but they are not buying, it is a sales/conversion problem — the page, the offer or the follow-up, not the creative. If they are buying but there are too few of them, it is an advertising problem again, this time of volume. Cost per customer is the product of ad efficiency and downstream conversion, so separating the two failure points before changing anything is what stops you rewriting creative when the fault is the page, or rebuilding the page when you are buying the wrong people.

The same logic applies to research, which is the activity most likely to masquerade as progress. Deep avatar work feels like work and generates artefacts, but nothing in it is validated: only copy placed in front of buyers with money behind it returns real information about whether an angle is right, so every additional week of profiling is another week of unvalidated guesses accumulating. Treat audience research as a capped input — enough to write, then ship — rather than an open-ended one, and be suspicious when building the research system is more appealing than the exposure of shipping. Once you have an initial grounding in a tactic, apply it even though you will get it wrong: the information environment supplies directly contradictory expert instructions (always use a squeeze page / never use one; this font / that font), so more reading cannot adjudicate between them and only your own test can. Prudence decays into procrastination, and the "one more course and I'll be ready" belief never ends while the money to buy the courses does.

Scope that corollary strictly: it governs *starting* work, never *touching running work*. Ship fast, then leave it alone — once a campaign is live, the learning-phase freeze, the doom-cycle rule and the do-nothing rule govern instead.

**Evidence offered:** Single consulting-call anecdote where the founder asked four creative questions and the real answer was "get inventory". Assertion, no data. The research-cap half is weaker still: pure assertion delivered as a personality read ("most of the people who love AvatarMaxxing are actually just using it as a cope for not taking action"), with no data and no example of a research project that failed to pay off. The constraint-chain and pain-displacement half rests on two worked illustrations and one anecdote — the pizza parlour whose real constraint was oven capacity; Frank Kern wasting weeks hunting membership software when his real constraint was believing he needed it (reportedly 2,000 trial signups in 65 minutes once he dropped the belief); a hypothetical SEO example arguing the sales page, not traffic, is the true bottleneck — plus Jay Abraham foreword anecdotes (a radio Per-Inquiry constraint solved by rephrasing the ask, claimed to yield 1,000 stations and $13M), a medical analogy, Deming and Drucker quotes, and an unsourced "studies have shown" 90% figure. The apply-immediately half is assertion plus a two-group taxonomy (appliers vs planners) and the contradictory-guru examples, with some unsupported psychologising (perfectionism as low self-esteem). The advertising-versus-conversion split adds one case: a portfolio company spent $150k over twelve weeks, got qualified leads on the phone, blamed the ads and quit — an estimated ~$30M of enterprise value forgone. ## Step 2 — When a channel that used to work declines, the first question is "what did we stop doing in the months before the decline?" — asked and answered in writing before anyone debates which asset (creative, copy, offer, page, price) is at fault.

**Why it works:** A decline in a system that previously worked is usually a removed input, not a newly broken component. Debating components generates plausible answers indefinitely — every asset can always be argued to be underperforming, and each hypothesis costs a rebuild to test — whereas listing what changed produces a short, dated, checkable list, and restoring a removed input is usually cheaper than rebuilding an asset. This is Step 1's pain-displacement rule extended along the time axis: the pain surfaces downstream of the constraint in the funnel, and also downstream of it in time, so the month the revenue fell is rarely the month the cause happened.

The decision log in Step 6 is what makes this answerable at all. Without a dated record of what was started and stopped, "what did we stop doing" degrades into memory, which is exactly the failure this question exists to avoid.

**Evidence offered:** One narrative: a room of department heads blaming creative, copy, offer, pages and price, while the actual cause was that the founder had stopped making topical gym content — a diagnosis later confirmed by a customer survey. Self-reported story, no before/after figures, no account data, and the survey is described rather than shown.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable diagnostic, free, takes minutes, and complementary rather than duplicative — Step 1 says assume the visible pain is not where the constraint sits and Step 6 says keep a log, but neither names "what did we stop doing" as the first move on a decline. It matters more now than it would have six months ago, because a solo operator is running paid, email, organic and two marketplaces simultaneously, which means there are finally enough inputs for one to lapse unnoticed. The concrete candidates are specific: a review request that quietly fell out of the post-purchase flow, a monthly campaign send that did not go out, a Reddit or content presence that lapsed, an Amazon image or A+ module that got replaced, a bundle that was pulled. One caveat scales it down honestly: at ~54 orders/month a single soft month is often variance, so run Step 11's "common experience or isolated occurrence" test on the decline itself before treating it as a decline at all. Held at 7 because it is a question rather than a mechanism, and the evidence is a single story.

## Step 3 — Search for the constraint in three named places: rational (errors in your thinking), procedural (counterproductive work processes) and self-imposed (rules that exist only in your head) — and in particular write the self-imposed rules down and ask of each which you would discard if it meant an explosion of sales.

**Why it works:** Constraints hide differently depending on type, which is why an unstructured search misses them. A thinking error looks like a real requirement. A bad process is hard to see because it mirrors your own personality and strengths, so it feels natural rather than chosen. A self-imposed rule feels like a principle rather than a decision. Naming the three categories forces a search in places you would not otherwise look, and writing the self-imposed rules down converts an unexamined constraint into an explicit choice you can either defend or drop.

One hard caveat ships with it: discarding a rule is not automatically right. The output is a list of rules that must now be *justified* — from landed cost, capacity and the existing gates — not a licence to break them. marketing-offer-construction warns against reflexive percentage-off discounting and gates catalogue expansion at 12 SKUs; those survive the audit on their merits.

**Evidence offered:** Illustrative examples only: "I'll never ask friends or family for money", "I'll never market my product like that guy does"; plus Schefren's own account of assuming Strategic Profits needed front-end products, back-end products and JV networks, then reportedly doing $3.5M in week one and $7.4M in year one with just a free report and a back-end product. Self-reported, unverified.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (diagnosis), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial — an hour with a text file, no traffic, budget or team required. It is a generator sitting under the constraint-naming rule in Step 1 rather than an independent mechanism, which caps it. The self-imposed half has an honest form here: a solo operator at $2,700/mo carries unexamined rules — "never discount", "only sell single units", "never run paid", "never email more than once a month" — and the paid rule in particular was true until 2026-08-17 and is now false, which is exactly the stale rule this exercise is for. Run the audit, then re-derive each surviving rule from cost and capacity rather than habit.

## Step 4 — Map the conversion rate of every step a buyer passes through, run your tests at the largest drop-off, and cap the fight: if you cannot beat the current best version in four attempts, stop testing that step and move to the next-largest drop-off.

**Why it works:** Throughput is multiplicative across steps, so an equal percentage-point improvement is worth wildly different amounts depending on where it lands. On a three-step funnel of 30% opt-in / 5% apply / 50% schedule, adding five points at the 5% step doubles output (2x); the same five points at the 30% step returns 1.16x and at the 50% step only 1.1x. That converts Step 1's qualitative instruction — name the binding constraint — into a selection rule you can actually compute, and the computation needs no significance testing at all: you only have to see which step's number is smallest, which is legible at any volume.

The cap is the other half. Testing effort has diminishing returns against a single control, so once a step is near its ceiling each further attempt buys little while the next-largest drop-off sits untouched. Four attempts is an arbitrary number, but it is a *stated* one, which is the point — it makes abandonment a rule decided in advance rather than a mood decided when you are tired. Count attempts rather than weeks, give each attempt the volume Step 8 requires before it counts as an attempt at all, and write every result into the Step 6 log so the fifth idea starts from the accumulated four instead of from scratch.

**Evidence offered:** For the drop-off rule, a worked three-step arithmetic example (30% opt-in / 5% apply / 50% schedule) showing the 2x / 1.16x / 1.1x comparison — illustrative math, no account data, and the funnel shape is his own high-ticket one. For the cap, a description of a standing Monday testing schedule across his companies plus a bare assertion of the four-try threshold; no data supports the number.

**Fit here (7/10):** Durable, free, and runnable today from Shopify and Amazon reports plus Klaviyo — no attribution stack required. It points straight at the known worst step here: an 18–22.5% repeat rate is by far the lowest-converting stage in the whole chain, while PDP and listing conversion are comparatively healthy, so the multiplier arithmetic says post-purchase and reorder work beats another bullet rewrite — which is what Step 1 asserts qualitatively and this makes computable and arguable. The concrete deliverable is a single map, built once and refreshed monthly: sessions → add-to-cart → checkout → purchase → repeat purchase for Shopify, and the coarse equivalent for Amazon. Held at 7 because it partly restates Step 1's prioritisation, and because at ~54 orders/month the step rates themselves are directional rather than precise (Step 11's representativeness question applies to your own numbers too) — though the *ranking* of steps is robust even when each rate is noisy, which is what the rule actually needs. The four-attempt cap is worth 6 on its own: its logging half is already carried by Step 6, so the additive part is only the abandonment threshold, and the honest scale-down is that the source's one-week read window does not exist here — 54 orders/month will not produce a readable weekly delta — so count attempts, not weeks, and size each one per Step 8.

## Step 5 — Turn the constraint into an ordered work queue: plan by prerequisite (state the objective, surface every reason you have not already achieved it, convert each obstacle into an intermediate objective, then map which objectives depend on which), and run that queue serially — finish one project and get it earning before starting the next, picking among the unblocked candidates the one likeliest to make the most money in the shortest time.

**Why it works:** People are measurably better at naming what they don't want and what stands in their way than at naming the steps to a goal, so starting from obstacles produces a more complete list than reverse-engineering from the objective. Converting each obstacle into a sub-goal turns a reactive complaint list into a proactive plan, and dependency ordering surfaces what must be finished before anything else can start — which is what stops effort landing on a step that cannot yet pay off.

Serialising then does the rest. Profit accrues from a project's completion date, not from work-in-progress, so running three projects concurrently pushes every completion toward the end of the window and all three earn for only a few weeks; running them one at a time in the same total hours lets the first complete early and earn throughout. Dependency order says what is *allowed* to go first; "which starts producing revenue soonest" chooses among what is allowed, and Step 4's drop-off map is how you estimate that.

**Evidence offered:** For the prerequisite-planning half, assertion only — presented as the author's adaptation of a Theory of Constraints thinking tool taught to clients for years and "responsible for many of the incredible success stories", with no worked example, no named case and no figures. For the serialisation half, two illustrative Gantt-style diagrams (ABC/ABC/ABC finishing at weeks 7/8/9 = 3 profit-weeks, versus AAA/BBB/CCC finishing at weeks 3/6/9 = 9 profit-weeks) with a claimed "300% more profit" — illustrative arithmetic only, no account data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (planning and effort allocation), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. It extends Step 1 in a real direction: that rule says name the binding constraint before critiquing an asset but does not say how to convert a constraint into an ordered work queue. The dependency step is the useful half for a solo operator with no team, because it enforces sequencing already scattered across the library — finish the PDP before generating emails from it, build one flow before adding the next, establish a cost-per-entry baseline before touching creative. Concretely: the obstacle "buyers quit in week two" becomes the objective "publish a dated transition timeline", which is a prerequisite for the post-purchase education emails generated from it. The serialisation failure mode is live right now: one operator is simultaneously standing up a $30/day Meta campaign, needs a post-entry email sequence, has an unbuilt subscribe-and-save cadence and a PDP that wants a results timeline, and the natural move is to half-build all four. Capped at 5 because "build one at a time" is already recorded twice (marketing-lifecycle-email-flows; marketing-upsell-offer-design) and the section partly restates Step 1 and the offer-type staging rule in marketing-upsell-offer-design; the additive parts are the obstacle-to-objective conversion, the explicit dependency map, and the ordering criterion (revenue soonest, not most interesting). At ~54 orders/month a completed project's revenue signal is directional over weeks, so the sequencing is judged rather than measured. The "fuzzy vision / trajectory" material in the source is motivational and is not adopted.

## Step 6 — Tag attributes before results arrive and keep one written decision log: a metrics row is not an analysis, so record each asset's qualitative properties (format, who delivered it, imagery and production quality, setting, messaging angle, awareness level) at the moment you produce it — and record every marketing change in the same place (which surface, what changed, why, what you expected, what actually happened, and the reason for any miss).

**Why it works:** Performance numbers tell you which asset won but carry no information about why, so acting on them alone means producing "more variants of B" without knowing which property of B did the work. Attributes recorded at production time turn a set of results into a set of testable explanations, and recurring attributes across winners become the thing you deliberately reproduce. They are also the only record that lets you later answer *how* a losing angle was executed (Step 9) rather than relying on memory.

The decision log generalises the same discipline past creative assets to everything else you change — offers, prices, subject lines, PDP copy, campaign edits. Writing the change down forces the thinking to be explicit and makes it retrievable, so past decisions and misses can be re-read and checked rather than reconstructed; requiring a stated reason for every miss is the only accountability mechanism a solo operator has, since nobody else is holding one; and scanning back over the log surfaces recurring positive and negative patterns that are invisible in the moment — including the repeats that Step 13 says to answer with a written process rather than another fix. It is also the only thing that makes Step 2's "what did we stop doing" answerable, and the place Step 4's attempt count and Step 12's review dates live. A fixed slot (weekly at this volume, not daily) is what converts the log from an intention into a record.

**Evidence offered:** Walkthrough of a four-variant test slide, showing what the numeric readout omits, plus one case where the same creator was identified as the recurring driver across multiple winning ads and was re-hired on that basis. Practitioner demonstration, no controlled comparison. The log half is weaker: twenty years of personal practice plus self-reported life outcomes (grades from D's and F's to summa cum laude, partnerships with Jay Abraham, Gary Halbert, John Carlton, building Strategic Profits) and the claim that the journal birthed the 'YOU' diagram behind the Internet Business Manifesto — anecdotal and entirely self-reported, with no data connecting the practice to any business result.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (a feedback-loop discipline, not a platform mechanic), so age is irrelevant. Its native home — a Meta creative test — does not exist here, but the attribute-tagging half translates honestly onto the two surfaces a solo operator actually has: Klaviyo campaign history (tag each send by angle, format, and awareness level *before* comparing opens and clicks) and Amazon/PDP image changes (record which lever changed, which marketing-product-image-stack already asks for, so the frames can be re-run as proper A/B pairs once sessions support it). Capped at 5 because it is close kin to the decile contrast in Step 7 below — the additive part is only the explicit attribute taxonomy recorded ahead of results — and because at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 subscriber list every conclusion is directional judgement, not a finding. The decision log is worth 5 on its own and matters more here than at scale precisely because nothing is statistically readable at this volume, so memory is otherwise the only record and Step 9's "we tried that" interrogation has nothing to check against; it is capped because it substantially overlaps the tag-at-production-time rule above and marketing-product-image-stack's written record of which lever changed on each frame, with the additive part being only that the log also covers offers, prices, emails and campaign edits. The personal-development uses of journaling (feelings, relationships, self-awareness) have no marketing translation and are not adopted.

## Step 7 — Run a common factors analysis: put your top 10% and bottom 10% of comparable assets side by side — content pieces, ads, customers, sales call recordings — and name the discrepancies between them, because those discrepancies are the transferable skill.

**Why it works:** Doing volume produces data; the data only becomes skill when you close the feedback loop by contrasting winners against losers. The differences between the best and worst performers isolate the variable that is actually doing the work, so you can deliberately make more of what wins and less of what doesn't, instead of averaging across everything. The tags from Step 6 are what make the discrepancies nameable rather than vibes.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from personal practice — he says it is how he learns things nobody teaches, and applies it to content, ads, customer avatars, and sales scripts. No worked example, no numbers, no controlled comparison.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (a learning/feedback loop, not a platform mechanic), so age is irrelevant. Executable today at zero cost on two surfaces a solo operator actually has: Klaviyo campaign history (which sends earned opens and clicks vs. which died) and Shopify order history (what the repeat buyers who drove 45–52% of revenue have in common versus one-and-done buyers). Capped at 5 because the deciles are tiny here — ~54 orders/month, 12 SKUs, a small list, and no working attribution — so every conclusion is directional judgement rather than a finding, and the ad-and-content version of the exercise needs volume this business does not have. It also partly overlaps the top-customer identification already in marketing-retention-offers and the winner-dissection rule parked in marketing-paid-creative-testing; the additive part is the explicit bottom-decile contrast, which neither owns.

## Step 8 — Compute the minimum volume a test needs before you run it: derive the sample size from the expected response rate, and if you cannot afford that volume, run the thing as an unmeasured ship with no verdict attached rather than treating the null as evidence about the channel.

**Why it works:** At a 0.5–1% expected response, 300 flyers have an expected yield of one and a half to three responses, so every outcome from zero to five is fully consistent with the channel working exactly as advertised. The result is dominated by noise, but the operator reads it as a verdict — "this channel doesn't work" — and permanently discards an option when the only thing demonstrated was insufficient volume. Doing the arithmetic first forces the decision into one of two honest shapes: either the test is affordable at readable size and worth running, or it is not, in which case you ship without claiming a measurement and leave the idea in the queue. This is Step 9's interrogation pointed forwards — Step 9 asks, after a flat result, whether the execution or the idea failed; this asks, before the spend, whether any result could have been readable — and it is the volume counterpart to Step 12's horizon in time.

**Evidence offered:** The flyer anecdote — 300 flyers put out, one angry phone call, zero leads — set against a mentor who tested with 5,000 per day and treated a 0.5–1% response as decent-to-winning. Anecdote plus response-rate arithmetic; no controlled comparison and no worked sample-size calculation beyond the rate itself.

**Fit here (7/10):** Probably the single most relevant idea in this skill for a business at ~54 orders/month, a sub-1,000-person list and $30/day, because nearly every test available here is under-powered by default and the failure it prevents — retiring a good angle after two sends or four days of spend — is the standing risk. Worked forward: a 2% purchase rate on a 900-person send is ~18 orders, so a subject-line A/B cannot separate a 10% lift from noise; four days at $30/day is a few thousand impressions, which will not produce a readable purchase rate no matter how the creative is judged. The output is not paralysis — it is knowing in advance which tests can return verdicts (image-vs-image on the highest-traffic Amazon listing over a month; a flow present versus absent over a quarter; the repeat rate measured across two quarters) and which are simply ships to be judged qualitatively and re-queued rather than declared dead. It also sizes the attempts Step 4 counts. Free, no people, no budget. Held at 7 because it prevents bad retirement decisions rather than producing revenue, and because at this volume its answer is usually "you cannot power this" — useful the first several times, repetitive after that.

## Step 9 — Before retiring an angle, interrogate the execution and run down the production stack: "we tried that" is an unproven verdict, not a dead one, until you can say how many ads or sends it got, how many separate attempts, in how many formats, what the actual hooks were, whether the angle led in the hook or was buried inside a weak asset — and which link in the production chain (editor, creator, voiceover, image, opening shot, opening caption, pacing, sound, product demonstration, assembly, copy) might have broken instead of the idea.

**Why it works:** A winning asset is a strong idea multiplied through a chain of independent execution decisions, not a single artefact. Hook, pacing, format, imagery, who says it, how it is written — each is a separate failure point, and any one broken link kills an otherwise good ad. A result is therefore only attributable to the copy or the angle if every other link was competent; when one is broken, the flat result gets misread as "the angle doesn't work" and a viable idea is permanently shelved on the evidence of one bad execution. Forcing the interrogation converts a vague memory of failure into a specific, checkable claim: either the angle was genuinely tested across several competent executions and can be retired, or it was tested once, through a broken link, and belongs back in the queue with a different hook, image or format. This is the same attribution problem as Steps 6 and 7 pointed backwards — the attribute tags and decision log recorded at production time are what let you answer these questions honestly instead of from memory — and the first question to ask is Step 8's: was there ever enough volume behind it for a verdict to exist?

The corollary matters more here than in an agency: the length of the chain determines how readable a flat result is. For a solo operator shipping statics, the controllable stack collapses to image plus copy — no editor, no creator, no voiceover, no pacing or sound — so far fewer links can silently break. That is precisely why a flat static is more readable than a flat video, and why an angle can be trusted (or retired) sooner here than in the source's context. Ship statics, and the interrogation shortens to two questions: was the image wrong, or was the copy wrong?

**Evidence offered:** One anecdote: an angle the client said had never been a big winner became a second large winner within 21 days, cannibalising spend from his earlier ~$750k winner and projected to run ~$1M in spend. Single account, self-reported, no comparison against the client's earlier attempts. The production-stack half is weaker: an eleven-item enumerated list of production factors plus the claim that "you could write the best ad in the world and still see it bomb" if any one is wrong — assertion only, no test, no account data, no example of a specific factor being isolated.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable-principle class (attribution of a result to the right variable), so age is irrelevant. It is unusually load-bearing at this scale for the opposite reason to the source's: at ~54 orders/month, a sub-1,000-person list and $30/day, nothing here has ever been tested hard enough to be declared dead, so the standing risk is retiring an angle after one flat email subject line or one static plate. The interrogation questions are free, take minutes, and apply to the surfaces that actually carry history — Klaviyo campaign results and past PDP/Amazon copy — as well as to the new Meta account, where the $30/day giveaway campaign will produce flat creatives that one operator has to explain. Held at 6 because it prevents a bad retirement decision rather than producing revenue, and because at this volume the honest answer to "how heavily was it tested" will almost always be "not at all", which makes the checklist short. The production-stack list is worth 5 on its own: it substantially overlaps the nine-failure-point dependency argument behind the static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing, and the additive part is narrow — an explicit factor list to run down, plus the solo-operator collapse to image-plus-copy that makes the flat static the more trustworthy signal.

## Step 10 — Read an unusually high conversion rate as a narrow-funnel symptom, not a win: when you widen the top, expect the rate to fall while absolute orders rise, and optimise for total sales rather than the percentage.

**Stage:** scale — parked until the scale phase opens.

**Why it works:** A narrow funnel is a filter. Only prospects with the tenacity to self-qualify ever reach the offer, and very few people have that tenacity, so the ones who arrive convert at a flattering rate on a tiny base. Widening the lure admits more unqualified people *and* more qualified ones; the denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the percentage drops even as absolute sales climb. Reading the falling rate as damage is the failure mode — it leads to re-narrowing the funnel and shrinking the business to protect a vanity ratio. This is the same attribution discipline as the rest of this skill applied to a rate rather than an asset: name which variable moved (audience composition) before concluding anything about quality. It is also the one place where the largest-drop-off rule in Step 4 needs care — a step's rate can fall for compositional reasons while throughput rises.

**Evidence offered:** IT support company client anecdote — more leads, lower sales conversion rate, treated as the expected and correct outcome. Anecdote, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** The underlying principle is durable and transfers cleanly off the phone: an unusually high conversion rate on a narrow funnel means only self-qualified people are reaching it, and widening the top should be expected to lower rate while raising absolute orders — so optimise total sales, not the percentage. That is a real guard against a live failure mode once paid traffic starts diluting a currently self-selected audience. What is missing is a readable signal: at ~54 orders/month a conversion-rate movement is noise, so the diagnostic cannot be acted on yet. Trigger: enough monthly orders that a rate shift is distinguishable from variance.

## Step 11 — Vet every imported claim before you act on it: run any outside benchmark, rule of thumb or case study through an explicit written checklist — separate what was actually stated from what you are inferring, ask whether the speaker has an ulterior motive or hidden agenda, whether the evidence is sufficient, whether this is the common experience or an isolated occurrence, and which part of it you want to be true.

**Why it works:** Beliefs are acquired passively from whatever you read and watch, and humans draw conclusions automatically and unconsciously, so a single anecdote is silently upgraded into a general rule and a self-interested source is silently trusted. Motive, evidence sufficiency and representativeness are the three places a persuasive claim most often fails; wishful thinking is what stops you checking, so naming the thing you want to be true is what restores skepticism. Written questions work where intent does not, because the check has to survive the moment of enthusiasm that follows a good-sounding number.

The same three questions govern your own data. "Is this the common experience or an isolated occurrence?" is what keeps a two-order week from becoming a trend, what stops one soft month being treated as the decline Step 2 diagnoses, and it is the discipline behind the directional-only caveat attached to Steps 4, 6 and 7.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only — enumerated question lists (eight questions for misleading information, four for wishful thinking, four for jumping to conclusions) plus one illustrative example: wanting to believe others are "crushing it" at adwords arbitrage. No data, no worked case of the checklist catching a bad claim, and the document itself is a sales piece for the author's own courses.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (evidence evaluation), so the 2010 date is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, and it lands on a real recurring failure here: almost every tactic in this library rests on an unquantified practitioner assertion — "recovers up to 30% of abandoned carts", "50–60% of promotion sales land in the final hours", a 5% budget-scaling step, a $100/day ASC floor, a four-attempt testing cap — each produced by a creator with an ulterior motive from an account nothing like a $2,700/mo solo operation. The representativeness question is exactly what stops an imported benchmark being treated as a forecast at ~54 orders/month. Capped at 5 because it is a vetting habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it partially overlaps the existing discipline of running the arithmetic yourself rather than inheriting a rule of thumb, and the source offers no evidence the checklist works.

## Step 12 — Fix the evaluation horizon in writing before you start slow-feedback work: correct actions often produce no detectable signal for months, so state the review date and the continue-or-stop condition up front rather than deciding against impatience later.

**Why it works:** Most business actions give no immediate confirmation that a correct decision was correct. Combined with impatience for feedback, the absence of a visible result gets mistaken for the absence of a result, and programs that are in fact working get killed. Setting the horizon in advance moves the decision to a moment when you are not yet invested in seeing movement, so the later review is a check against a stated condition instead of a mood.

This is Step 9's discipline pointed forwards in time, as Step 8 is pointed forwards in volume: Step 9 stops you retiring an angle on one flat execution after the fact; Step 8 stops you running a test that could never have reported; the horizon stops you abandoning a flow, a review-accumulation push or a reorder cadence before it could possibly have reported.

**Evidence offered:** Analogies rather than business data: heart disease as 25.4% of US deaths, seat belt fatality statistics, and the joke about a man who swam 90% of the English Channel and turned back because he was tired. No account figures, no worked example of a business program abandoned prematurely.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (feedback timing), age irrelevant. Real translation here rather than pure encouragement: at ~54 orders/month and a sub-1,000 list, the highest-value work — post-purchase and transition-period flows, review accumulation, the subscribe-and-save cadence — produces no readable signal for months, and the standing risk for one operator is killing a flow in week three because nothing visibly moved. The usable form is to write the horizon and review date into the decision log (Step 6) when the work starts. Capped at 5 because it heavily overlaps restraint rules already recorded (learning-phase freeze, do-nothing and pre-committed degradation signals, and Step 9's don't-retire-an-angle-prematurely rule), and because as delivered it is closer to encouragement than mechanism; the additive part is only setting the horizon in advance for non-paid slow-feedback work, where no learning phase exists to anchor it.

## Step 13 — When a problem recurs, the deliverable is a written process, not another fix: after the Step 1 diagnosis has named the real constraint behind the recurrence, state the outcome you wanted and then complete the sentence "the solution is to install a system that…" — design that system as a checklist, and run installing it through the Step 5 queue like any other project.

**Why it works:** A one-off fix repairs the instance and leaves the process that produced it intact, so the same problem returns and keeps consuming attention indefinitely — the Whack-a-Mole failure in Step 1, but across time rather than across symptoms. Forcing the answer into the form "install a system that…" converts a repair into a documented process, and each documented process permanently removes a whole category of problem from the operator's plate, which is the only way a one-person operation frees capacity to grow. The trigger is the Step 6 decision log: scanning back over it is what makes a recurrence visible as a recurrence rather than as three unrelated bad weeks — and a lapsed process is the most common answer to Step 2's "what did we stop doing".

**Evidence offered:** An eight-step procedure worked through on a single hypothetical (hiring an incompetent employee → the real constraint is the absence of a hiring and training system), plus a ten-item sub-checklist (A–J) for designing the system. No business outcome data, no named case.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable process-design principle and free. Roughly half does not transfer — the steps on staffing, accountabilities, non-compliance enforcement and employee notification presuppose people who do not exist here, and the worked example is a hiring problem for a solo operator with no team. What transfers is the terminal instruction: when a marketing problem recurs, the output is a written process, not another fix. The real recurrences here are concrete — the deodorant SKU stocking out (which caps everything downstream and no marketing work repairs), a post-purchase flow silently breaking, listing copy drifting out of sync across 12 SKUs and two channels, review requests never going out. A checklist file costs an hour and prevents the recurrence. Capped at 5 because the source's first three steps (identify, reframe from person to system, probe) duplicate Step 1's diagnosis and its sequencing duplicates Step 5, so only the "install a system that…" terminal move is additive; the evidence is one hypothetical; and it protects hours rather than producing revenue.
