# Acquisition Channel Selection

## There are only four things one person can do to make people aware of what they sell — warm outreach, cold outreach, content, and paid ads — and until you are at seven figures you should run just one or two, ideally content plus warm outreach because they are the cheapest.

**Why it works:** Nobody can buy from you unless they know you exist, and the four channels exhaust the ways a single operator can create that awareness; concentrating on the cheapest one or two avoids spreading a solo operator's finite hours across eight half-run channels.

**Evidence offered:** His own sequence as a beginner: a year of warm outreach producing ~13 customers, then his first content post ('the free training project') publishing those results, then later paid ads and cold outbound. Anecdotal, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (channel taxonomy and focus discipline), so age is irrelevant. Its real value here is the focus rule, not the taxonomy: paid is explicitly gated behind Tracking → CRO → Offer/AOV, and retention — not traffic — is the binding constraint, so the correct read is that organic content plus warm outreach to people who already know the brand are the only two acquisition channels worth any hours at $2,700/mo. Capped at 5 because cold outbound has no honest form for a $12 consumer deodorant, warm outreach for a solo operator is a handful of people rather than 25 customers at $10k/yr, and the whole worked example is a high-ticket service model. It is a coverage-and-focus checklist, not a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

**Refinement — at the earliest level the unit of focus is narrower than a channel: one offer, to one avatar, on one platform, and you only add once that combination reliably produces engaged leads.** Engaged leads are the moment money starts; splitting a small operator's attention across offers, avatars and platforms means none of them ever gets enough input volume to produce a readable result.

**Evidence offered:** Personal roadmap anecdote (started with warm outreach to everyone he knew). No figures.

**Fit here (7/10):** Directly actionable now: 12 SKUs and a $30/day Meta campaign standing up, where the biggest failure mode is running several offers or audience ideas at once and never getting a readable signal. Applied here that means one offer (the soap giveaway), one avatar, Meta only, until engaged leads arrive at a readable rate. Extends the channel-count rule above from channels down to offer and avatar, so it sharpens rather than restates.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 16 of 16)*

**Extension — beyond doing the four activities yourself, recruit 'lead getters' who advertise on your behalf: customers who refer, employees, agencies, and affiliates. Audit which of the four you have and build the next one, expecting them roughly in that order.** The core four have a linear input-to-output relationship with your own time. Lead getters break that link: you spend time once to acquire the lead getter, and they generate engaged leads continuously without further time from you, so leads per hour worked rises. Each type has a distinct strength and the sequence follows a founder's growing capacity to manage people.

**Evidence offered:** A four-scenario thought experiment about work/leads/leverage, plus the author's claim that all four types were used to scale to $100M+. No per-type economics.

**Fit here (6/10):** The audit is runnable today with a spreadsheet, and two legs are live now: customer referrals (which sit directly on the binding constraint at 18–22.5% repeat) and affiliates/partners in natural body care — the same population the partnership sections below prospect. Employees and agencies are team-gated and should not be considered at $2,700/mo. Marked down because the enumeration is close to the channel taxonomy above, so it lands as an added claim rather than a new system.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 11 of 16)*

## Post your customer success stories into the online communities and groups where your buyers already gather, to generate leads from them.

**Why it works:** Communities aggregate people who already have the problem and are actively discussing it; a concrete result story reads as peer proof rather than advertising in that context, so it pulls inbound interest at zero media cost.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion inside the narrative: 'I'll post our success stories in a few gym groups to get leads from there' — one of three lead sources that produced $215,000 in profit in 30 days, not isolated or measured.

**Fit here (6/10):** This is the free content leg of the cheap channel pair above, which matters against $2,700/mo revenue and $900/mo of Meta budget. Natural deodorant, sensitive-skin and eczema communities on Facebook and Reddit are full of problem-aware buyers, and a solo operator can post one customer result story a week. Capped at 6 because self-promo rules make it fragile — one moderator can end the channel — and it is a slow trickle that does not move the retention constraint.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 1 of 16)*

## Forecast a channel before committing to it and reverse-engineer the required inputs: measure the conversion rate at each step (e.g. one in five engages, one in five of those takes the free offer, one in four of those buys), multiply by your own order value, then solve for how many reach-outs, emails or entries per day the revenue target demands.

**Why it works:** Outreach and funnel response are roughly linear at small volumes, so once the response ratio is known, output becomes a scheduling problem rather than a guessing problem — and the same arithmetic run forward tells you in advance whether the hours or spend are worth it.

**Evidence offered:** Stated benchmarks plus two worked examples: 500 reach-outs/week × a $400 offer = $104,000/year; 100 emails = 1 customer, therefore 10,000 emails = 100 customers = 333/day = three senders.

**Fit here (6/10):** The arithmetic is durable and executable today; the imported benchmark numbers are from a high-ticket service model and must be vetted against real numbers before acting on. Run against a $50.46 AOV rather than his $400, a hundred reach-outs returns roughly one order — which is the useful output: warm outreach here is for seeding and re-engagement, not revenue. It also converts a revenue target into required entries/emails per week and exposes when a target is arithmetically impossible at $30/day, which is the cheapest way to kill a channel before spending hours on it.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 4 of 16)*

## Commit to a fixed advertising input — a daily ad spend, posting cadence and ask cadence — and hold it unchanged and uninterrupted for a long window (his Rule of 100: 100 consecutive days) rather than running in bursts, then read the output at the end of the window.

**Why it works:** Volume of primary actions is the single input most under your control, and inputs are the only controllable variable: if cadence fluctuates, month-to-month output changes cannot be attributed to anything. Holding the input constant for long enough turns the growth numbers into trustworthy feedback.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion framed as the creator's one promise, plus his own practice of posting twice weekly for four years before charting, and the framing that 10→15 listeners is 50% monthly growth.

**Fit here (6/10):** Honest scaled-down version: keep the $30/day Meta campaign running continuously rather than pausing it whenever a week looks bad, and block a fixed daily slot (30–45 minutes, not 100) for ad and content production. Literal 100 minutes/day plus 100 daily reach-outs is not survivable for one person fulfilling ~54 orders/month. Partially overlaps the recorded evaluation-horizon step; the addition here is holding the input constant across that window rather than merely fixing a review date. Avoid the vanity-metric trap the source invites: the prime directive is revenue, not followers.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 6 of 16)*

## When you want more leads, exhaust 'more' and 'better' on the channel you already run before going 'new' — and when you do go new, follow the order: new audiences and placements on the platform you are already on, then every media type it supports, then another platform, then an entirely new acquisition activity.

**Why it works:** Each step outward costs more learning and setup than the last, so return per unit of work falls as you move right; the cheapest incremental leads come from surface area you have already paid to learn — the same account, pixel and creative library. Plateaus are usually mistaken saturation, not real saturation.

**Evidence offered:** Two anecdotes — a chiropractor-niche owner claiming saturation at $30k/mo on one platform in a $15.1B industry, and a weight-loss operator afraid of saturating a 1B-DAU platform at $40k/mo — plus his own roadmap of expanding paid ads to reach affiliates before adding channels.

**Fit here (7/10):** A stop-rule against the solo operator's real failure mode: adding TikTok, Pinterest and a newsletter while the one live Meta campaign is badly under-exercised at $30/day. Gives a concrete ordering — work Reels, Stories and feed placements and every format the account supports before opening any new platform. Overlaps but does not duplicate the recorded claim about not opening a new demographic, which governs audiences inside paid rather than the order of channel and placement expansion.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 10 of 16)*

## Only advertise on a platform where four things are true: you have used it and gotten value from it as a consumer, you can target people interested in your stuff on it, you know how to format ads for it, and you have the minimum money required to place an ad.

**Why it works:** Consumer familiarity gives an intuition for the format and feed; targetability and format knowledge make spend efficient rather than random; the budget floor is a hard gate on whether the platform's optimizer can work at all.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, framed as a principle that survives platform change. No figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Cheap to apply, but Meta is already picked and standing up at $30/day, so the checklist earns its keep only the next time TikTok, Pinterest or Amazon Ads is considered — which the expansion-order rule above says should be late anyway. The fourth criterion (budget minimum) is the useful one now: it is the honest test for whether a second platform should be opened at all on $900/mo, and on that test the answer today is no.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 8 of 16)*

## Before prospecting partners, derive the partner profile from your current binding constraint: name the resource that is capping output, then describe the company whose list, audience, product or capability would make that constraint irrelevant — and prospect against that profile before you need it.

**Why it works:** Another company already controls the resource you lack (customers, expertise, products, distribution, people), so borrowing it via partnership is faster than building it — but a partnership is only worth anything if it dissolves the specific thing capping output. Deriving the profile from the constraint first means the search targets a known gap instead of generating a pile of generic co-marketing conversations. And because good partners are hardest to find once you already need them, profiling and prospecting in advance is what makes the partnership available when the constraint bites.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with a three-way taxonomy of network members (people with answers, people with resources, people with specialized skills) and the observation that 'A-listers' know other A-listers, so a contact who cannot help will often introduce someone who can. Framed as belonging 'at the very top of every online entrepreneur's to-do list'. No partnership economics, no conversion figures, no named case.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (distribution partnerships), so the 2008 date is irrelevant, and it needs no ad budget, attribution or team — a solo operator can write the profile and send ten emails. It is the selection criterion the reciprocal-deal tactic below is missing: 'cold-email ten small non-competing brands' gives no way to choose which ten. Applied here the profile is drawn from retention, not traffic — a brand whose buyers churn at the same moment RSC's do, a list of sensitive-skin buyers, or a complementary routine SKU to bundle — rather than 'anyone in clean beauty'. Chains onto Step 1 of marketing-performance-pattern-analysis, which is where the binding constraint gets named. Capped at 5 because RSC brings almost nothing to a swap at a sub-1,000 list and ~54 orders/month, the outcome depends entirely on someone else agreeing, none of the referred revenue will be attributable, and the source's 'executives who control key resources' framing has no honest form at this size.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 4 of 6)*

## Strike a reciprocal deal with a non-competing business that already has your customers, so each of you routes buyers to the other (his example: a gym and a chiropractor trading referrals) — and when your list is too small to trade on parity, pay in another currency instead.

**Why it works:** Another business has already paid to acquire the exact people you want and has their trust; a reciprocal arrangement borrows that audience at no media cost, and because it is mutual the partner has a standing incentive to keep sending people.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as one of the four 'lead getters' with the gym/chiropractor example. Assertion only, no partnership economics or conversion figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (distribution partnership), age irrelevant, and it is one of the very few acquisition moves that needs no ad budget, no media buyer and no attribution — a solo operator can cold-email ten small non-competing brands (natural soap, sensitive-skin, zero-waste) and propose a co-marketing bundle or an insert swap. Choose those ten by the constraint-derived profile above, not by who is easy to find. Honest translation exists for a physical catalog, unlike the agency and employee variants in the same list. Held to 5 because RSC brings very little to a swap: a sub-1,000 subscriber list and ~54 orders/month make it a weak trading partner, the outcome depends entirely on someone else agreeing, and none of the referred revenue will be attributable at this volume.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

**Refinement — a small list does not disqualify you; a reciprocal swap is only one of several currencies.** The real constraint on partnering with a much larger list owner is usually that you have not spent time courting them, not that your list is small. Larger partners will trade access for attention, status, or content they can reuse: a friendship built at an industry event, or an interview that presents them flatteringly and that they can promote to their own audience. So a party with almost no list can still pay — in hours, in a flattering feature, in a genuine relationship — for exposure it could never buy with audience parity. 'My list is too small' is a self-imposed constraint standing in for the real one (no courting effort invested).

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with two described paths — beginners making friends with larger entrepreneurs at industry conferences, and asking to interview a list owner in a way that flatters them and helps them promote their own products. No named cases, no conversion or partnership figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (partnership and distribution), age irrelevant, and it answers the exact objection that caps the reciprocal-deal tactic above at 5 — that RSC brings very little to a swap. The answer is that the swap is the wrong frame: offer a feature or interview instead of parity. Honest solo form: interview or profile founders of adjacent non-competing small brands (natural soap, zero-waste, sensitive-skin) for the blog or email, give them something resharable, and let the relationship be the currency. Costs hours only. Capped at 5 because it is close kin to the tactic it refines, conference travel is real money against $2,700/mo, small clean-beauty founders receive this ask constantly, none of the traffic will be attributable at this volume, and it does nothing for retention.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 2 of 6)*

**Refinement — the strongest currency is not a feature or a commission but an upgrade to the partner's own offer: design the integration so your product makes their package more valuable and more differentiated than their competitor's.** A partner keeps promoting what improves their core business, not what pays a one-time cut; bundling your product into their membership or package raises their perceived value against the business down the street, so the arrangement survives without you pushing it.

**Evidence offered:** Chiropractor case study — including one to two adjustments with every gym membership raises the gym's membership value and signals the gym cares about member safety; plus the Prestige Labs nutrition-orientation example. No partnership economics for either.

**Fit here (7/10):** This business's constraint on partnerships is that a sub-1,000-list, $2,700/mo brand has nothing to trade on parity — this names a currency it does have: a physical product that makes a yoga studio's, salon's or spa's package look better (a deodorant or soap included with a membership, a class series, or a treatment). Distinct from the flattering-feature refinement above, which pays in attention and relationship; this one pays by upgrading the partner's own offer, which is the version that keeps promoting itself. Not a 10 because it costs real COGS per unit against thin margins, and the referred revenue stays unattributable at ~54 orders/month unless coded — see the partner-level 3:1 test below.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 15 of 16)*

## Judge an affiliate or partner by comparing what it cost you to land them against the gross profit of every customer they send, and require at least 3:1 — improving it by lowering the cost to land partners, adding a launch process so more of them activate, or deepening integration so each is worth more.

**Why it works:** Affiliates do not repay you directly, so the normal LTGP:CAC frame misattributes; measuring at the partner level tells you whether to recruit more, fix activation, or fix integration, rather than guessing which end is broken.

**Evidence offered:** Worked arithmetic: $4,000 to acquire an affiliate, $120,000 in sales over 12 months, 75% margins, 40% payout, $54,000 left, 12.5:1 ratio.

**Fit here (6/10):** Honest scaled-down version runnable today: give every partner a unique discount code, track gross profit per code against the product and hours spent landing them, and use the three levers to decide whether the problem is recruitment, activation or integration — where 'deepen integration' means the offer-upgrade refinement above. At ~54 orders/month one partner's code produces single-digit orders, so conclusions stay directional, which is what the pattern-analysis skill already prescribes at low volume. It is also the only thing that makes partnership hours accountable rather than open-ended.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, part 15 of 16)*
