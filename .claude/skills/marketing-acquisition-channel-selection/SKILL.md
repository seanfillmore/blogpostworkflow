# Acquisition Channel Selection

## There are only four things one person can do to make people aware of what they sell — warm outreach, cold outreach, content, and paid ads — and until you are at seven figures you should run just one or two, ideally content plus warm outreach because they are the cheapest.

**Why it works:** Nobody can buy from you unless they know you exist, and the four channels exhaust the ways a single operator can create that awareness; concentrating on the cheapest one or two avoids spreading a solo operator's finite hours across eight half-run channels.

**Evidence offered:** His own sequence as a beginner: a year of warm outreach producing ~13 customers, then his first content post ('the free training project') publishing those results, then later paid ads and cold outbound. Anecdotal, no figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (channel taxonomy and focus discipline), so age is irrelevant. Its real value here is the focus rule, not the taxonomy: paid is explicitly gated behind Tracking → CRO → Offer/AOV, and retention — not traffic — is the binding constraint, so the correct read is that organic content plus warm outreach to people who already know the brand are the only two acquisition channels worth any hours at $2,700/mo. Capped at 5 because cold outbound has no honest form for a $12 consumer deodorant, warm outreach for a solo operator is a handful of people rather than 25 customers at $10k/yr, and the whole worked example is a high-ticket service model. It is a coverage-and-focus checklist, not a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

**Refinement — locate yourself on the seven-level advertising roadmap and take only the action that level prescribes.** Level 1 is one offer to one avatar on one platform via warm outreach; level 2 is maxing personal capacity with warm outreach plus posted content; level 3 is hiring; level 4 is fixing the product until referrals are consistent; level 5 is more platforms and methods; level 6 is veteran executives. Each level has a different bottleneck, so acting like a level you are not at — hiring before you have a repeatable input, adding platforms before the first one is maxed — spends money on the wrong constraint. This is the same staging as the 'one or two channels until seven figures' rule, expressed as a diagnostic rather than a revenue threshold.

**Evidence offered:** The author's own path through each level and Acquisition.com's use of the roadmap across portfolio companies. No comparative data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Usable today as a diagnostic: at ~$2,700/mo combined and one operator, Real Skin Care sits at level 2–4 — personal-capacity content plus a live $30/day Meta campaign, with the level-4 referral/product work being the honest next step given repeat rate sits at 18–22.5%. That reading says explicitly: do not go to level 5 (more platforms) yet. Scored 6 because it is orientation, not execution detail, and the level-3 hiring rung is unreachable at this revenue.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 16 of 16))*

## Pick one advertising activity and commit to a fixed daily volume floor sustained for 100 consecutive days — 100 minutes a day making ads and 100 straight days of running them, or 100 reach-outs a day, or 100 minutes a day making content with at least one post released daily.

**Why it works:** Engaged-lead output is primarily a function of input volume, not cleverness; doubling inputs raises leads even with zero improvement in quality, and a 100-day horizon is long enough that the channel's real economics surface instead of being read off a two-week sample.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus a personal guarantee ('I don't make many promises, but this is one') and an unnamed community example. No figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle about input volume, and it supplies what the four-channels rule above leaves out — that rule says which channel, this says how much and for how long. The honest scaled-down version for one person at $30/day: hold the Meta campaign running for 100 consecutive days at the committed budget rather than killing it in week two, and book a fixed daily block for creative and email production instead of a literal 100 actions. The literal 100 warm reach-outs/day has no translation to a sub-1,000-list DTC catalog. Pairs with the step-conversion equation below, which turns the daily floor into an arithmetic quota rather than an arbitrary number.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 10 of 16))*

## Track every step-conversion of the acquisition process (contacts → opens/pick-ups → replies → engaged leads → sales), build the equation 'for every X contacted I get Y customers', then reverse a revenue target into the required input volume and divide it into a daily quota — gating the whole channel on lifetime profit per customer being at least 3x acquisition cost.

**Why it works:** Outreach and advertising behave like a conversion equation; once the input:output ratio is measured, output stops being a hope and becomes an arithmetic function of daily input volume, which also exposes when budget or headcount is the actual limiter. Without the ratios you cannot tell whether to do more, change the message, or stop.

**Evidence offered:** Worked per-channel examples (100 calls at 20% pickup × 25% take = 4 engaged leads; 30% open × 10% reply = 3% engaged), a portfolio company at 4% engagement and 30:1 returns, and the 100 emails → 1 customer → 10,000 emails → 100 customers → 333/day worked example.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable planning arithmetic; runnable on a spreadsheet with a hundred outreach attempts or a month of Meta data — these are counts, not significance tests. Directly usable now: at ~54 orders/month, $50.46 AOV and $30/day, the operator can state a target of e.g. 40 incremental orders and back out the entries, list adds and impressions required, which will immediately show whether $30/day can arithmetically produce it. The specific benchmark rates are B2B high-ticket and should be treated as directional. The 3x lifetime-profit-to-CAC gate is the honest kill criterion for the Meta campaign once the 100-day commitment above has run.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 7 of 16))*

## When a channel plateaus, exhaust volume first (more), then optimization (better), and only expand to 'new' when marginal returns from more/better drop below it — taking 'new' in the order new placement on a platform you already know, then a known placement on a new platform, then an entirely new acquisition activity.

**Why it works:** More is the cheapest lever because it requires no new skill; better compounds on volume you already have; new costs the most learning per lead, so it is ordered last and internally ordered by leads-per-unit-of-work. Skipping to 'new' spreads a solo operator across platforms where they have no competence and no data.

**Evidence offered:** Illustrative story of a $2M/yr chiropractor-niche business spending $30k/mo on one platform claiming market saturation, contrasted with a peer spending $30k across four platforms in a week. Anecdotal.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable sequencing principle, directly relevant now: with one live Meta campaign at $30/day, the temptation to add TikTok or Pinterest before Meta has been run harder or improved is the specific error this prevents, and the placement-before-platform ordering is not in any other claim here. Partially overlaps 'don't open a new market until growth gets hard', hence not higher. Read it together with the roadmap refinement above — 'more platforms' is a level-5 action and this business is not at level 5.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 10 of 16))*

## Screen an advertising platform against four tests before spending: you have used it as a consumer, you can target your buyer inside it, you know how to format ads for it, and you can afford its minimum spend.

**Why it works:** Each test removes a specific failure mode — unfamiliarity with the medium, no way to narrow the audience, format mismatch, and being priced out before you can gather signal — so the platform choice itself does the first round of audience filtering.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, framed as the author's personal checklist. No data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable platform-selection logic. Value is limited because Meta is already chosen and the account is being stood up — it mostly confirms an existing decision. It stays useful as the gate to apply before anyone suggests TikTok, Pinterest or Amazon Sponsored Brands as a second channel on a $900/mo total budget: the affordability test alone disqualifies most of them, and the more/better/new ordering above says the question should not even be reached yet.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 8 of 16))*

## Post your customers' success stories into the online groups and communities where your buyers already congregate, as a standing lead source alongside ads.

**Why it works:** Proof posted inside a community the buyer already trusts borrows that community's credibility and reaches people who would never see an ad; it costs nothing but time, so it runs in parallel with paid without competing for budget.

**Evidence offered:** Hormozi: 'I'll post our success stories in a few gym groups to get leads from there' — one of the three lead sources credited with the $215,000 profit month. Anecdotal, no per-channel attribution.

**Fit here (6/10):** Honest translation exists: natural-deodorant switchers, low-tox and zero-waste communities on Reddit and Facebook are real and reachable by a solo operator at zero cost, which matters against $2,700/mo revenue and a $30/day ad budget. Durable principle, not platform mechanics. Capped at 6 because most such groups ban overt self-promotion, so the tactic has to be run as answering switch-related questions with a customer result rather than posting promos, and it is slow relative to the giveaway campaign already standing up. It is the content half of the 'content plus warm outreach' pair, aimed at places rather than a feed.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 1 of 16))*

## Beyond the four things you can do yourself, four categories of people can advertise on your behalf — customers/referrers, employees, agencies and affiliates — and they arrive in a natural order, referrals first and affiliates last; you acquire each of them using the same core four channels.

**Why it works:** Other people running outreach, content or ads for you decouples lead volume from your own hours, so output stops being capped by the solo operator's time — but each type also demands a management capability you only acquire in sequence, so attempting affiliates before you can manage people tends to fail.

**Evidence offered:** Author's claim that $30M of customers said someone referred them, plus his own sequence: referrals, team, agencies, then a $1.5M/mo affiliate program. Anecdotal.

**Fit here (6/10):** Two of the four (customers, affiliates) are genuinely available to a solo operator at 54 orders/month; employees and agencies are not. So the live content reduces to a real sequencing instruction: work referrals from existing buyers before building any partner or affiliate program — which is also consistent with retention, not traffic, being the binding constraint, since the same buyers you would ask to refer are the ones you need to bring back. Partially overlaps the partnership claims below, which is why it sits here as the ordering rule rather than as a second set of partner tactics.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 11 of 16))*

## Before prospecting partners, derive the partner profile from your current binding constraint: name the resource that is capping output, then describe the company whose list, audience, product or capability would make that constraint irrelevant — and prospect against that profile before you need it.

**Why it works:** Another company already controls the resource you lack (customers, expertise, products, distribution, people), so borrowing it via partnership is faster than building it — but a partnership is only worth anything if it dissolves the specific thing capping output. Deriving the profile from the constraint first means the search targets a known gap instead of generating a pile of generic co-marketing conversations. And because good partners are hardest to find once you already need them, profiling and prospecting in advance is what makes the partnership available when the constraint bites.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with a three-way taxonomy of network members (people with answers, people with resources, people with specialized skills) and the observation that 'A-listers' know other A-listers, so a contact who cannot help will often introduce someone who can. Framed as belonging 'at the very top of every online entrepreneur's to-do list'. No partnership economics, no conversion figures, no named case.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (distribution partnerships), so the 2008 date is irrelevant, and it needs no ad budget, attribution or team — a solo operator can write the profile and send ten emails. It is one half of the selection criterion the reciprocal-deal tactic below is missing: 'cold-email ten small non-competing brands' gives no way to choose which ten. Applied here the profile is drawn from retention, not traffic — a brand whose buyers churn at the same moment RSC's do, a list of sensitive-skin buyers, or a complementary routine SKU to bundle — rather than 'anyone in clean beauty'. Chains onto Step 1 of marketing-performance-pattern-analysis, which is where the binding constraint gets named. Capped at 5 because RSC brings almost nothing to a swap at a sub-1,000 list and ~54 orders/month, the outcome depends entirely on someone else agreeing, none of the referred revenue will be attributable, and the source's 'executives who control key resources' framing has no honest form at this size.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 4 of 6)*

## Build the partner prospect list by asking 'who's got my leads?' — what do my best customers buy, where do they go, what do they like to do, who do they work for — then group the answers into categories (products, equipment, services, groups they belong to, events they attend) and prioritise any business that falls into more than one category.

**Why it works:** Prospecting from your customers' actual behaviour surfaces businesses whose warm audience is already made of your buyers, so the list is pre-qualified before you pitch; multi-category overlap signals unusually dense concentration of your leads.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus the ALAN example — a 200-item list of products and services agency owners buy, which sorted into six repeatable categories he now reuses for every new hit list.

**Fit here (7/10):** A free, one-afternoon research exercise a solo operator can do today with a spreadsheet and a few calls or emails to existing customers. It is the other half of the partner selection criterion: the constraint-derived profile above starts from what is capping output, this one starts from where the customers physically are, and the ten brands worth emailing are the ones that satisfy both. The category list doubles as the target list for the community-posting tactic above — the groups customers belong to are the groups to answer questions in.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 14 of 16))*

## Strike a reciprocal deal with a non-competing business that already has your customers, so each of you routes buyers to the other (his example: a gym and a chiropractor trading referrals) — and when your list is too small to trade on parity, pay in another currency instead.

**Why it works:** Another business has already paid to acquire the exact people you want and has their trust; a reciprocal arrangement borrows that audience at no media cost, and because it is mutual the partner has a standing incentive to keep sending people.

**Evidence offered:** Stated as one of the four 'lead getters' with the gym/chiropractor example. Assertion only, no partnership economics or conversion figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (distribution partnership), age irrelevant, and it is one of the very few acquisition moves that needs no ad budget, no media buyer and no attribution — a solo operator can cold-email ten small non-competing brands (natural soap, sensitive-skin, zero-waste) and propose a co-marketing bundle or an insert swap. Choose those ten by the constraint-derived profile and the 'who's got my leads?' categories above, not by who is easy to find, and run buyer referrals before this per the four-categories ordering. Honest translation exists for a physical catalog, unlike the agency and employee variants in the same list. Held to 5 because RSC brings very little to a swap: a sub-1,000 subscriber list and ~54 orders/month make it a weak trading partner, the outcome depends entirely on someone else agreeing, and none of the referred revenue will be attributable at this volume.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "If I Wanted To Become a Millionaire in 2025, This Is What I'd Do [FULL BLUEPRINT]" (AN2KpRBsmRY)*

**Refinement — a small list does not disqualify you; a reciprocal swap is only one of several currencies.** The real constraint on partnering with a much larger list owner is usually that you have not spent time courting them, not that your list is small. Larger partners will trade access for attention, status, or content they can reuse: a friendship built at an industry event, or an interview that presents them flatteringly and that they can promote to their own audience. So a party with almost no list can still pay — in hours, in a flattering feature, in a genuine relationship — for exposure it could never buy with audience parity. 'My list is too small' is a self-imposed constraint standing in for the real one (no courting effort invested).

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with two described paths — beginners making friends with larger entrepreneurs at industry conferences, and asking to interview a list owner in a way that flatters them and helps them promote their own products. No named cases, no conversion or partnership figures.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (partnership and distribution), age irrelevant, and it answers the exact objection that caps the reciprocal-deal tactic above at 5 — that RSC brings very little to a swap. The answer is that the swap is the wrong frame: offer a feature or interview instead of parity. Honest solo form: interview or profile founders of adjacent non-competing small brands (natural soap, zero-waste, sensitive-skin) for the blog or email, give them something resharable, and let the relationship be the currency. Costs hours only. Capped at 5 because it is close kin to the tactic it refines, conference travel is real money against $2,700/mo, small clean-beauty founders receive this ask constantly, none of the traffic will be attributable at this volume, and it does nothing for retention.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 2 of 6)*

## Run the daily advertising action yourself only until it earns enough to pay someone else to do it — then make hiring that person the new lead-getting goal and repeat the same process.

**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Personal work capacity, not the platform, is what caps output first; buying back the repetitive advertising input with earned revenue is the only way to raise volume without a new channel, and recruiting is itself a lead-getting problem you already know how to solve.

**Evidence offered:** Author's own hiring sequence at level 3 — his first hires were a videographer and a media buyer. Anecdotal.

**Fit here (5/10):** Blocked purely by people and money — a videographer, editor or media buyer is the team gate, and $2,700/mo revenue with $900/mo committed to Meta cannot fund a hire. Trigger: the Meta campaign plus retention work producing enough contribution margin that a repeated weekly task can be paid for rather than done at 11pm. It is the level-3 rung of the roadmap refinement under the four-channels claim above, and it should not be attempted before the level-4 referral and product work. Scored on the merit it will have then: a real but generic scaling move.

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "$100M Leads" (book, (part 16 of 16))*
