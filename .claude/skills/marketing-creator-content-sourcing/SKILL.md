# Creator Content Sourcing

## Activate creator content through Meta partnership ads because the delivery starts with the creator's own seed audience and expands from there, reaching net-new people your pixel keeps missing.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens. Recorded now so it is not re-derived later.

**Why it works:** A partnership ad is served first to the creator's engaged followers and then to lookalikes of them. That lets it escape the audience rut an account develops after years of optimizing against its own pixel. Meta's persona-based Andromeda targeting also rewards content that maps to new personas.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus secondhand claims. A performance summit statement said creator content is the future of selling. Named brand interviews: Pacted and Caraway said small creators/UGC performed best on partnership ads, while Jones Road said bigger influencer and celebrity deals performed better for them. No spend, CPA or incrementality figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Platform-mechanics class, published fresh (2026-08), so it is current. The traffic gate it was parked behind is open, so the "long park on a fast-decaying claim" reasoning no longer applies, which is what raises it from 4. Two of the three things it needed now exist: an ad budget and a live Meta account. The remaining one is a creator, and the giveaway is itself the cheapest way to get one. Entrants are self-selected brand-interested people, and paying one in product for a usable clip costs COGS rather than cash (see the customer-as-creator tactic below). Held at 6 rather than higher for two reasons. A partnership ad requires the creator to grant permission on their own handle, a coordination step a solo operator has not done before. And the delivery-behaviour claims are unverified here. Partnership-ad attribution is also what makes per-creator commission trackable under the hybrid pay structure below.

## Judge whether a person can carry a video within the first 2-3 seconds of a clip, on delivery rather than looks: energy and vocal variety (speeding up and slowing down), eye contact and magnetism, opening mid-conversation rather than on product features — and rule out anyone whose cadence reveals they are reading a script.

**Why it works:** Delivery is what holds attention. A monotone script-read registers as an ad and gets skipped, while vocal variety and direct eye contact pull the viewer in regardless of production quality. Leading with product features before establishing any story loses the viewer before the claim lands.

**Evidence offered:** Six teardowns of real clips, two of them her own:
- a gummy creator praised for vocal variety and a 'girl talk' vibe
- Emily D'Angelo, praised for eye contact and magnetism
- two 'good but not great' clips flagged for scripted intonation
- an apparel clip criticized for cutting straight to product features
- a side-by-side of her own flat and high-energy videos

Practitioner judgment, no view or conversion data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (on-camera delivery), so age is irrelevant. Executable today and free. The only realistic on-camera source here is repeat customers or the founder, and this gives concrete accept/reject criteria for a video before it goes onto a Shopify PDP or an Amazon listing video slot. The Amazon slot is on the larger ~$1,800/mo channel. Capped at 5 because it is a selection filter, not a revenue mechanism. The 'reject anyone who reads a script' bar is also harsh when your candidate pool is a handful of willing customers rather than a paid roster, so the practical version is to prompt for conversational answers rather than reject the person. The same person also varies session to session. A flat take is evidence about the take, not necessarily about the person, so re-shoot before writing someone off. This filter screens the performer; the technical-defect pass below screens the finished file, and a clip has to survive both.

## Use your own customers as creators — they will often do it in exchange for product alone, and the result is either your best-performing ad or a flop, because VIP customers frequently cannot show up on camera.

**Why it works:** A real customer carries credibility no hired creator can fake. The only cost is product, and the only risk is delivery quality, which you can screen before using the footage.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion, with an explicit caveat that she is nervous recommending it because outcomes are bimodal. She reports seeing it work and seeing customers accept product as the only compensation. No figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class, and the one item in this video with a genuine solo-operator translation. Repeat customers are 45-52% of revenue. At ~54 orders/month the operator can personally identify and email the handful of enthusiastic repeat buyers. This is the same top-customer cultivation already in marketing-retention-offers, extended to asking for a short video. Compensation is a stick of deodorant, not a fee, so it clears the budget constraint entirely. The output has real homes: the Shopify PDP, the Amazon listing video slot on the larger ~$1,800/mo channel, and post-purchase email. Held at 6 for three reasons:
- delivery quality is a coin flip
- the video still has to be edited by a solo operator with no designer
- there are hard compliance limits: product-for-content is a material connection that must be disclosed under FTC rules, and it must never be routed into an Amazon review, which is a TOS violation

## Scale brief depth inversely to the creator's experience: give UGC and small creators a fully prescriptive storyboard with the script written out line by line; give established influencers only campaign context plus content pillars, shooting guidelines and explicit dos and don'ts so they write and send back their own script for approval; and with celebrities and high-caliber talent write the script yourself close to verbatim.

**Why it works:** Inexperienced creators do not know what converts, so leaving the script open produces weak content. Experienced creators know their own audience's voice better than you do, so guardrails constrain the strategy without flattening the native delivery that makes UGC read as authentic. With celebrity talent the brand needs to control message and hook while borrowing the face, and talent will follow a well-written script closely.

**Evidence offered:**
- A teardown of her agency's own brief for a top-performing video: a full clip-by-clip storyboard.
- The contrasting briefs she personally receives as an influencer: campaign context only, and she returns the POV and script.
- A Jones Road Beauty brief with content pillars, shooting guidelines and dos/don'ts but no script.
- Her report that she scripted celebrity ads verbatim at a nine-figure beauty brand and most followed them 'to a T'.

No test or performance data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (briefing), so age is irrelevant. It resolves directly into the customer-as-creator path adopted above. A repeat customer filming a testimonial is the least experienced case on the scale, so the brief must be fully prescriptive: the exact question to answer, the sentences to open on, the length. Free and executable today. Capped at 5 for two reasons. Only the fully prescriptive end of the scale will ever apply here, because the influencer-guardrail and celebrity-verbatim ends presuppose paid talent that is unreachable at ~$2,700/mo. And it is a production-quality lever rather than a revenue mechanism.

## Build every brief from the same fixed set of components: a description of the video type wanted, the product linked and already shipped, a reference ad, the setting and wardrobe/style guidelines, a storyboard table of clips with the script and the visual for each, upload instructions, and recording guidelines.

**Why it works:** A fixed component list removes the decisions the creator would otherwise make badly. It also eliminates the round trips caused by missing information. The reference ad in particular communicates the target faster than a description does.

**Evidence offered:** A section-by-section walkthrough of a real brief her agency used to produce a recent top-performing video. Single example, no comparison against a looser brief.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (asset briefing) and free. It is the concrete artifact that makes the customer-testimonial path produce usable footage instead of a shaky one-take. The reference-example component is the highest-value part. It echoes the exemplar rule in marketing-email-design-production and the reference-photo rule in marketing-ai-product-imagery, so it transfers with a known mechanism. For a customer filming on a phone, the recording-guidelines component is where the stability rules go: phone braced or propped, auto-zoom off, no digital zoom, one continuous take. Those are the defects that get a finished clip rejected in the QA pass below. Held at 5 because the wardrobe/setting and camera-spec sections are agency overkill for a customer filming on a phone, and this is a production checklist, not a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Watch every creative asset scrupulously yourself before it ships and reject it for technical execution defects — shaky camera, constant auto-zoom, anything that makes the viewer physically uncomfortable — not just for message quality.

**Why it works:** A strong script is delivered through an execution layer with many independent failure points, and a defect in that layer destroys the ad regardless of how good the concept is. Motion defects in particular make the viewer look away or feel nauseous, so the message is never received at all. Naming the specific defect, rather than a vague 'something's off', is what makes it fixable.

**Evidence offered:** One anecdote. A video from an expensive, well-credentialed creator with good on-camera delivery was unwatchable because of shaky footage and an auto-zoom cycling every couple of seconds. The author asserts the ad would have been 'a total flop' had it shipped. No test, no spend figures, no before/after.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (asset QA), so age is irrelevant. The solo operator here is both maker and reviewer, so the honest translation is that your own discomfort watching a clip is reason enough to reject it, and the defect gets named specifically before a re-shoot. It has real homes: the only video sources available are a repeat-customer testimonial clip for a Shopify PDP or Amazon video slot, or a clip on the live $30/day Meta campaign. It also adds a concrete line to the fully prescriptive brief the customer-as-creator path already requires: 'phone braced or propped, auto-zoom off, no digital zoom, one continuous take'. Free and editorial.

Capped at 5 for four reasons:
- it is a checklist item rather than a revenue mechanism
- the evidence is a single anecdote
- video is deliberately rare here under the static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing
- marketing-ai-product-imagery already owns the equivalent QA pass for static frames

The additive part is narrow: motion and stability defects, plus the rule that a physically uncomfortable asset is rejected even when the delivery and the script are both good. A deliberately raw, ungraded phone look is not a defect. The defects are motion and framing instability, not low production polish.

## Write a UGC video script so that every single sentence performs one deliberate belief-shifting job in sequence — personal credibility, specific pain, a second use case, relatability, regret/hindsight, product reveal, simplicity, mechanism, objection handling, authority citation, competitive differentiation, mechanism justification, desired outcome, scarcity transition, demand signal, availability, FOMO, CTA.
**Why it works:** Each line moves the viewer one small step, so the buy decision arrives as the last in a chain of increasingly obvious realizations rather than as a pitch the viewer has to accept in one jump. Nothing in the script is decorative, so there is no dead air to scroll away during.

**Evidence offered:** One annotated competitor UGC ad the creator says he studied. Assertion only beyond that single teardown.

**Fit here (7/10):** Natural deodorant is exactly the category where these beats map cleanly: here is the mechanism, here is why it beats the aluminum stick, here is the objection about the transition period. Durable-principle class (script/copy structure), so its Aug-2026 age is irrelevant and its longevity is not in doubt. Parked at team because executing it means an on-camera person and an editor: the deliverable is a shot video, not a document. Trigger: when a customer-creator or an editor is available to shoot, hand them this beat sheet as the storyboard script. It is the content of the storyboard-table component in the fixed brief list above, at the fully prescriptive end of the brief-depth ladder. It does not duplicate the seven-beat body sequence in copy-hooks-and-formats: that one stops at the mechanism, while this one carries through proof, differentiation, scarcity and close. There is a tension with the sub-25-second format spec below. At that length only a subset of these beats fits, so pick the ones the specific objection needs rather than trying to land all eighteen.

## Let the ad's job dictate the format, and when the job is earning attention with a testimonial, that means a phone-shot vertical UGC video under 25 seconds with no colour grade.
**Why it works:** Format is a delivery decision downstream of the job, not a starting preference. Name what the ad has to accomplish first, then pick the cheapest form that accomplishes it. When the job is earning attention from a cold audience, a raw, ungraded, vertically shot phone video reads as a real person's account rather than as advertising, so polish actively works against the job.

**Evidence offered:** Demonstrated in one worked example. The builder committed to video only after the job was set, named UGC testimonial as the form, and stated explicitly what a static could have done instead. All three executions specified were under 25 seconds, vertical and phone-shot. No performance comparison against a graded or longer cut.

**Fit here (6/10):** Sound, but the asset is a customer on camera, and UGC production plus a creator to deliver it are exactly what the team gate parks. The static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing stands for the solo operator precisely because a static has two failure points one person controls. Trigger: a willing repeat customer or paid creator who can carry a clip. At that point the job-to-format rule and the 25-second/vertical/no-grade spec become the brief. They belong in the recording-guidelines and video-type components of the fixed brief component list above, and they set the runtime budget the beat sheet above has to fit inside. The 'no colour grade' instruction also protects the solo operator from the one edit step they have no tooling for. Scored on the merit it will have then.

## When other people on a review chain have already approved an asset, do not treat their sign-off as agreement — say the defect out loud anyway, because nobody cares about the ad's success as much as you do.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Reviewers suppress their own perception once someone else has signalled approval, so a defect that everyone individually noticed passes unchallenged. One person naming it releases the rest of the group to confirm it. Deference is also misplaced: the editors and vendors most brands use are cheap, fast and have no direct-response training, so the owner's judgment is usually the better one.

**Evidence offered:** The same single anecdote. A head of growth, a second marketing exec and the video editor had all said 'looks good', and all three separately admitted the same nausea once it was flagged. Plus an email exchange with a reader about imposter syndrome. Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (review discipline / pluralistic ignorance), so age is irrelevant, and the mechanism is real and non-obvious. Parked under `team` because it needs at least one other person in the approval chain, which does not exist today. There is no designer, editor, agency or media buyer, so there is nobody to defer to and nobody to speak up against. Trigger: the first time an outside editor, designer or agency produces an asset that Real Skin Care ships. Scored 5 on the merit it will have then. It is a management and review habit rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is one anecdote, and it substantially overlaps the personal-review rule above. The additive part is only the instruction not to read a collaborator's approval as agreement.

## Classify creators as UGC creator, creator, influencer, or celebrity based on how they cultivate community — readable in their comments — not on micro/nano/macro follower tiers.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Follower count no longer predicts reach under persona-based targeting. The type of community a person has built does predict whether their audience will respond to a brand message. The comment section is the readable signal: emphatic, personal replies mean the audience treats the account as a voice they trust rather than a feed they scroll.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from ten years of practice plus definitional examples. A 50K-follower account hitting 100K views with emphatic comments is an influencer. Love Island alumni are celebrities because their notoriety originated off-platform. No performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** A screening heuristic, not a spend commitment: judge a prospective creator by the kind of community readable in their comments rather than by follower tier. Durable-principle class (how audiences respond to endorsement), not fast-decaying platform mechanics. It plugs straight into the sourcing/screening step of this skill. It is also the axis the brief-depth ladder above is indexed on, so classifying correctly tells you how prescriptive the brief has to be. What is missing is the creator relationship itself: paying or partnering with someone who is not the solo operator. Trigger: the first time there is budget or product-seeding capacity to engage an outside creator at all. The original rejection leaned on 'no creator budget and no media buyer', which is a people gate, not a merit problem.

## Track which specific video editors and which specific creators produced past winners, and route new scripts to those named people rather than rotating through whoever is available.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Execution quality is not evenly distributed across vendors. The same script routed through a strong editor or a strong on-camera creator produces a materially different asset. The person doing the execution is a variable you can select rather than a constant, so tracking who produced past winners and re-assigning them raises the hit rate without changing the script at all.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only, stated as advice to agency-side strategists ('fight to have those video editors be the ones'). No hit-rate comparison between vendors and no named example.

**Fit here (5/10):** Needs editors, creators or vendors who are not the solo operator. Today there is no editor, designer, creator roster or agency, so there is nobody to select between. Durable-principle class (vendor selection), so age is irrelevant. Trigger: the first time Real Skin Care has used more than one outside editor or more than one customer-creator. At that point, keep a written record of which person produced which result and re-brief the proven one rather than rotating. Scored 5 on the merit it will have then, not on today's inability to run it. It is a selection heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism and the evidence is pure assertion. It also sits close to the within-person-variance argument this skill already carries (a single flat take is evidence about the take, not the person). The additive part is only the instruction to track vendor-level outcomes and deliberately re-assign the winners. ## Stop doing one-off 'churn and burn' creator deals: work with multiple creators simultaneously and move to long-term partnerships — creators on monthly retainer, given internal email addresses, titles, and standing deliverables.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** On-camera performance varies within the same person. A roster averages out that variance, and a longer relationship lets you capture a creator's best work rather than one bad session. A creator embedded in the brand over time also produces more knowledgeable content and represents the brand publicly, compounding trust in a way a single sponsored post cannot.

**Evidence offered:** A side-by-side of two of her own videos, one flat and one high-energy, as proof that the same creator varies. Plus named examples: her own Motion chief-evangelist arrangement, Caraway's retained creator roster, and Hype 10's full-time creators.
