# Creator Content Sourcing

## Activate creator content through Meta partnership ads because the delivery starts with the creator's own seed audience and expands from there, reaching net-new people your pixel keeps missing.
**Stage:** traffic — gate OPEN as of 2026-08-17. Live; no longer parked.

**Why it works:** A partnership ad is served first to the creator's engaged followers and then to lookalikes of them, so it escapes the audience rut an account develops after years of optimizing against its own pixel — and Meta's persona-based Andromeda targeting rewards content that maps to new personas.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion plus secondhand claims: a performance summit statement that creator content is the future of selling, and named brand interviews (Pacted, Caraway said small creators/UGC performed best on partnership ads; Jones Road said bigger influencer and celebrity deals performed better for them). No spend, CPA, or incrementality figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Platform-mechanics class published fresh (2026-08), so it is current, and the traffic gate it was parked behind is open — the "long park on a fast-decaying claim" reasoning no longer applies, which is what raises it from 4. Two of the three things it needed now exist: an ad budget and a live Meta account. The remaining one is a creator, and the giveaway is itself the cheapest way to solve that — entrants are self-selected brand-interested people, and paying one in product for a usable clip costs COGS rather than cash (see the customer-as-creator tactic below). Held at 6 rather than higher because a partnership ad requires the creator to grant permission on their own handle, which is a real coordination step a solo operator has not done before, and because the delivery-behaviour claims are unverified here.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Judge whether a person can carry a video within the first 2-3 seconds of a clip, on delivery rather than looks: energy and vocal variety (speeding up and slowing down), eye contact and magnetism, opening mid-conversation rather than on product features — and rule out anyone whose cadence reveals they are reading a script.

**Why it works:** Delivery is what holds attention; a monotone script-read registers as an ad and gets skipped, while vocal variety and direct eye contact pull the viewer in regardless of production quality. Leading with product features before establishing any story loses the viewer before the claim lands.

**Evidence offered:** Six teardowns of real clips, including two of her own — a gummy creator praised for vocal variety and 'girl talk' vibe, Emily D'Angelo for eye contact and magnetism, two 'good but not great' clips flagged for scripted intonation, one apparel clip criticized for cutting straight to product features, and a side-by-side of her own flat versus high-energy videos. Practitioner judgment, no view or conversion data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (on-camera delivery), age irrelevant. Executable today and free: the only realistic on-camera source here is repeat customers or the founder, and this gives concrete accept/reject criteria for a video before it goes onto a Shopify PDP or an Amazon listing video slot — the surface that carries the larger ~$1,800/mo channel. Capped at 5 because it is a selection filter, not a revenue mechanism, and the 'reject anyone who reads a script' bar is harsh when your candidate pool is a handful of willing customers rather than a paid roster; the practical version is to prompt for conversational answers rather than to reject the person. Note also that the same person varies session to session — a flat take is evidence about the take, not necessarily about the person, so re-shoot before writing someone off. This filter screens the performer; the technical-defect pass below screens the finished file, and a clip has to survive both.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Use your own customers as creators — they will often do it in exchange for product alone, and the result is either your best-performing ad or a flop, because VIP customers frequently cannot show up on camera.

**Why it works:** A real customer carries credibility no hired creator can fake; the cost is only product, and the risk is purely delivery quality, which you can screen for before using the footage.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion with an explicit caveat that she is nervous recommending it because outcomes are bimodal. She reports having seen it work and having seen customers accept product as the only compensation. No figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** Durable principle class, and the one item in this video with a genuine solo-operator translation. Repeat customers are 45-52% of revenue and at ~54 orders/month the operator can personally identify and email the handful of enthusiastic repeat buyers — the same top-customer cultivation already in marketing-retention-offers, extended to asking for a short video. Compensation is a stick of deodorant, not a fee, so it clears the budget constraint entirely, and the output has real homes: the Shopify PDP, the Amazon listing video slot on the larger ~$1,800/mo channel, and post-purchase email. Held at 6 because delivery quality is a coin flip, the video still has to be edited by a solo operator with no designer, and there are hard compliance limits: product-for-content is a material connection that must be disclosed under FTC rules, and it must never be routed into an Amazon review, which is a TOS violation.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Scale brief depth inversely to the creator's experience: give UGC and small creators a fully prescriptive storyboard with the script written out line by line; give established influencers only campaign context plus content pillars, shooting guidelines and explicit dos and don'ts so they write and send back their own script for approval; and with celebrities and high-caliber talent write the script yourself close to verbatim.

**Why it works:** Inexperienced creators do not know what converts, so leaving the script open produces weak content; experienced creators know their own audience's voice better than you do, so guardrails constrain the strategy without flattening the native delivery that makes UGC read as authentic; with celebrity talent the brand needs to control message and hook while borrowing the face, and talent will follow a well-written script closely.

**Evidence offered:** Teardown of her agency's own brief for a top-performing video (full clip-by-clip storyboard) contrasted with the briefs she personally receives as an influencer (campaign context only, she returns POV and script), plus a Jones Road Beauty brief with content pillars, shooting guidelines and dos/don'ts but no script. Notes she personally scripted celebrity ads verbatim at a nine-figure beauty brand and most followed them 'to a T'. No test or performance data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (briefing), age irrelevant, and it resolves directly into the customer-as-creator path adopted above: a repeat customer filming a testimonial is the least experienced case on the scale, so the brief must be fully prescriptive — the exact question to answer, the sentences to open on, the length. Free and executable today. Capped at 5 because only the fully-prescriptive end of the scale will ever apply here — the influencer-guardrail and celebrity-verbatim ends presuppose paid talent that is unreachable at ~$2,700/mo — and it is a production-quality lever rather than a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Build every brief from the same fixed set of components: a description of the video type wanted, the product linked and already shipped, a reference ad, the setting and wardrobe/style guidelines, a storyboard table of clips with the script and the visual for each, upload instructions, and recording guidelines.

**Why it works:** A fixed component list removes the decisions the creator would otherwise make badly and eliminates the round trips caused by missing information — the reference ad in particular communicates the target faster than description does.

**Evidence offered:** Walkthrough of a real brief her agency used to produce a recent top-performing video, section by section. Single example, no comparison against a looser brief.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable principle class (asset briefing), free, and it is the concrete artifact that makes the customer-testimonial path produce usable footage instead of a shaky one-take. The reference-example component is the highest-value part and echoes the exemplar rule already in marketing-email-design-production and the reference-photo rule in marketing-ai-product-imagery, so it transfers with a known mechanism. For a customer filming on a phone, the recording-guidelines component is where the stability rules go — phone braced or propped, auto-zoom off, no digital zoom, one continuous take — because those are the defects that get a finished clip rejected in the QA pass below. Held at 5 because the wardrobe/setting and camera-spec sections are agency overkill for a customer filming on a phone, and this is a production checklist, not a revenue mechanism.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Watch every creative asset scrupulously yourself before it ships and reject it for technical execution defects — shaky camera, constant auto-zoom, anything that makes the viewer physically uncomfortable — not just for message quality.

**Why it works:** A strong script is delivered through an execution layer with many independent failure points, and a defect in that layer destroys the ad regardless of how good the concept is. Motion defects in particular make the viewer look away or feel nauseous, so the message is never received at all. Naming the specific defect (rather than a vague 'something's off') is what makes it fixable.

**Evidence offered:** One anecdote: a video from an expensive, well-credentialed creator with good on-camera delivery was nonetheless unwatchable because of shaky footage and an auto-zoom cycling every couple of seconds; the author asserts the ad would have been 'a total flop' had it shipped. No test, no spend figures, no before/after.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (asset QA), so age is irrelevant. The solo operator here is both maker and reviewer, so the honest translation is: your own discomfort watching a clip is a sufficient reason to reject it, and the defect gets named specifically before a re-shoot. It has a real home — the only video sources available are a repeat-customer testimonial clip for a Shopify PDP or Amazon video slot, or a clip on the live $30/day Meta campaign — and it adds a concrete line to the fully prescriptive brief the customer-as-creator path already requires ('phone braced or propped, auto-zoom off, no digital zoom, one continuous take'). Free and editorial. Capped at 5 because it is a checklist item rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is a single anecdote, video is deliberately rare here under the static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing, and marketing-ai-product-imagery already owns the equivalent QA pass for static frames — the additive part is narrow: motion and stability defects, and the rule that a physically uncomfortable asset is rejected even when the delivery and the script are both good.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #3: If you see something, say something" (social post)*

## When other people on a review chain have already approved an asset, do not treat their sign-off as agreement — say the defect out loud anyway, because nobody cares about the ad's success as much as you do.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Reviewers suppress their own perception once someone else has signalled approval, so a defect that everyone individually noticed passes unchallenged. One person naming it releases the rest of the group to confirm it. Deference is also misplaced: the editors and vendors most brands use are cheap, fast and have no direct-response training, so the owner's judgment is usually the better one.

**Evidence offered:** The same single anecdote: a head of growth, a second marketing exec and the video editor had all said 'looks good', and all three separately admitted the same nausea once it was flagged. Plus an email exchange with a reader about imposter syndrome. Assertion, no data.

**Fit here (5/10):** Durable-principle class (review discipline / pluralistic ignorance), so age is irrelevant, and the mechanism is real and non-obvious. Parked under `team` — it needs at least one other person in the approval chain, which does not exist today: there is no designer, editor, agency or media buyer, so there is nobody to defer to and nobody to speak up against. Trigger: the first time an outside editor, designer or agency produces an asset that Real Skin Care ships. Scored 5 on the merit it will have then — it is a management and review habit rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is one anecdote, and it substantially overlaps the personal-review rule above; the additive part is only the instruction not to read a collaborator's approval as agreement.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #3: If you see something, say something" (social post)*

## Classify creators as UGC creator, creator, influencer, or celebrity based on how they cultivate community — readable in their comments — not on micro/nano/macro follower tiers.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Follower count no longer predicts reach under persona-based targeting, but the type of community a person has built predicts whether their audience will actually respond to a brand message. The comment section is the readable signal: emphatic, personal replies mean the audience treats the account as a voice they trust rather than a feed they scroll.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion from ten years of practice plus definitional examples (a 50K-follower account hitting 100K views with emphatic comments is an influencer; Love Island alumni are celebrities because their notoriety originated off-platform). No performance data.

**Fit here (6/10):** A screening heuristic, not a spend commitment: judge a prospective creator by the kind of community readable in their comments rather than by follower tier. Durable-principle class (how audiences respond to endorsement), not fast-decaying platform mechanics, and it plugs straight into the sourcing/screening step of this skill — it is also the axis the brief-depth ladder above is indexed on, so classifying correctly is what tells you how prescriptive the brief has to be. What is missing is the creator relationship itself: paying or partnering with someone who is not the solo operator. Trigger: the first time there is budget or product-seeding capacity to engage an outside creator at all. The original rejection leaned on 'no creator budget and no media buyer', which is a people gate, not a merit problem.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Source creators by browsing TikTok and Reels inside your niche looking for hyper-viral posts from accounts that did not already have millions of followers.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** A post that outperformed its own account's follower base is evidence the person can make content that travels, which is the trait you are actually buying — not reach.

**Evidence offered:** Described as where her team starts, with the caveat that sourcing this way alone 'is going to be a tough go'. Assertion, no hit-rate or conversion data.

**Fit here (6/10):** Sourcing heuristic with a clean, testable mechanism, durable rather than platform-dated, free to execute (browsing costs nothing) and it produces a concrete shortlist you can then screen on delivery in the first 2-3 seconds and classify by comment-section community. What is missing is the person on the other end — engaging a creator means paying or seeding someone who is not the solo operator. The original rejection's 'no ad account' reasoning is now false. Trigger: any budget or product-seeding allowance for one outside creator.

## Recruit from actor networks such as Backstage when UGC creators and influencers keep underdelivering, because trained actors know how to emote.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** The scarce skill in on-camera content is performance, and actors are selected and trained for exactly that, so casting for it directly beats hoping a creator or a customer happens to have it.

**Evidence offered:** Anecdote from managing a Brooklyn studio where they routinely hired actors to shoot UGC. No performance comparison against creator-sourced content.

**Fit here (5/10):** Sound and honestly translatable: when creator content keeps underdelivering, the missing variable is on-camera performance, and casting for that skill directly beats hoping a customer has it — it is the explicit fallback for the bimodal outcome the customer-as-creator path above accepts. Durable talent-sourcing principle, not platform mechanics. Blocked purely by people and production — an actor, a shoot, direction — none of which the solo operator supplies. Trigger: the business is already running creator content, has hit a ceiling attributable to flat delivery, and has budget for a paid shoot. Mid score because it is a fallback move, not a first move.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Find a freelancer by first writing down the exact skill combination the job needs and the prior career path an ideal candidate would have, then searching for that specific combination of terms plus 'resume' rather than browsing job boards.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** A generic search or job-board browse returns a large undifferentiated pool you cannot filter; specifying the skill combination and the experience path first turns the search into a filter that only people who already have both can pass, so the shortlist is pre-qualified before you contact anyone.

**Evidence offered:** A worked example for a website build (CSS + video editing, ex-graphic-designer and ex-webmaster) with the literal query string and three named results, plus a Firefox extension recommendation. No hit-rate data.

**Fit here (4/10):** Platform-mechanics class for the execution detail — a 2008 Google resume-search string and a Firefox extension are ~18 years old and the pool of freelancers with indexed HTML resumes has moved to LinkedIn, Upwork and portfolio sites — so the recipe must be rebuilt rather than trusted. What survives is the durable kernel: derive the exact skill combination and prior-role path from the job before searching, and search for that combination rather than for a category. Parked under `team` because it exists only to hire someone who is not the solo operator. Trigger: the first time there is budget for a paid outside asset (a product photographer, or the video editor implied by the static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing). Scored on the merit it will have then: a narrow sourcing heuristic that overlaps the TikTok-browsing and Backstage entries above, though it pairs usefully with the track-the-winners rule below — the search defines who gets a first shot, the track record decides who gets the next one.

*Source: Rich Schefren — "The Entrepreneurial Emergency" (special report, part 4 of 6)*

## Track which specific video editors and which specific creators produced past winners, and route new scripts to those named people rather than rotating through whoever is available.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** Execution quality is not evenly distributed across vendors, and the same script routed through a strong editor or a strong on-camera creator produces a materially different asset. Because the person doing the execution is a variable you can select rather than a constant, tracking who produced past winners and re-assigning them raises the hit rate without changing the script at all.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion only. Stated as advice to agency-side strategists ('fight to have those video editors be the ones'), with no hit-rate comparison between vendors and no named example.

**Fit here (5/10):** Needs editors, creators or vendors who are not the solo operator, and today there is no editor, no designer, no creator roster and no agency, so there is nobody to select between. Durable-principle class (vendor selection), so age is irrelevant. Trigger: the first time Real Skin Care has used more than one outside editor or more than one customer-creator, at which point the rule is to keep a written record of which person produced which result and re-brief the proven one rather than rotating. Scored 5 on the merit it will have then, not on today's inability to run it: it is a selection heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism, the evidence is pure assertion, and it sits close to the within-person-variance argument this skill already carries (a single flat take is evidence about the take, not the person) — the additive part is only the instruction to track vendor-level outcomes and deliberately re-assign the winners. Note the tension to hold: within-person variance says do not condemn someone on one bad take, while this says do promote someone on an accumulated record, so the unit of judgment is a track record across several assets, never a single file. The 'fight your client for them' framing has no honest form for a solo operator and drops away entirely.

*Source: Stefan Georgi — "Secret of the DTC Universe #1: A winning creative is only 30% about copy" (social post)*

## Stop doing one-off 'churn and burn' creator deals: work with multiple creators simultaneously and move to long-term partnerships — creators on monthly retainer, given internal email addresses, titles, and standing deliverables.
**Stage:** team — parked until the team phase opens.

**Why it works:** On-camera performance varies within the same person, so a roster averages out the variance and a longer relationship lets you capture a creator's best work rather than one bad session. A creator embedded in the brand over time also produces more knowledgeable content and represents the brand publicly, compounding trust in a way a single sponsored post cannot.

**Evidence offered:** Side-by-side of two of her own videos, one flat and one high-energy, as proof that the same creator varies, plus named examples: her own Motion chief-evangelist arrangement, Caraway's retained creator roster, and Hype 10's full-time creators. No roster-size, partnership-length, revenue or performance figures.

**Fit here (6/10):** The mechanism is durable and non-obvious — within-person variance is the reason a single flat clip is weak evidence about a person, and it is what makes both a roster and a repeat relationship pay. It is also the natural pairing for the track-the-winners rule above: a retainer is how you keep the proven performer once the record identifies them. The original call that this is 'structural, not stage' is exactly the reasoning the current rules overturn: recurring creator retainers need people who are not the solo operator, which is a team gate. Trigger: creator content is already earning its cost on a one-off basis and there is recurring margin to commit to a retainer.

*Source: Dara Denney — "How I Source, Brief & Manage Creators (My Exact System)" (_VtpfVeB2Kc)*

## Compensate creators on backend performance rather than upfront fees when you are starting out or want volume without strict brand guidelines — the model performance-pay UGC platforms (Tribe, Influy) are built on.
**Stage:** scale — parked until the scale phase opens.

**Why it works:** Shifting creator compensation to the back end removes the upfront cash barrier, so a low-budget brand can accumulate content volume it could not otherwise afford; the creator carries the risk in exchange for upside.

**Evidence offered:** Assertion that she has used essentially all the UGC platforms and finds the performance-pay model 'really interesting' for brands starting off. No take-up rates, content-quality comparison, or results.

**Fit here (5/10):** The durable kernel is the compensation structure, not the vendor: paying on the back end removes the upfront cash barrier, which is exactly the constraint a $2,700/mo brand has. It fails today on the earnings side — a creator cannot make meaningful money off a share of ~54 orders/month, so nobody good accepts the deal. Trigger: order volume high enough that a performance share is worth a creator's day of shooting (roughly several hundred orders/month). The named platforms (Tribe, Influy) are fast-decaying tooling specifics and should be re-verified rather than trusted when this unparks; the pay-on-backend structure is what carries.
