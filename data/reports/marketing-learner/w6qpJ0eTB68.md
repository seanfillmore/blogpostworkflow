## Adopted

### Build the 'yap' (founder talking straight to the phone) as the core skill and the brand's moat, and escalate it through four levels. OG yap: just talk. Graphic yap: overlay a visual to support a point. Prop yap: build the talk around a physical prop such as a notebook or a numbers poster. Cinematic yap: talk with B-roll overlaid. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Pure storytelling with no visual crutch trains the hook, curiosity gaps, tension and the problem-solution arc. Once that lands, each added layer (graphic, prop, B-roll) raises production on a story that already works. 'Distribution is the only moat left': in categories with little product differentiation, buyers flock to the solution they have seen, so a founder who can yap well wins over equivalent products nobody knows exist.

**Evidence:** Lucas Pactor grew Tribe by talking to camera, often without a title hook or captions, beating functionally similar competitors. Alex Earle 'yapped her entire career' through get-ready-with-me videos to a Netflix show. Brier's notebook and Poor Boy Coffee's numbers poster are cited as prop yaps.

**Fit:** Close to the best-fit organic format for RSC: a solo founder, a phone, and a category (natural deodorant and body care) with modest product differentiation, where 'distribution is the moat' applies directly. Example levels: a prop yap holding a whiteboard of a batch's real cost, the $2.7K-to-$83K/month number, or the ingredient jar; a cinematic yap over batch-making B-roll. The talk track ports to Meta video ads and email. The existing founder-personal-brand claim gives no format ladder, so the four-level structure is new.

### Build a content-ideas habit by picking five categories completely unrelated to yours, pulling ten formats, concepts or structures that are working there, writing a translated version of each for your own category, and saving every source video. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Competitors in a category converge on the same formats. A format imported from an unrelated vertical is proven to hold attention but feels new in your feed. The drill also turns passive scrolling into analysis, so translation becomes a habit.

**Evidence:** Worked example: the '$1 vs $10 vs $100 cookie' baking format translated into a '$1 / $100 / $1,000 running shoes' test. Plus the hosts' agency track record. Mostly assertion.

**Fit:** The solo operator can run this directly on RSC's own organic short-form, the surface it was written for. The natural deodorant and body care category is crowded with the same 'aluminium is bad' and armpit-sniff formats, so imported formats are a real differentiation lever. Example translation: '$3 drugstore stick vs $14 natural vs $40 luxury deodorant, worn through a gym session' as a tiered-test video, then reused as a us-vs-them static and as Meta primary text. The competitor-teardown skill's description mentions importing structures from unrelated verticals, but none of its recorded claims holds this concrete drill (5 categories, 10 ideas, a written translation for each, saved sources). So this is an edit, not a duplicate.

### Before publishing, run every video through two tests. Sound-off test: is it still captivating watching only the visuals? Eyes-closed test: is it still interesting listening only to the audio, with no flat 'first we did this, then we did this' narration? — 7/10

**Why it works:** Viewers consume short-form in two partial modes: muted scrolling, and audio-only half-attention. A video that relies on one channel to carry the other loses whichever group isn't getting that channel. Testing each channel alone exposes dead visuals and flat narration separately, and passing both means each layer independently gives a reason to keep watching.

**Evidence:** Assertion only. Stated in two excerpts and illustrated with a vlog narration example.

**Fit:** This is a free pre-publish check one person runs at the edit. On RSC's Reels and TikTok: watch the cut muted and confirm the product action reads on screen (application, texture, the sweat-test moment). Then listen with eyes closed and confirm the voiceover still tells the story. It carries straight over to Meta video ads, where muted autoplay is the default. No existing claim covers a two-channel independence check. The only thing holding the score back is the absence of evidence beyond assertion.

### Run the brand's content as a serialized, numbered episodic story ('day X of building this brand', or a part-one video with a compelling premise). Cut off each episode at an unresolved problem or the moment of payoff with 'follow for part two to see if we fix it', then deliver the follow-up parts. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The day count or part number sets up continuity and investment in the journey. The cliffhanger turns curiosity about the outcome into a follow, because following is the only way to get the resolution. Each episode feeds the next one's audience, and the platform keeps serving later posts to people who engaged with earlier parts.

**Evidence:** A realtor's three-video series about an inherited Irish house nobody had entered for 20–30 years went from 2K to 250K followers in five days, reportedly around 20M views; the hosts call it a 'perfect storm'. Bad Hombres built a die-hard following with 'follow for part two' episodes and did about $1M selling burritos only in Arizona.

**Fit:** A durable format and an honest fit for a solo founder making his own products. Example: 'Day 14 of taking Real Skin Care from $2.7K to $83K/month' is a real, stakes-laden journey, with batches, ad tests and reformulations as episodes. A bounded series such as 'two-week switch to natural deodorant, part 1' also works. Cliffhangers must come from real open problems. It complements, rather than duplicates, the founder-personal-brand and pre-launch show-your-work claims. Follows are not revenue on their own, so pair the series with the lead-magnet or PDP CTA per the existing ask rules.

### Hold attention by introducing stakes. Narrate the setbacks and uncertainty ('it started raining, the whole shoot is outside, we just lost 30 minutes') so the viewer stays to see whether it works out, instead of reciting 'first we did this, then we did this'. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Stakes create an open question about the outcome: will it work? A chronological list of events has no unresolved tension, so nothing compels the next second. A setback mid-piece re-opens the loop after the hook has already been paid.

**Evidence:** The creator contrasts a flat 'then we did this' narration with a stakes version. Bad Hombres built its following on 'everything's going good… then our whole machine broke'.

**Fit:** A durable storytelling principle. Founder videos on RSC's own channels can narrate real stakes: a batch that won't set, a failed scent reformulation, an order rush before a ship date. The same structure works in Meta video primary text and email narratives. It is distinct from the hook-loop claim (about the opening line) and from 'lists, steps and stories', which doesn't name mid-piece setbacks as the retention device. Stakes must be real events, not fabricated drama.

### Make one to three 'one-shot' videos a week: a single roughly 7-second high-impact clip with a text-on-screen hook that tells the complete story (optionally on a trending audio). — 7/10

**Why it works:** It is the lowest-barrier format that can still go viral. It forces three skills: finding the single most impactful clip, telling a complete story in minimal on-screen words, and writing a text hook that stops the scroll. The text on screen confirms what the clip is showing.

**Evidence:** Assertion, plus the golf grip fix example: the coach adjusts the thumb, walks out of frame, and the shot goes straight, with 'POV: your thumb was just in the wrong position' on screen. Called 'still a staple on TikTok and Instagram'.

**Fit:** A durable, cheap format one person can shoot on a phone today. RSC candidates: one clip of a lip balm swipe, a deodorant application, or a sweat-through-the-day reveal, with a one-line text story ('POV: you switched off aluminium and it's 4pm'). The on-screen line is reusable as Meta primary text and static ad copy. No existing claim defines this format.

### Your day is not boring; your packaging is. Turn an ordinary day into a vlog by narrating minor moments as story beats, naming incidental characters ('this is my TSA agent, Lisa'), and surfacing small real stakes ('my bag was the last one off, I thought it was lost') instead of summarising it as 'nothing, I just flew to New York'. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Specific named moments and micro-stakes make the viewer feel taken along for the ride, which turns awareness into fandom. Skipping details, as people do in conversation, removes exactly the material that holds attention.

**Evidence:** Bad Hombres, Softies Burgers and Mile Off Running are cited as vlog brands. Bad Hombres did $1M in Arizona-only sales on a die-hard fan base. The creator re-narrates his own travel day as a demonstration.

**Fit:** A phone-only solo format, runnable today. A batch day, a farmers-market morning or a packing session becomes a vlog by narrating the real micro-moments: oils that wouldn't emulsify, 40 orders to ship by 3pm, a label printer jam. It builds the fandom behind a retention-led model where repeat buyers are about half of revenue. It is distinct from the existing 'manufacture an experience' and 'record the real process' claims, which cover what to capture, not how to package the mundane into beats.

### Develop scriptwriting skill through copy work. Transcribe proven viral videos and write the whole transcript out word for word, by hand or in a doc, 3–10 times per video for 5–7 days. Then write down the patterns you noticed (hook-to-first-sentence transitions, how curiosity is introduced, pacing, transition words) as your own writing framework, and keep doing some copy work as an ongoing practice. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Like a musician learning songs before writing them, rewriting proven scripts builds structural instincts as muscle memory. Writing the pattern notes turns those instincts into an explicit framework. When output quality drops, the cure is to go back to copy work.

**Evidence:** Anecdotal. The host's videos stopped performing; he went back to copy work, and a video within five days 'performed exceptionally well'. Also a 50-threads-in-50-days writing streak, and a newsletter writer who could finish an issue in two hours.

**Fit:** One person at a laptop can do this today. It feeds every surface RSC writes for: spoken short-form hooks first, then Meta primary text, email subject lines and Amazon bullets. The closest existing claim (read the top transcript and model it block by block) is a per-script step, not a repeated skill-building drill with a written pattern log. The score is held back by thin evidence (one self-reported anecdote) and an indirect payoff: craft improves first, conversion later.

### Do visual copy work: break a proven reference video into frames every 2–5 seconds, count how many distinct shots it uses for its runtime, label each shot type (wide, portrait/selfie, macro) and the angle, aesthetic and action, and map each visual back to the exact line it carries. Then script your own lines with a planned string of shots per line. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Visual pacing and shot selection carry retention as much as the words do. Counting shots and mapping them to lines shows the cut density and shot grammar that keep a proven video watchable. You can then plan 'wide, then selfie, then a jolt' when you write the line, rather than finding it in the edit.

**Evidence:** Assertion, plus a worked example ('I was walking down the street in New York when…' mapped to a three-shot string).

**Fit:** The operator films and edits RSC's own short-form and paid video himself, so he can run this today. Existing claims cover copying a reference's angles and replicating its timing in the edit. The new parts are the quantified breakdown (shots per runtime, shot-type tally) and assigning shots to each script line at the writing stage. The score is held back by that overlap and by assertion-only evidence.

### Because algorithms now distribute by topic and interest rather than by follower graph, plan organic content as a broad, high-reach topic with a specific interest layered underneath it. Deliberately cover the adjacent interest clusters around your category, not only the product itself. — 6/10

**Why it works:** The platform classifies each video by topic and interest and serves it to people with that interest, whatever the account's existing followers. A topic everyone in the audience shares gets reach, and the layered interest makes it specific. Owning a whole cluster of adjacent interests trains the algorithm on who to show the account to.

**Evidence:** The host pivoted a 20K-follower marketing TikTok to New York interest content: a 400K-view first video and roughly 15–20M views in total, with no help from the prior followers. The co-host plans to cover nervous-system, breathwork, calm and sleep clusters for his own brand.

**Fit:** This is a platform-mechanics claim and current as of October 2026. RSC can run it today on its own channels, for example topic = sweat and body odour, layered interests = gym bag, first date, summer commute, pregnancy and sensitive skin, plus adjacent clusters like clean ingredients and oral care. It adds the topic-plus-interest layering method and the 'followers no longer gate reach' premise to the existing rings and topic-bucket claims. The score is held back by that overlap and by single-creator evidence.

### When planning a month of organic content, weight the mix by production time. Make a handful of heavy multi-hour concept videos (around 5), and fill the rest with fast formats such as talking-head 'yap' videos and one-shot B-roll pieces (around 10 each), so you hit the posting cadence efficiently. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Concept-heavy videos are the likeliest breakouts but cost 5–6 hours each. Filling the rest of the calendar with low-effort formats keeps volume up without burning out a small operator, and each format type still gets enough repetitions to read.

**Evidence:** Assertion (raised by the co-host as a planning consideration).

**Fit:** Directly relevant to a solo operator producing all of RSC's organic video himself, where production time is the real budget. The existing 3-2-1 mix allocates by topic bucket, not by production cost, so this adds an independent axis. Runnable today, scaled to whatever monthly post count he commits to.

### When your core topic and script style are widely copied and per-video reach is falling (e.g. 500K–1M down to 250–300K), reinvent. Cap the copied core topic at about 20% of output and move the rest to 'one-of-one' formats that depend on access only you have, such as production vlogs, 'come with me' ride-alongs and one-shot continuous takes. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Easy imitation floods a format. Once competitors copy a script style, the algorithm and the viewer see interchangeable videos, so the ceiling on any one video falls. Formats built on footage only the creator can capture, like his own production process or a single continuous take through a real event, cannot be copied, so they stand out from the saturated pool. Keeping about 20% on the core topic keeps the content tied to what funnels to the business.

**Evidence:** Anecdotal. The creator's strong videos fell from 500K–1M to 250–300K views over a year as others copied his scripts. A peer (JT) 'snapped' over 3–4 months by rotating new formats, including a one-shot with 300K likes. Orange is cited as reinventing into a character. He asserts vlogs are 'back' on Instagram. No controlled comparison.

**Fit:** The natural-deodorant and clean-body-care category is saturated with interchangeable 'aluminum is bad / switch to natural' scripts. RSC's uncopyable material is footage of the operator hand-pouring, curing, filling, labeling and packing batches, which he can film and post alone today. What is new beyond existing skills is the copycat-saturation trigger and the explicit ~80/20 split between access-only formats and the category topic. The score is held at 6 because the evidence is two creators' anecdotes from a different niche and the split is a rule of thumb. 'Vlogs are back on IG' is a fast-decaying platform claim: current as of October 2026, but re-check within about 18 months. Spoken hooks name the access ('come with me while I pour 40 bars of deodorant') and translate to Meta primary text and email subjects.

### Build a 15-second video in three beats: open on a universal lived experience everyone in the audience recognises, add one line of context, then 'take a shot' at someone, ideally self-deprecatingly at a group you visibly belong to. — 5/10

**Why it works:** The universal experience earns instant 'that's me' recognition. The context line makes the stakes clear. The jab adds the tension and humour that drive shares and comments, and aiming it at yourself keeps it likeable rather than mean.

**Evidence:** One video ('everyone in New York's first question is what neighborhood are you from… god forbid you say Murray Hill'): roughly 400K views and 20K likes.

**Fit:** A compact spoken-hook structure the operator can film today. Example: 'Everyone who switches to natural deodorant gets asked the same thing… do you smell? And god forbid it's week two of the detox.' It is self-deprecating, and the target is a condition rather than a competitor, which matches the existing enemy-as-condition rule. It can later be adapted into Meta primary text openers. The score is held back by single-video evidence and because it suits reach better than selling a SKU.
