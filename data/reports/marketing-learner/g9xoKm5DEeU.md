## Adopted

### Do not let the model plan the edit itself. Have it return a planning document with the script beside the clip and, for each segment, scene designs recommended from the style library. Choose and annotate the scene for every line yourself, balancing variety and flow. Then paste that plan back as the build instruction. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The model builds animations well but is not creative enough to plan a great edit unaided. Without a strong plan, individually good animations do not add up to a watchable video. Human judgement on scene choice and balance is the step that decides quality.

**Evidence:** Creator's stated experience ('if you let AI do the planning on its own, it's actually not creative enough'), plus his walkthrough of the plan.

**Fit:** This is the highest-value step in the source. It keeps the operator's judgement at the decision point, since he is the editor and strategist, while handing execution to the model. It fits the solo-operator reality exactly and applies to any LLM-built RSC video, organic or paid. The existing director-skill claim converts a script into a shot list for generative video. It does not cover a model-recommended, human-selected scene plan for editing existing footage, so this is not a duplicate.

### Design on-screen captions with two fonts and a placement rule. Take each line of five words or fewer and pick out its most important words. Set those words large and bold in the centre, and tuck the remaining words smaller into the gaps above or below. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Emphasising the key words visually makes the viewer read the point rather than every word. The contrast between a plain sans font and an italic serif reads as premium, deliberate design rather than auto-captions. That raises perceived production value and holds attention.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus a breakdown of the reference creator's captions and the finished demo edit.

**Fit:** Organic short-form is a live surface the operator films and edits himself. Caption design is a pure editing decision he can apply today to every founder yap and Meta video ad in CapCut or Reels. No existing claim covers caption typography or emphasis placement. It is not higher because it is a polish lever on retention, not a conversion lever, and the binding constraint is conversion rate. The serif-italic look must be checked against RSC's brand identity rather than copied wholesale.

### Govern the A-roll-versus-cutaway rhythm by script function. Open on the speaker's face with captions so the viewer connects with him first. Return to the face whenever a new item or point is announced, and cut to a visual whenever that item is being described. — 6/10

**Why it works:** The face builds connection and signals a new section. The visual then makes the description concrete. A predictable announce-then-show rhythm keeps the viewer oriented in a list structure and makes every cutaway earn its place, so the edit is balanced and not visually exhausting.

**Evidence:** Assertion, demonstrated through his scene-by-scene planning of the four-career-patterns clip.

**Fit:** The rule translates directly to RSC's founder yaps and 'N reasons' paid video formats. For example, say 'reason two: no aluminium' on camera, then cut to a visual of what aluminium-free means. The operator can run it alone on today's footage. No existing claim specifies when to sit on the face versus when to cut away. The existing timing claims cover cut-to-word sync and clip trims, not this structural rule.

### Train the LLM on an editing style before asking it to edit:
1. Download about 20 strong reference videos and break each scene down by asking why it was designed that way.
2. Screenshot every good scene onto a board and categorise the scenes into the style's principles, naming the fonts, colours, sizes and patterns.
3. Give the videos plus the board to the model. Prompt it to make a word-by-word transcript and a frame-by-frame breakdown of animation placement relative to the script, design composition and animation timing.
4. Package the result as a skill with an extracted per-animation reference library. — 6/10

**Why it works:** The reference videos teach the model the logic of where and how animations sit against the script. The human-built board teaches it the design. The extracted library gives it concrete references to copy later. Without this, the model produces generic 'slop' edits.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus the comparison of a slop edit against his trained-skill output, and the 225 extracted animations.

**Fit:** The operator already runs Claude skills and edits his own video. Codifying RSC's own house edit style, using his best-performing organic clips plus competitor references, turns every future edit into a repeatable build rather than a manual one. This is a solo laptop task. Existing claims cover modelling one reference video by hand and a hook/script corpus. Neither covers training a visual edit-style skill from a categorised scene board. ### Have Claude produce the whole motion-graphic edit of a raw talking-head video on a standard subscription. It handles cuts, the planning document, text, lines, graphs, illustrations, revisions, sound and render. Once the plan is approved, the model:
- analyses the plan;
- pulls the matching references from the style library;
- writes an asset list;
- fetches the assets and fonts itself;
- builds each scene in code (Remotion) and renders a draft;
- asks you for anything it cannot find.
The operator spends about 15 minutes planning the edit and reviewing passes, instead of hand-editing or hiring an editor. — 6/10

**Why it works:** An agentic LLM that writes and renders video in code turns a written edit plan and a reference library into finished motion graphics. One person can then produce them without a motion designer or paid plugins. The asset list makes sourcing explicit before the build starts. The human's time moves from executing the edit to planning it and approving passes. Total cost becomes a flat subscription, and wall-clock time drops to about 2 hours, mostly unattended.

**Evidence:** One demo edit built this way. The creator's time breakdown:
- 15 minutes for cuts and the planning doc;
- 15 minutes of human planning;
- about 60 minutes for Claude to build the edit;
- 10 minutes of revisions;
- 15 minutes of sound.
Cost was a $100/month Claude plan. It is one demo from a course seller, with no audience-performance data.

**Fit:** This fits a solo operator who is his own editor, for whom organic yaps and graphic yaps are a live channel and a paid-creative source. Moving an edit from hours of hand-cutting to about 15 minutes of planning raises how many videos he can publish under the posting-volume and 100-day-cadence claims. No existing skill covers agentic LLM end-to-end editing of filmed talking-head footage. Two RSC adaptations apply. First, source product shots and B-roll from his own phone footage. Second, use only clearly public-domain or licensed fetched assets; the 'never use stock footage in an ad' claim still governs paid cuts. It is not higher because the evidence is one vendor demo, and the output's value depends on the style-skill and planning steps. The time savings should be verified on RSC footage. These are fast-decay tool mechanics, but current.

### Build the cutaways in an educational talking-head video from a fixed menu of scene types, each matched to what that script segment is doing. Every scene shares the same textures, edges and palette. The menu is:
- a graph for numbers or trajectories;
- a zigzag line through phases for progression;
- a side illustration with text for two things at once;
- a quote card for the closing line;
- an object or character callout for a named person or item;
- a painting reveal for a story moment;
- a single-subject centre illustration for a concept. — 5/10

**Why it works:** A small, consistent visual vocabulary makes the edit look like one designed piece rather than a collage. Mapping scene type to script function makes the choice for each segment fast and repeatable. Shared texture and colour carry the brand look across very different scenes.

**Evidence:** Assertion and a taxonomy of about 130 reference scenes from one creator's videos, plus one demo edit.

**Fit:** This fits RSC's educational organic pillar: ingredient mechanism, the deodorant adjustment-period timeline as a graph, and drugstore vs natural as a side-by-side. It also makes the 'graphic yap' level of the founder ladder concrete, and the operator can run it alone. There are two cautions. First, the source aesthetic is philosophical and product-free, so the product must still appear (see the existing closing-product-frame claim). Second, its paintings and stock B-roll conflict with the existing 'never use stock footage in an ad' claim on paid cuts. So scope it to organic education, using RSC's fixed brand identity rather than Brooks' look.

### In a minimal-animation talking-head edit, map each on-screen graphic event type to a fixed sound, all over a low cinematic music bed:
- a click when text appears;
- a highlighter or marker sound when a line or graph draws;
- a whoosh on each transition. — 5/10

**Why it works:** A one-to-one link between graphic events and sounds gives the viewer an audio cue each time new information appears. That pulls attention onto the overlay and keeps the pacing feeling active in a sparse visual style. It also makes the sound pass a repeatable recipe rather than a fresh decision on every cut, and the animations feel tactile and premium.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus the demonstrated final edit.

**Fit:** RSC already escalates founder yaps to 'graphic yaps': text, ingredient callouts and comparison graphics over the talking head. A standing event-to-sound mapping makes those sound-ready in minutes, and one person can apply it in CapCut or have Claude apply it. The existing sound-design skill's 'what do you see' bucket covers diegetic in-world sounds. This extends it to UI and motion-graphic sounds, which are not already recorded. It is a modest craft lever, not a revenue driver on its own. Runnable today.

### Before the sound pass, put a folder of about 20 of the target style's best-performing videos in front of the AI editor. Have it derive from those references which sound effects go where and what the music should sound like, instead of choosing sounds by hand. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Proven videos in a style encode its audio conventions: what triggers a sound, how dense the effects are, and the mood of the music bed. An agent that pattern-matches against a corpus reproduces those conventions more consistently than ad-hoc choices. Because the corpus persists, each later edit is faster.

**Evidence:** Single demonstration: the finished Arthur Brooks edit is played on screen. The creator says the next run will take about half the time. No performance data.

**Fit:** RSC's founder films and edits his own organic short-form, and a reference corpus is something one person assembles at a laptop. He can save 15–20 top-performing videos in the formats he copies, such as founder yaps and natural-deodorant explainers. Feeding them to the editor keeps the sound pass consistent across every reel and every organic clip later run as paid creative. The existing see/feel claim derives effects from the shot, while this derives them from a style corpus, so it is distinct. Merit is moderate because audio polish is a secondary lever next to hook and angle, and the only proof is one course-seller demo.
