## Adopted

### Perception is reality: an improvement the buyer never perceives creates no value, so spend on making the time delay and effort feel smaller rather than only on actually shrinking them. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Value exists only in the prospect's mind, so a cheap perceptual intervention can beat an expensive operational one. The London Underground's dotted arrival map cost a few million and raised rider satisfaction more than making trains faster would have for billions — because what hurt was not the wait but the uncertainty of the wait.

**Evidence:** The London tunnel dot-matrix map anecdote (a few million vs billions), plus the assertion that 'many times they will have no idea' what you improved. Observational, no data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and unusually well matched: RSC cannot shorten the natural-deodorant transition period, but it can publish a dated week-by-week map of what happens and when — exactly the dot-matrix-map move, where the wait stays the same and the uncertainty disappears. Zero cost, solo-executable on the PDP, Amazon A+ block, and post-purchase emails, and it attacks the 18–22.5% repeat rate. It is the 'why' behind the adopted results-timeline tactic and generalizes it: any true reduction in effort must be stated on the page or it earns nothing.

### Engineer a big emotional win as close to the purchase as possible, and explicitly name the short-term benefits that appear along the way — because buyers purchase the long-term dream but only stay long enough to reach it because of the early wins. — 8/10

**Why it works:** The gap between paying and receiving the promised outcome is where people quit. An early, visible victory reinforces the purchase decision, builds trust, and creates momentum; the interim benefits (energy, social contact, small visible changes) are what carry them through a long ramp.

**Evidence:** Gym-client example (ads live and a first $2,000 sale within seven days against a $239,000/yr promise), the bikini-body example where higher energy and new friends are unpurchased-but-retaining benefits, and the deliberate use of a more aggressive opening diet for a fast win. Anecdotal, with an unnamed appeal to science.

**Fit:** Durable retention principle and the best-fit tactic in this excerpt: retention is the binding constraint, repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue, and the known churn point is the transition period — weeks where the long-term outcome has not arrived and the buyer concludes the product failed. It gives the post-purchase flow a job no existing entry names: define a concrete win observable in the first 48 hours (no irritation, scent holds through the evening, one swipe is enough) and list interim benefits so week two reads as progress. Zero cost, no attribution. Not 9 because a consumable cannot manufacture a win as vividly as a service — it must be an honest early observation.

### Before writing any offer or copy, list every problem the buyer encounters in the sequence they encounter it — what they must do immediately before, during, and after using the product — and for each, list the four reasons they'd fail or quit (not worth it, won't work for me, too hard/confusing, takes too long). — 8/10

**Why it works:** Purchases stall on unresolved friction the seller never thought to name. Walking the buyer's actual sequence in 'insane detail' produces dozens of specific objections instead of a vague guess, and the four value drivers act as a generator so each step yields four distinct problems rather than one.

**Evidence:** Worked example: a weight-loss problem list across grocery shopping, cooking, eating, and exercising, producing '16 core problems with two to four sub problems, so 32 to 64 problems total'; a second list from Brooke Castillo's relationship course. Assertion plus demonstration, no conversion data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and a zero-cost, no-traffic, solo-executable exercise that produces the raw input every other adopted skill consumes: the natural-deodorant buyer's real sequence is choose a scent → first application → transition period → residue on shirts → running out mid-transition → deciding whether to reorder. Since retention is the binding constraint and the churn cause is a misunderstood transition period, an explicit before/during/after problem inventory is exactly what populates the PDP FAQ, results timeline, post-purchase sequence, and next bundle. No existing skill owns the generative inventory.

**Target skill:** `marketing-problem-solution-inventory` (create)

### Enumerate every obstacle the prospect believes they will hit, commit to answering all of them, and build a concrete solution artifact for each — because a single unaddressed item is routinely the whole reason someone doesn't buy. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Objections are not weighted averages — a buyer with one unresolved dealbreaker does not buy at 90%, they don't buy at all. So coverage of the full objection set matters more than the polish of any one answer, and solving each obstacle with a real artifact (a guide, a template, an instruction) removes the veto instead of arguing with it.

**Evidence:** Anecdote: he refused to let weight-loss clients eat out and lost many sales, then improvised an 'eating out guide' to close a rent-critical sale, kept the guide, and never lost that objection again; plus the rule stated twice ('if only one of these needs is missing in a solution, it can cause someone not to buy'). Explicit caveat that failing to solve every obstacle still lets you sell, just to fewer people. No figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. Natural deodorant has a short, enumerable objection list — transition period, 'it quit by noon', staining, baking-soda irritation, scent choice, melting in transit — and each can be answered with free on-page copy, an FAQ block, an Amazon A+ module, or a card in the box. CRO-stage work requiring no spend, traffic, or attribution, attacking both first-purchase conversion and the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Not 9 because conversion-friction-audit already covers the risk/returns/proof half, and it must be reconciled with the information-fatigue rule: every objection gets an answer somewhere, collapsed by decision-relevance, not stacked above the fold.

### A guarantee only has teeth in conditional form — 'if you don't get X result in Y time period, we will Z' — and the conditions should be the specific actions the customer must take to succeed. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Without the 'or what', a guarantee is a vague reassurance nobody prices in. Naming the result, the deadline, and the remedy makes the promise concrete and shifts risk onto the seller. Conditions do double duty: they cap your downside so you can afford a bolder promise, and they instruct the buyer in the behavior that actually produces the outcome, so a larger share of claimants succeed and never claim at all.

**Evidence:** Contrast of a bad guarantee ('we will get you 20 clients guaranteed') against a conditional one with deadline and stated remedy; a secondhand Jason Fladlien claim of conversion moving 2-4x from guarantee quality; the $25,000 store-buyout guarantee on a $2,997 course conditioned on stated ad spend, claimed to have generated an extra $3M in sales while paying out only 10 refunds; plus the assertion that conditional guarantees typically outperform a traditional 30-day money back guarantee in net conversions.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and an unusually exact fit. Natural deodorant has a named result and a named window — get through the transition period — and the existing skill covers naming the fear and placing the promise but not structuring it. 'Use it daily for 30 days following the included guide; if you're still not dry, we'll replace it or refund you' is free to write, sits on the PDP and an Amazon bullet, needs no traffic or attribution, and the condition itself pushes the buyer through the exact churn point holding repeat at 18–22.5%. Not 9 because the remedy is real margin at a $50.46 AOV, the outsized-payout versions are margin-suicide here, and Amazon's returns policy partly duplicates it on the larger channel.

### Match the guarantee type to your ticket size and buyer type — broad, unconditional satisfaction guarantees work best for lower-ticket B2C, while higher-ticket or B2B offers should use specific, conditional guarantees. — 8/10

**Why it works:** On low-ticket consumer purchases most buyers never bother to claim, so a maximally generous promise buys a large conversion lift at a small realized refund cost; as ticket and fulfillment cost rise, the same promise becomes expensive, so conditions and specificity are needed to keep it affordable.

**Evidence:** Reports using an unconditional satisfaction guarantee selling weight-loss programs with only two claims out of 4,000 sales in three and a half years; the low-ticket-vs-high-ticket rule stated as practitioner judgment with no data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and the selection rule the existing guarantee tactic lacks: RSC is exactly the case the source says should go broadest — low-ticket B2C consumables at a $50.46 AOV, where the redemption cost of a no-questions 30-day promise is bounded and the CRO/Offer stage is reachable with zero spend. It also warns against over-engineering conditions on a $12 deodorant. Not higher because Amazon (~$1,800/mo) already gives buyers a generous platform return policy, so incremental lift concentrates on the ~$875/mo Shopify side, and refund cost is real cash at this revenue level.

### There are only three levers of growth — get more customers, increase average purchase value, and increase purchase frequency — so diagnose which lever you can actually move before doing any work. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Revenue is literally new customers per month multiplied by lifetime value (average cart value × number of purchases). Any activity that does not move one of those three numbers cannot move revenue, so the arithmetic filters out work with no revenue path.

**Evidence:** Worked arithmetic only: 10 clients/mo × $1,000 lifetime value caps the business at $10,000/mo. Assertion plus illustrative math, no data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, age irrelevant. Load-bearing here because the gated sequence rules out lever one for now, leaving AOV ($50.46) and purchase frequency (18–22.5% repeat) — exactly the two numbers the business is weakest on. Zero cost, solo-executable, no attribution needed. Not higher because it is a diagnostic frame rather than a mechanism and partly overlaps the four-offer-type coverage audit in marketing-upsell-offer-design.

### Compute lifetime value on gross profit (revenue minus the direct cost of serving one more customer), not on revenue, before deciding what you can afford to give away. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Only gross profit is actually available to fund discounts, guarantees, free items, and acquisition. Sizing incentives against revenue overstates what you can spend, so a promotion that looks affordable at the top line can be cash-negative per order.

**Evidence:** Definitions with worked examples ($10 lotion at $2 cost = 80% gross profit; $1,000/mo agency at $100/mo cost held five months = $4,500 LTGP). Definitional, no performance data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. This is the missing sizing constraint under nearly every offer tactic adopted here — the permanent loyalty rate, the 80–90% splinter, store credit, free-item lead magnets — all capped in scoring precisely because the margin give must be sized from real landed cost at a $50.46 AOV and ~$2,700/mo revenue. Solo-executable with a spreadsheet. Not higher because it produces a number, not a revenue mechanism, and postage makes per-order gross profit fiddlier than the book's service examples.

### Stop competing on bigger claims (the top of the value equation) and put your effort into the bottom two terms — making the outcome immediate and the process effortless — because claims are easy to make and therefore non-differentiating. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Anyone can promise a larger outcome, so top-side claims converge across competitors and stop carrying information. Time-to-result and effort are hard to actually reduce, so a reduction there is defensible and is what the best companies (Apple, Amazon, Netflix) actually sell. Mathematically, driving the bottom terms toward zero drives value toward infinity regardless of how modest the promise is.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus the author's own career reversal ('the older I get, the more I have shifted my focus to the hard stuff') and the iPhone/one-click Amazon/Netflix examples. No numbers.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and a prioritization rule the existing four-term audit does not contain: it tells a solo operator which two terms to work first. Load-bearing here because natural deodorant's churn cause is a time-and-effort problem (transition period, reapplication, 'does it work by 3pm'), not a promise problem, and the temptation at $2,700/mo is to escalate claims. Zero cost, no attribution. Not higher because it is a direction rather than an artifact and must be paired with a concrete page change.

### Convert each listed problem into a solution mechanically by adding 'how to' and reversing the problem into solution-oriented language, producing a checklist of exactly what the offer must deliver. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Reversing the problem statement guarantees the solution language answers the objection in the buyer's own terms, and doing it as a mechanical transform means you never write from a blank page — the problem list becomes the headline list.

**Evidence:** Demonstrated transform: 'buying healthy food is expensive' → 'how to buy healthy food for less than your current grocery bill'; 'cooking takes too much time' → 'how to cook meals in under 5 minutes'. Called 'copywriting 101'. Assertion plus demonstration.

**Fit:** Free execution layer on the problem inventory, applying directly to PDP section headers, Amazon A+ blocks, and post-purchase subject lines ('the transition period will make me smell worse' → 'how to get through the first two weeks without a mid-day panic'). Partially overlaps how-to stems in marketing-awareness-level-messaging, but that skill picks the stem by awareness stage while this derives the sentence from a specific named objection. Held at 7 because reversed problem statements can drift into unsupportable claims in body care and must be paired with the existing specificity rule.

### Score every candidate offer component on the sales-to-fulfillment continuum — perceived value to the buyer against cost and labour to you — cutting high-cost/low-value first, then low-cost/low-value, and keeping only low-cost/high-value and high-cost/high-value items. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Every value-add consumes a finite delivery resource (your time, your cash), and perceived value and delivery cost are independent variables, so an offer built by intuition carries components that cost real money without moving the buying decision. Scoring each candidate on the four value-equation terms exposes which components are margin leaks and which are free leverage; the sweet spot is the highest ratio of perceived value to fulfillment load.

**Evidence:** 'Step #5: Trim & Stack' with the four-question value screen and the live-in-with-the-client thought experiment (infinitely valuable but unaffordable delivery, stepped back until bearable), plus the Gym Launch anecdote of flying out 21 days per gym at ~$100k/mo up front, then replacing it with a taught version at a third of the price serving hundreds of gyms from home. Anecdotal, self-reported.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and the missing cost discipline for a $2,700/mo solo operation that has already adopted several give-something-away tactics: a printed transition-period guide or scent-matching quiz is low-cost/high-value, while free travel sizes and deep-discount splinters are real COGS plus postage at a $50.46 AOV. It also kills offer ideas carrying per-order labour (handwritten notes, personal consultations, hand-picked bundles) in favour of zero-per-order-cost ones. Free to run as a one-hour screening pass. Not higher because it is a filter, not a revenue mechanism, and the four-term equation itself already lives in conversion-friction-audit.

### Favor bonuses and deliverables that are created once and given away endlessly — checklists, tools, templates, guides, calculators — because they carry the biggest gap between what they cost you and what they're worth, and they score high on the value equation for low effort and zero time delay. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Anything delivered per-customer scales your cost with your volume, capping margin. An asset created once is paid for on first use and then supplies value to every subsequent customer for free, so it can be attached to every offer indefinitely without eroding margin — and a condensed checklist is usable immediately, which scores higher on perceived value than a longer training.

**Evidence:** His Excel meal-plan generator (~100 hours to build, then 15 minutes per personalized plan sold at high prices); gym assets (bulking blueprints, eating-out system, travel guide, grocery calculator, plateau plans) later used by 4,000+ gyms; the book itself; plus the author's note that information products work as bonuses because of 'high perceived value, low cost, and zero operational effort.' Anecdotal.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and it fits a solo operator with no team better than almost anything else here: build the transition-period timeline guide, the scent/SKU match quiz, and the named routine sheet once, then attach them to every SKU, bundle, packaging insert, Amazon A+ block, and post-purchase email forever. Zero marginal cost, no ad budget, and it feeds the retention constraint since the guide is what gets a first-time buyer through the churn window. Also the affordability counterweight to 'give away something other people charge for', which pushes toward samples with real COGS. Not higher because a printed insert still carries per-unit print cost and a PDF has weaker perceived value in physical body care.

### Assemble the offer as named problem→solution modules, enumerate every component explicitly, give each a justified dollar value and an explicit link to the objection it removes, and show the stacked total against the actual price. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Perceived value is built only from what is explicitly enumerated — deliverables the buyer cannot see do not count. A stack of individually named and priced components each mapped to a problem the buyer already has forces a value-based rather than price-based decision, because nothing on the market has the same component list to compare against, and the stack doubles as objection handling since each reason not to buy is answered by an item they are being given.

**Evidence:** Worked example: 'Foolproof Bargain Grocery System ($1,000 value)', 'Ready in 5min Busy Parent Cooking Guide ($600 value)' and six more modules totalling $4,351 sold at $599 (facilities later sold versions at $2,400–$5,200); the infomercial precedent of one $38.95 knife plus 37 additional items; an eleven-point bonus checklist (name it, relate it to their issue, say how you created it, prove it, paint the after-state, price it). Assertion plus self-reported pricing history, no conversion data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and it extends the already-adopted named-bundle tactic with a component-and-value layer nothing currently covers: a 'Natural Switch Starter' page listing the deodorant, the refill, the transition guide, and the scent-match quiz as separate itemized lines with stated values and the fear each kills. Free and solo-executable on a Shopify bundle PDP and an Amazon A+ module, and it lifts the $50.46 AOV with no new traffic. Held at 7 because a 12-SKU physical catalog has a hard ceiling on how many components exist to enumerate, and inflated 'value' figures on a $50 body-care bundle read as infomercial — every ascribed price must be a defensible à-la-carte referent.

### Give every promotion an explicit believable 'reason why' and a real end date — re-wrap the same core promotion every month with a new seasonal name and dates (New Year Promo, Valentines Lovers Promo, Sexy By Spring Special, Grand Opening, Anniversary, Back to School) and display the dates everywhere in copy and on landing pages. — 7/10

**Why it works:** A stated reason answers 'why is this being offered and why should I act now', converting a bare price cut into a time-bounded event; the seasonal wrapper supplies a legitimate start and finish for something you actually run year-round, so you get a real deadline plus novelty without changing the offer economics. Without a reason-why the discount reads as the normal price and buyers learn to wait for the next one.

**Evidence:** Worked example listing four consecutive monthly promo names, asserted to make conversions 'go through the roof' and to take five minutes of editing, named as his number-one strategy for local businesses; plus the fraternity-party-planner framing ('we had a party because a guy got his wisdom teeth removed') and a list of example reasons. No figures.

**Fit:** Durable promotional framing with no platform dependency. It is the cheapest solution to a real solo-operator problem: what to email a small list about every month without inventing new products or deepening discounts, and it is directly corrective for the reflex to send a bare percent-off code. Re-uses bundles and multipacks already adopted, costs nothing, needs no ad budget or attribution, and every send lands on the retention surface where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided. Not higher because the deadline must be genuinely honored or it erodes list trust, and at ~54 orders/month the lift is judged, not measured.

### Change the unit you sell so it cannot be price-compared — assemble promotion, value proposition, premium price, and guarantee into one incomparable package instead of selling the same comparable item cheaper. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Comparability is what drives prices down: if a shopper can line your product up next to an equivalent, the cheaper one wins by default and margins compress to survival level. A package with no direct equivalent forces the buyer to evaluate it against buying nothing, so price is set by perceived value rather than by the nearest competitor, and the offer does the differentiation work that sales explanation cannot.

**Evidence:** Before/after money math for a lead-gen agency: $1,000 down plus $1,000/mo retainer versus a one-time pay-for-performance offer with a guaranteed appointment count; described as real rounded numbers from one company, plus a claim of $500k/yr to $28M/yr in 18 months on under $1M of ad spend. No controlled comparison.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and natural deodorant on Amazon is close to the pure commodity case described — a single stick sits next to Native and Every Man Jack and loses on per-unit price.
