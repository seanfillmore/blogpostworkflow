# Billy Mays Marketing Magic

**Creator:** Rich Schefren  
**Source:** blog post — `billy-mays-marketing-magic`  
**Published:** 2009-07-08  



Found 25 tactics: 14 adopted, 11 rejected.

## Adopted

### Demonstrate rather than describe: lead with the product attribute whose result you can actually show working on camera or in frame, design the demonstration to be dramatic enough to produce a 'wow', and treat any attribute whose result cannot be shown as unsellable in a visual slot (relegate it to text). When a demonstration goes stale, shoot a bigger one rather than repeating it. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A remote buyer who cannot see, touch or hold the product has no sense of ownership, so desire has to be created visually. A shown result is believed because the prospect draws the superiority conclusion themselves; an asserted one is discounted. A dramatic demonstration telegraphs the benefit faster than words can, while complex, invisible or prevention-based results have no demonstration available and never produce the 'wow' that stops a scroll.

**Evidence:** Teardowns only, no conversion data: OxiClean stains disappearing on camera, Mighty Mend It repairing a flag that withstands hurricane-force winds, the DualSaw cutting a car in half, Billy holding a live skunk for an odor killer, dangling from the HMS Bounty for Mighty Putty, hitting his hand with a mallet for Impact Gel insoles; plus the negative case that prevention-based products 'never sell well' because there is no visible result, and the assertion that 'regular claims with heaps of evidence are not even a fraction as potent.'

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (creative and claim selection), so the 2009 date is irrelevant. Free, editorial, and it lands on the Amazon image stack that carries the larger ~$1,800/mo channel plus the Shopify PDP. The runnable form is a single static frame, not a stunt video — there is no videographer or editor, and the static-first rule in marketing-paid-creative-testing already argues a static has two failure points one person controls. It gives a concrete sorting rule for a 12-SKU body-care catalogue: residue on a dark shirt, the no-burn claim on sensitive skin, one swipe versus three, the end-of-a-12-hour-shift frame all photograph as visible results; 'aluminium-free' and 'made in USA' do not and belong in text rather than the hero frame. Held at 6 because marketing-product-image-stack already owns the transformation format, the main-image lever list and the 1-second comprehension test, so the additive part is narrow — the selection rule that the frame leads on whichever attribute reads as a visible result. Bounded by the Amazon compliance guardrail already recorded there: the demonstration must be the real product producing a real result, which strips away exactly the 'over-the-top' latitude the source is selling.

### Set a distinct front-end 'strike price' whose job is acquiring customers, separate from back-end pricing whose job is lifetime value — and require the front-end item to carry a large multiple over landed cost so it can fund the media, fulfilment and distribution behind the acquisition. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Front-end and back-end products are optimised against different objectives, so pricing them on the same logic sub-optimises both. A front end priced above the market's impulse threshold does not acquire; one without a large margin multiple over landed cost cannot pay for the media that delivers it, no matter how well it converts.

**Evidence:** Billy Mays' stated 'magic number' of $19.95 for two-minute spots, Schefren's own $47 front-end strike price for his information business, and the DRTV requirement of roughly a five-times markup to cover media, distribution and fulfilment (achieved by manufacturing overseas). Self-reported figures from two very different businesses, no test data.

**Fit:** Durable pricing logic (offer construction), so the 2009 date is irrelevant. Live right now: a $30/day Meta campaign is running against ~$2,700/mo combined revenue, so whichever SKU becomes the paid entry point has to carry contribution margin above cost per acquisition, and the offer that follows the soap giveaway is exactly this decision. marketing-offer-construction already owns the low-COGS splinter entry offer, the price-escalation procedure and the four-offer-types audit; the additive part is the explicit front-end/back-end price split and the margin gate that sits on the front end. Held at 6 because none of the specific numbers transfer — $19.95, $47 and a 5x markup come from DRTV impulse goods and information products, not a $12 deodorant carrying real COGS and postage at a $50.46 AOV — so the honest form is to compute landed contribution margin against allowable cost per acquisition rather than import a multiple. At ~54 orders/month the resulting price point is a judgement call, never a measurement.

### Put the product in the hands of real users in the hardest use case before you build any promotion — give free samples in exchange for honest opinions and what they would pay — and kill or fix the product if the feedback is not strong. — 6/10

**Why it works:** No amount of promotion rescues a product that does not deliver; disappointment eventually catches up and destroys the reputation the sales message depends on. Testing in the most demanding real-world conditions surfaces failures the seller cannot see from the inside, and asking testers what they would pay surfaces the price before any money is committed to the campaign.

**Evidence:** Impact Gel insoles tested with workers standing 8+ hours a day on concrete at an oil-change shop; the DualSaw tested with the Clearwater Fire Department; Schefren's own VIP customer group receiving products in advance with authority to kill a launch, plus an AWAI joint-venture product in field testing. Anecdotal throughout, no figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle (product validation before promotion), age irrelevant, and nothing in the current fleet owns it — marketing-review-mining sources proof from products already shipped, marketing-retention-offers cultivates top buyers for repeat revenue, but nobody covers a go/no-go panel before a variant ships. Honest solo-operator translation: before releasing a new scent, a reformulation or a bundle, send free units to a handful of the top repeat buyers in the hardest use case (12-hour shifts, heavy sweaters, baking-soda-sensitive skin) and act on the answers. Costs a few units of COGS, needs no traffic, no attribution and no team, and it attacks the binding retention constraint at its source, since the 18–22.5% repeat rate is driven by first-use disappointment. Two hard limits: marketing-offer-construction gates catalogue expansion, so this applies to variants and reformulations rather than SKU 13; and product-for-feedback must never be routed into an Amazon review (a flat TOS violation) and must be disclosed as a material connection if the feedback later becomes a testimonial. Held at 6 because the evidence is anecdotal and a panel of five is a signal, not a measurement.

### Build proof on a Sinatra test — one extreme example that, if the product survives it, implies it will handle every ordinary case ('if it kills skunk smell, imagine the litter box'; 'if it mends a parachute, it'll mend your jeans') — and go recruit the high-profile hardest-case users who supply it, even unprofitably. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A single sufficiently extreme case establishes credibility across the whole domain without needing volume of proof, because the buyer reasons a fortiori from the hard case to their own easy one — and prospects believe a conclusion they draw themselves far more than a claim they are handed.

**Evidence:** The Ginsu cutting a tin can then a tomato; the What Odor skunk; Mighty Mend It tested in a wind tunnel and on a parachute jumped from 13,000 feet; Schefren's own 'coached 80 of the top 100 gurus' positioning. Teardowns and self-report, no conversion figures.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (claim and proof construction), age irrelevant, purely editorial and free. Genuinely additive: marketing-conversion-copy-angles owns specificity, claim calibration and attribution, and marketing-review-mining owns picking quotes whose before-state matches the reader — but nothing owns picking the hardest case precisely because it dominates the easy one. This catalogue has real hard cases sitting in the review corpus (twelve-hour shifts, people who 'sweat more than most', baking-soda-reactive skin), so the extreme-case proof can be sourced honestly rather than invented. Held at 6 because in body care the extreme claim must stay a displayed customer account rather than a manufactured stress test, and step two of the source's version — enticing high-profile users at a loss — costs real COGS at a $50.46 AOV, so the affordable form is asking existing hardest-case buyers for a detailed review through the Shopify request, never on Amazon where directed solicitation is a TOS violation.

### Depict the product being used with visibly minimal effort — hold the cloth by the corners, keep the grip loose, keep the motion small — and audit your existing marketing for imagery, words and pictures that unintentionally signal the customer will have to work hard. — 6/10

**Why it works:** How much effort a demonstration appears to take is registered subconsciously even when the viewer never notices it consciously, so the same product shown with visible exertion promises a harder experience than the same product shown effortlessly — and perceived effort is a purchase barrier independent of price.

**Evidence:** Two frame-level teardowns with timestamps (Awesome Auger at 0:35, Zorbeez at 0:09), each pointing at the grip and body posture, with the argument that the viewer's subconscious picks it up. Observational only, no test.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (perceived effort), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. Genuinely additive: marketing-product-image-stack governs who is in a lifestyle frame, the setting and the emotional register but never how much effort the depicted use appears to take, and marketing-conversion-friction-audit governs actual effort in the value equation and the rule that unperceived improvements create no value — this is the imagery-level version of the same term. It has an exact form here: one swipe rather than repeated scrubbing, a loose grip, no reapplication, a lifestyle frame where the product is used in passing rather than laboured over. That matters for the two surfaces carrying revenue — Amazon secondary slots on the ~$1,800/mo channel and the Shopify PDP — and it costs nothing. Held at 6 because it is a staging discipline rather than a revenue mechanism and, at ~54 orders/month, will never be measurable.

### Sell the removal of a pain the buyer feels today rather than prevention of a problem they might face later — and if they do not yet perceive the problem, confront them with it before offering the solution. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Impulse purchases require a problem the buyer wants solved right now; a preventative benefit has no felt urgency and no demonstrable result, so there is nothing to act on. Pain removal is easier to sell than progress toward a goal, and naming a problem the buyer has not consciously registered ('are you tired of knives that just don't cut?') creates the felt problem the offer then resolves.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus one worked headline ('are you tired of knives that just don't cut?') and an unattributed appeal to 'breakthroughs in NeuroMarketing'. No test data, no cited study.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (desire selection and positioning), so age is irrelevant. Free, purely editorial, applies to the PDP hero, Amazon bullets and the Meta primary text now running at $30/day. It substantially overlaps the desire-ranking rule already in marketing-awareness-level-messaging, which has already reached the same conclusion for this catalogue — aluminium-health worry is broad but low in urgency, mid-day odour anxiety is the default lead. Capped at 5 for that overlap and because the neuromarketing claim is asserted with no source. The genuinely additive part is a sharper categorical test than the three-axis ranking: ask whether the buyer can feel or see the result today, and if not, the claim is a prevention claim and belongs below the fold rather than in the lead.

**Target skill:** `marketing-awareness-level-messaging` (edit)

### Ask 'how does this product make my prospect's life easier?' and reverse the answer to find the pain, problem or frustration the campaign should target. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Every benefit statement contains an implied pain. Inverting the benefit surfaces that pain in the buyer's own terms, which is where the advertising has to start, because buyers respond to relief from something they feel rather than to an abstract improvement.

**Evidence:** Assertion with a single reusable prompt sentence. No worked output, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy generation), age irrelevant, free and solo-executable, and it completes a loop the existing skill only runs in one direction: marketing-problem-solution-inventory inverts problems into solution language and separately inventories every performance the product delivers, but never inverts a performance back into the pain it removes. Concretely: 'doesn't burn' inverts to 'the last one left my skin raw by day three'; 'holds through a long shift' inverts to 'checking your shirt at 3pm'. That output feeds the PDP, Amazon bullets and the giveaway primary text. Capped at 5 because it is one more mechanical transform inside an exercise already owned, and every inverted pain still has to clear the specificity and body-care claim rules before it ships.

**Target skill:** `marketing-problem-solution-inventory` (edit)

### Open by getting the prospect to agree or acknowledge the frustration — a question or a rhythmic statement of what they've been putting up with ('you swat, you shoo, you spray, but those flies still don't go away') — before making any claim. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Once a prospect has acknowledged a current pain, the likelihood of a purchase decision rises sharply; the acknowledgement is the equivalent of getting a foot in the door, because the buyer has now conceded the premise the rest of the pitch rests on.

**Evidence:** Four Billy Mays opening lines quoted (Awesome Auger, FLIES-Away, Slider Station, Tool Bandit), plus an unsourced appeal to 'advances in Neuromarketing have proven beyond a shadow of a doubt.' No data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (persuasion psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Applies to live surfaces: Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign, email subject lines, PDP hero copy. The category has an unusually clean version of the rhythmic form — 'you reapplied, you switched scents, you gave it two weeks, and it still quit by noon' — and the raw material is already in the voice-of-customer file. Capped at 5 because it sits very close to three rules already in marketing-conversion-copy-angles: negative framing, the prior-attempt walkthrough, and calling the buyer out by name. The additive part is narrow but real — the interrogative or concessive opener whose only job is to extract a silent yes before any claim is made. Must reconcile with the hook rules: the yes-opener still has to withhold the resolution and must not close the loop in line one.

### Eliminate the alternatives with sharp explicit contrast — compare your product against what the buyer already owns and uses ('you can't mince garlic with one of these, and if you use a press it's a mess') rather than making them connect the dots, and look for contrast on four axes: before/after, without your solution/with it, you/competitors, now/later. — 5/10

**Why it works:** When a prospect cannot easily see the difference between your product and the alternatives, the decision stalls; a sharp stated contrast lets them reach the superiority conclusion themselves, which they believe more than a claim, and gives the deciding brain something concrete to choose between.

**Evidence:** Two Billy Mays contrast lines (Grater Plater vs food processor and garlic press; Quick Chop vs a knife), plus an unsourced appeal to 'experts in Neuromarketing.' No figures.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, editorial. The additive part is specific and useful: the contrast target is the product class the buyer already owns — a conventional aluminium antiperspirant, or the natural stick that quit by noon — rather than a named competitor, which is exactly the constraint this catalogue operates under, since disparaging Native or Dr. Squatch's formulation is a claims and legal risk. The four-axis list is a cheap generator for PDP sections, Amazon bullets and A+ blocks. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three recorded rules: the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence in marketing-awareness-level-messaging, the us-vs-them graphic capped at three or four attributes in marketing-product-image-stack, and the shared-enemy-as-a-condition rule in marketing-conversion-copy-angles. Anything it generates must still be a defensible formulation fact.

### Consistency of persona is itself a sales asset: the same pitch delivered by a recognisable, familiar figure outsells the identical pitch from an equally skilled unknown, so keep the voice, look and signature elements identical across every appearance. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Constant repeated exposure produces familiarity, familiarity produces trust, and a pitch never stands on its own — the odds of a sale rise sharply when a relationship exists before the pitch. Consistent signature elements (voice, shirt, gestures) are what let repeated exposure accumulate into one recognisable person rather than many forgettable ones.

**Evidence:** The Pitchmen Grater Plater split test: Mays and Sullivan pitched the same product, same clothes, same lines, same scenes and camera angles, and Mays's version brought in 40% more revenue over a week of TV testing. One test, self-reported through a TV show, no spend or sample detail.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (positioning and trust), age irrelevant, free. It is the one item in this excerpt with a real controlled comparison behind it, and it has an honest solo-operator form: the founder is the only persona available, so hold the same voice, the same reason-for-formulating story and the same signature framing constant across the Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign, the founder-voice emails and the PDP — rather than writing each surface in a different register. Held at 5 because it substantially overlaps the spoken peer register and the authority-attribution lever in marketing-conversion-copy-angles, the additive part being only that consistency across surfaces compounds, and because the evidence is a celebrity effect built over years of national TV exposure, which a sub-1,000-subscriber list and ~54 orders/month cannot reproduce. Must be scoped away from Amazon titles and bullets, which need scannable claim copy.

### Introduce the price about halfway through the pitch and never later than two thirds, because the work that actually converts — bonuses, quantity doubling, risk reversal — all happens after the price has been stated. — 5/10

**Why it works:** The first half of the pitch only gets the viewer to consider buying; what moves them from considering to acting is the value being stacked on top of a price they already know. Withholding the price until the end means the strongest persuasion has nowhere to land.

**Evidence:** Observation of where Mays introduces price across his spots, plus a worked example of the Tool Bandit sequence (price, then second unit free, then a $10 bonus, then the totalled value). Observational, no split test.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (offer presentation sequencing), age irrelevant, free, editorial. The literal instruction does not survive an ecommerce PDP — Shopify and Amazon both put the price above the fold by default, so it is never withheld — but the underlying claim translates cleanly: the price is not the end of the argument, so bundle framing, free-portion framing, the guarantee and the quantity argument all belong below the price on the page and after the price line in an email, not before it. A concrete ordering rule for a solo operator building PDP sections and A+ blocks at zero cost. Held at 5 because it is a sequencing heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism, it interacts with the information-fatigue rule in marketing-conversion-friction-audit (order by decision-relevance, do not just stack), and at ~54 orders/month the ordering will be judged rather than measured.

### When the offer runs indefinitely and cannot carry a real expiry date, build urgency by attaching the bonus or extra unit to the act of ordering now ('order right now and I'll also send you...') rather than by claiming a deadline. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Conditional phrasing ties the extra value to the action rather than to a calendar, so the reader feels they must act to get everything on the table, while the seller never asserts an expiry that will not actually happen — which is what a permanently-running ad or an evergreen listing cannot honestly do.

**Evidence:** Observation only: Schefren notes that because the commercial ran continuously Billy 'couldn't have a time limit or an expiration date, but the way he described it made you feel that you needed to order now'. No data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (urgency framing), so the 2009 date is irrelevant. It fills a genuine hole in marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing, which currently prescribes only real, honoured deadlines and seasonal re-wraps — but the two surfaces carrying most revenue cannot hold a deadline at all: an Amazon listing (~$1,800/mo) is evergreen and policy-constrained, and an always-on Shopify PDP has no countdown. The honest translation is conditional bonus phrasing on the PDP and in cart/post-purchase copy ('add the refill now and the lip balm ships with it'), with the hard guardrail that the offer must actually be attached to that order rather than implied to be expiring. Capped at 5 because the source's own version leans on manufactured feeling, that drift is exactly what the existing skill's 'the deadline must be genuinely honored' rule prohibits, and at ~54 orders/month the effect will be judged, never measured.

### Close with an unhedged direct command — 'order right now', 'call now' — repeated through the back half of the pitch rather than softening the ask (the veteran pitchmen's 'chill-down'), and pair it with taking the risk off the buyer's shoulders. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Once the pain has been surfaced, the product demonstrated, the alternatives contrasted, the offer sweetened and the risk reversed, the only step left is to tell the reader exactly what to do; a timid or hedged ask leaves a persuaded reader with no instruction to act on and lets momentum dissipate, while the authoritative tone signals the seller's own belief in the product.

**Evidence:** Two phrases pulled from Billy Mays' pitches plus the Zig Ziglar line 'timid salespeople have skinny kids'. Assertion only, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant. Narrowly additive: marketing-email-design-production owns CTA placement and bridging the ask to what was just read, and marketing-conversion-copy-angles owns register and pronouns, but nothing owns the ask being an unhedged imperative repeated after the argument lands — the exact failure mode of a solo operator drafting with an LLM, since brand prose defaults to 'you can learn more here'. Free, editorial, applies to the Shopify PDP CTA, the post-purchase and reorder emails where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided, and Meta primary text on the $30/day giveaway campaign ('enter now' rather than 'buy now'). Capped at 5 because it is a one-line craft habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it is close kin to CTA rules already recorded, it must be scoped away from Amazon A+ content where cart-directing language is policy-constrained, and at ~54 orders/month no version of the sentence will ever be measurably better. The risk-reversal half of the source's pairing is not additive — the guarantee rule in marketing-offer-construction and the urgency rules in marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing already cover it, and bare 'order now' pressure with no expiring thing behind it is the empty version this catalogue has already ruled out.

### Validate an offer on the smallest, cheapest audience that predicts the full result — kill it there if it does not pull, and only then roll out to expensive channels; Schefren's own version is to test every new product to the house email list first, and only if it beats expectations go out to affiliates, PPC and SEO. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Direct response is trackable, so a cheap surface can predict a larger one; testing where the cost of a failure is smallest means most of the money and time gets spent riding winners rather than discovering losers at full price.

**Evidence:** Billy and Sully's cheap predictive TV test markets, Joseph Sugarman testing every ad in the Southwestern edition of the Wall Street Journal, and Schefren's own stated house-list-first sequence. Practitioner anecdotes, no figures.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (test ordering), age irrelevant. Additive on a different axis from the rules already recorded: marketing-awareness-level-messaging's cheap-reversible-first rule orders copy angles (ad text before PDP rebuild), while this orders audiences — validate a new bundle, price or promotion by emailing the owned list before putting any of the $30/day Meta budget or Amazon listing changes behind it. Free, needs no attribution (redemption reads off a discount code), and it is the natural companion to the existing 'run the price escalation on Shopify first, not Amazon' rule, since Amazon changes are slow to unwind on the larger ~$1,800/mo channel. Capped at 5 because it substantially overlaps those two rules, and because the source's stop-rule does not survive the scale-down: at a sub-1,000 subscriber list and ~54 orders/month, 'sales were disappointing, kill it' is a directional judgement on a handful of orders, so treat a flat list test as a reason not to fund it yet, never as proof the offer is dead.

## Rejected

### Engineer instant gratification — shorten the time between purchase and payoff, and make the result visible as fast as possible, because buyers want the magic pill rather than the year-long programme. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Already owned in three places: marketing-conversion-friction-audit carries the time-delay term of the value equation, the published week-by-week results timeline, and the London Underground rule that an unperceived reduction in wait earns nothing so the wait must be mapped and stated; marketing-post-purchase-onboarding carries engineering a visible win as close to the purchase as possible and naming interim benefits that carry the buyer through the ramp. Adopting would restate all three and degrade skill triggering, and RSC cannot actually shorten the transition period the way a coach can shorten a programme.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable principle and directly relevant to transition-period churn, but every actionable form is already recorded elsewhere.

### Find and spotlight what makes the product unique — a 'uniquely superior solution' — and remember that different sells better than merely better, so a difference that is not an improvement still deserves the spotlight. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** marketing-offer-construction owns the per-SKU differentiator audit and the fallback when a SKU has none (compete on faster/less risky/easier). marketing-awareness-level-messaging owns the four-places rule for locating a differentiator (delivery format, complementary ingredient, concentration, stage of intervention) and — for the exact claim that a non-superior difference is still worth leading with — the rule that identification material comes from the object's own appearance, construction and provenance (cardboard tube versus plastic twist-up). Adding this would create near-duplicate guidance across two skills.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable positioning logic but a restatement of three rules already carried across two skills.

### Convey visible enthusiasm and genuine belief in the product — projected energy, fast tempo and short sentences — because if you're not excited about what you sell, nobody will be. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** 'Are you passionate about your product? if not, sell something else' has no testable mechanism, and the one concrete residue — fast tempo, short sentences, energetic register — is already owned by the spoken peer-register rule in marketing-conversion-copy-angles ('write the way people actually talk... read a draft aloud'), which also scopes that register away from Amazon titles and bullets. An 'enthusiasm' variant would degrade triggering, and in body care unbounded enthusiasm pulls directly against the specificity and claim-calibration gates.

**Fit reasoning:** The load-bearing half is motivational with no testable mechanism; the concrete residue is already owned.

### Reveal 'the secret' behind the product — a named mechanism ('the secret is the X27 fiber technology, 27 times more absorbent than cotton') — because in a market that has heard every claim, a new mechanism is what makes the old promises believable again. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Schwartz's original is already mined into marketing-awareness-level-messaging, which owns the five sophistication stages, the three-position mechanism-depth dial, the compare-and-contrast form the mechanism sentence must take, the four places to find a differentiator, and the instruction to stop once the differentiator lands. The one thing added — the literal 'the secret is X' phrasing plus a number — is covered by the specificity rule in marketing-conversion-copy-angles. A fourth mechanism entry would degrade skill triggering.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable principle but wholly duplicative — a secondhand paraphrase of a source already mined in depth.

### Immediately after stating the price, sweeten the deal — add bonus items with a stated dollar value ('as a special bonus we'll also include...'), and/or double or triple the quantity ('order right now and I'll send you a second one absolutely free, just pay shipping and processing') — then total the whole thing against the price so the value dwarfs the cost. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** marketing-offer-construction already owns buy-X-get-Y where the free units outnumber the paid ones (buy four get eight, not buy one get one, with unit price raised so the total holds), the rule that the same economics can be stated as percent off, dollars off, a free portion or a total package price with a value anchor, the named-bundle tier rule, the prefer-adding-good-stuff-over-cutting-price rule, and the '$1 add-on recovers landed cost' variant. The only element not explicitly owned — free plus shipping — is a near-variant of the splinter rule and a poor fit: at a $50.46 AOV with real COGS and postage, 'second stick free, just pay shipping' on a $12 consumable can go cash-negative per order, the exact failure the splinter rule already warns about. Adopting the weaker form would muddy a rule already scored 8/10.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable in principle but almost entirely duplicative of a rule already scored 8 and adopted for this catalogue, and the one non-duplicated variant is economically unsafe here.

### Eliminate the buyer's perceived risk with a money-back guarantee, because remote buyers cannot see, touch or try the product and are otherwise being asked to carry all the risk themselves; refunds will be a small fraction of the incremental sales. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** marketing-offer-construction already owns risk reversal in a more specific and better-calibrated form for this business (name the buyer's single biggest fear, go broad and unconditional at low-ticket B2C, build the cost into the price, place the promise next to add-to-cart rather than in the footer), and marketing-conversion-friction-audit owns making return and guarantee terms visible on the page. Nothing here is additive at a $50.46 AOV.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable-principle class (offer construction), age irrelevant — rejected for duplication, not staleness.

### Choose products with mass appeal — strip out niching features or add features that widen the prospect universe, and apply the 'person on the street test': of ten random passers-by, how many are potential prospects? — 2/10

**Rejected because:** The product-modification half has no honest translation: marketing-offer-construction explicitly gates catalogue expansion for a solo operator at $2,700/mo ('don't add products, add ways to offer the products you already have'), and reformulating a deodorant for broader appeal is exactly the move that rule forbids. The messaging half duplicates marketing-awareness-level-messaging, which already carries the shared-centre rule and the headroom rule — and also carries the deliberate counterweight that a small advertiser should take a motive-defined splinter rather than the whole market, which is the opposite of this advice and better suited to a brand with a sub-1,000 list.

**Fit reasoning:** Two failures rather than a timing problem: the product-modification half is forbidden by an existing rule, and the messaging half is already owned twice over.

### Build high perceived value into the pitch by stating the value against the price — 'a $60 value, all for only $19.99'. — 2/10
