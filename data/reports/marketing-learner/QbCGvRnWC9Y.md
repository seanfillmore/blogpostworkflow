## Adopted

### Build ads on a three-part formula: the replaceable thing the buyer uses now, plus a specific situation, plus why this product is the better replacement in that situation. Then run the same competitor-product comparison across about six different situations as an array of ads. — 8/10

**Why it works:** 'X is better than Y' is abstract. 'Y in the gym sucks, X is better in the gym' gives the viewer a concrete use case they recognise from their own life. Listing situations multiplies distinct, believable entry points from one product truth.

**Evidence:** Claimed to have worked at Ultra, Laundry Sauce and Tube Science. Illustrated with 'nicotine in the gym sucks, Ultra is better in the gym'. No figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. Existing skills already hold the one-item routine swap and the contrast against the product class the buyer owns. What is new is the situation as a required third part, plus the drill of listing about six situations to produce an ad array. Paid front ends should use the skin line, which carries $15-25 contribution. Example situations: drugstore lotion that leaves you sticky after a hot shower, hands in winter, after shaving. Deodorant situations (gym, summer commute) belong in organic video and retention or AOV email rather than cold paid, because deodorant carries only $2-5 after shipping. Each situation is a self-filmed talking head today, then a static or flexible-ad variant in the live test campaign.

### Build a post-purchase sequence that closes the loop on the reason the customer bought ('here's how to use it for X', then 'how did it go for X'), but deliberately add other use cases, because a sequence tied only to the acquisition use case hurts retention. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Reconnecting with the original purchase reason gets the customer to a first result. A buyer with only one use case stops buying when that situation ends (for example, they stop going to the gym). A second, everyday use case keeps the habit alive.

**Evidence:** Ultra found that funnels too specific to the pre-workout use case hurt retention, and that adding a secondary use case ('take it after lunch') made customers stickier. Stated as an observed finding, no numbers.

**Fit:** Retention is the binding constraint: repeat customers drive about 45-52% of revenue, and the low-margin deodorant, soap, toothpaste and lip balm lines are meant to be harvested through retention. The single-use-case trap is not in marketing-post-purchase-onboarding. Tag Klaviyo profiles by acquisition angle or landing page, send use-case-matched onboarding, and deliberately introduce a second daily situation: a post-gym deodorant buyer also hears about travel and all-day office wear; a hand-cream buyer also hears about cuticles and post-shower use. Runnable today by one person.

### Find a theme that recurs consistently in customer reviews but appears nowhere in the brand's own messaging, then rebuild the positioning and ad messaging around it (Laundry Sauce: 'I used to hate doing laundry, now I love it'). — 7/10

**Why it works:** Customers have already told you what they want the product to do for them. A recurring theme that marketing has never used is a proven desire with no competing message. Building the message back from it makes ads sound like the buyer's own inner voice.

**Evidence:** Laundry Sauce reframed its brand mission and messaging around 'loving laundry' after the theme was spotted in reviews. Presented as a pattern seen at Tube Science, Laundry Sauce and Ultra. No numbers given.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. It is the inverse of the existing evidence-map audit: that audit strikes claims customers never make, while this surfaces what customers repeatedly say that RSC never claims. RSC has an 18-22.5% repeat rate, and repeat buyers carry about 45-52% of revenue, so its reviews likely hold reasons people come back that are missing from the PDP, Amazon bullets and Meta primary text. Conversion rate is the binding constraint, and this feeds it directly. One person with a review export can run it today. Translate it to self-filmed short-form hooks first, then primary text, email subjects and Amazon bullets.

**Target skill:** `marketing-creative-evidence-map` (edit)

### Treat creators as crowd-sourced creative strategists: give them product and an array of talking points, review their output daily, and pull out the new nicknames, hook openers and phrasings that work so the founder can reshoot them himself. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Many creators improvising on a brief produce far more hook and naming variants than one strategist could. Daily review surfaces the winners before launch, and refilming them in-house spreads the proven lines across every channel.

**Evidence:** Ultra has 50+ creators live on Meta plus many TikTok Shop affiliates, reviews them for 2+ hours daily, and its co-founder reshoots winning ideas weekly.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. RSC already runs Trybe creator UGC in its live creative-test campaign, so the scaled-down version runs today. Review every Trybe delivery as a hook source, log each new nickname or opener, and refilm the strongest himself for organic short-form, then statics, primary text and email subjects. The 50-creator roster is a team-stage version, but mining creators already delivering is solo work. Existing skills cover sourcing, briefing and routing, not mining creator output for language.

### Treat a proven messaging angle as a funnel, not just an ad concept. When customer testimonials reveal a real use case, make more ads on it and build a dedicated hybrid 'intro offer' landing page per angle that makes the visitor read a short use-case explanation before reaching the buy box. — 7/10 · parked until `cro`

**Why it works:** The visitor gets more reasons to buy, specific to the situation the ad promised, before the buy box appears. The page stays congruent with the angle instead of dropping a use-case-specific click onto a generic PDP. The host also claims, unverified, that educated buyers are stickier.

**Evidence:** The 'pre-workout alternative' use case surfaced in testimonials. Ultra built an angle-specific landing page that 'helped a lot', sent about 70% of paid traffic to such pages in spring, and scaled the approach to several messaging funnels. The creator has no retention data for these pages.

**Fit:** Conversion rate is the binding constraint and the CRO gate is open. One person can build an angle-specific Shopify page today. When reviews show a recurring use case (aluminium-free deodorant for gym sweat, lotion for winter hand-cracking), that use case gets its own Meta ads and its own page, with a short use-case block above the lotion or set offer. This differs from the existing pre-sell advertorial claim because it is one page per angle with education before the buy box, not a separate advertorial step. It also stays within the two-element first funnel: ad → one page. Reading whether it beats the PDP will be slow at ~16 Shopify orders/month, but that does not block the build.

### Pack a paid ad with selling points, almost too many, leading with the strongest and most emotionally resonant reasons. The classic vehicle is the 'N reasons to try this product' video, shot across several formats (talking head, podcast studio). — 7/10

**Why it works:** If the ad alone makes the viewer want the product badly enough, the landing page and purchase hoops matter less. A numbered list holds the viewer to the end, even through points they disagree with, because disagreement creates engagement. Paid tolerates far more density than organic.

**Evidence:** Tube Science used it to control the outcome when it had no control over clients' landing pages; 'every performance ad copied' the format. Assertion beyond that.

**Fit:** Runnable today on his own phone: a self-filmed 'five reasons I switched to aluminium-free deodorant' or 'five reasons this lotion replaced my drugstore one' for Reels and TikTok, then the same list as Meta primary text, email body and Amazon bullets. Three things are new beyond the existing numbered-list scaffolds: the named 'N reasons to try' paid format, the rule that a paid cut should be denser than the organic cut, and ordering reasons by emotional strength. To reconcile with body-structure's 'carry one dominant idea', keep one dominant buying motive and make the N reasons supporting proof under it. Conversion rate is the binding constraint, and an ad that pre-sells hard addresses it directly.

**Target skill:** `marketing-copy-hooks-and-formats` (edit)

### Withhold the product until the end with a 'design your dream product' hook: 'if you were creating the perfect X today, here are the five things it would need', list the five, then reveal the product. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The viewer is sold on the benefits and the effect before they know what is being sold. That lowers ad resistance and protects brand feel, and the list holds them to the reveal.

**Evidence:** Assertion; described as 'very paid performance'.

**Fit:** This fits RSC better than it fit the guest. Sean actually formulated the products, so the reveal is honest: 'If I were designing the perfect natural deodorant from scratch, it would need these five things… so I made it.' The existing 'keep the product out of the hook' claim covers organic captions generally; the new gambit is the dream-spec list and the end reveal. Runnable today as a self-filmed Reel, then as Meta video and primary text.

### When even a single customer describes using the product for a reason nobody else mentions, treat it as a candidate new angle and amplify it in ads on their behalf. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A real but unvoiced use case is a reason to buy that no competitor is advertising. Stating it in an ad 'makes it real' for everyone who shares that situation but never connected it to the product.

**Evidence:** Assertion only. Framed as the core lesson from Tube Science: 'that's a whole new unlock'.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, and a different angle generator from counted-utterance mining. The outlier review should be only the angle's premise, not fabricated proof, which keeps the existing traceability rule intact. Validate it first through the cheap, reversible surface, per the existing doubtful-angle rule: a self-filmed hook on RSC's own social channels, then a static or flexible-ad variant. Runnable today by one person.

### 'Scale into credibility': validate a message fast and cheaply in a low-credibility format first. Once it works, re-run the same proven message through a more credible messenger: a more authoritative voice in the same format, or placement on real podcasts. — 6/10 · parked until `team`

**Why it works:** Cheap tests de-risk the message. Credibility is then added to a message already known to convert, rather than spent on untested ones.

**Evidence:** Ultra moved from fake podcast ads to real podcast ads and more credible voices. Assertion only.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. The existing credibility skill says to raise the authority behind a message; this adds the order of operations: earn the message cheaply, then pay for credibility only on proven winners. That fits a solo operator's budget logic. Carrying it out needs a credentialed person on camera (an esthetician or dermatologist) or paid podcast placements, so it is parked behind team at full merit. Trigger: a message that has won on RSC's own creative and Trybe UGC, plus budget for a credentialed voice or a host read.

### Let the concept decide static versus video. Test on statics by default, but when a concept depends on situational nuance a single frame cannot carry, go straight to a talking-head video in the real setting. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A static can falsely kill a concept whose payoff needs context. In those cases a talking head in the actual setting is more likely to land the message on the first attempt.

**Evidence:** Ultra's account is about 50/50 static and video. Tennis/Adderall example: earlier tennis statics never took off, and the plan is a talking-head video on a court.

**Fit:** Platform-mechanics-adjacent, but the principle is durable. It is a stated exception to the existing 'start testing with statics' rule, and it stops a nuanced situational angle from being retired on a static that could never carry it, which is the failure Step 9 of pattern analysis warns about. He films talking heads himself, so this is runnable today, and the video doubles as organic short-form.

### Set a brand-specific benchmark for creative return: attributed revenue per concept divided by the cost to produce it (for example 5x or 10x). Measure every creative source against it, cut the sources that fall below, and keep raising the bar. — 6/10 · parked until `scale`

**Why it works:** Production cost differs widely by source. A per-concept, per-source return-on-production-cost number turns creative sourcing into a comparable ROI decision. Weak sources get cut, budget concentrates on producers who beat the benchmark, and the bar can tighten because some sources already beat it.

**Evidence:** Ultra spent 2-3 months running many agencies plus an internal team to find its benchmark, cut the agencies below it, and keeps the ones that beat it; some deliver well above it.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. No existing claim measures creative production cost against the return each concept produces; routing scripts to past winners is about attribution, not source economics. RSC has two sources today: self-produced creative and paid Trybe UGC. A readable per-source comparison needs enough attributed purchases per concept and source, which ~16 Shopify orders/month does not yet supply. That is a volume gate, so it is parked behind scale at full merit. Trigger: the creative-test campaign graduating winners to the $100/day scaling campaign with enough purchases per source to separate signal from noise. Until then, log production cost per concept next to results so the benchmark can be computed once volume arrives.

**Target skill:** `marketing-paid-media-measurement` (edit)

### To break into a segment where your product has no obvious substitute, research what that segment actually uses or relies on in the relevant situation (for example, Reddit threads on what pro tennis players take before a match), then position the product as the replacement for that. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Replacement framing needs an incumbent. If the segment does not use the obvious competitor, find the thing it does use and build the situational swap against that.

**Evidence:** Tennis example: pouches had no way in, tennis statics failed, and Reddit research surfaced Adderall as the incumbent. Not yet run.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. It extends the replacement formula to segments where the incumbent is not obvious. For RSC, that could mean finding what nurses, runners or tattooed buyers actually put on their skin. One person can do the research today from Reddit and reviews, then test it organically or in the existing creative-test campaign. The idea is untested even at the source.

### When buying outside creative production, hire agencies that specialise in one specific format (street interviews, podcast-style ads with hired actors and built sets) rather than generalists. — 5/10 · parked until `team`

**Why it works:** A format specialist already has an optimised production pipeline for that one format. You buy a proven format output rather than a general creative pitch.

**Evidence:** Knox's own preference. Street Talk and Matrix Media mentioned as examples.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. It adds a selection criterion the existing ten-point agency screen lacks: buy the format, not the agency. Carrying it out means paying an agency, so it is parked behind team at full merit. Trigger: a messaging angle that has won in his self-shot version and would scale in a format he cannot stage alone, such as street interviews or multi-actor podcast sets.

### Give every shopper who clicks an affiliate's or partner's link a unique single-use discount code instead of one shared code, so codes cannot leak to coupon sites and pay commission on sales the partner did not drive. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Shared codes spread to Honey, coupon sites and Reddit. Buyers who would have paid full price then get a discount, and the partner is credited for the sale. Single-use codes cannot be reused.

**Evidence:** Sponsor read: Social Snowball claims a client recovered $684K in five months. This is a paid endorsement, so the evidence is weak.

**Fit:** The leakage mechanism is real and becomes material once RSC hands codes to creators, partners or giveaway entrants. The sponsor's tool is not needed: Shopify's free bulk unique-code generation does the same job today. Any code given to Trybe creators, partners or giveaway lists should be unique and single-use. The score reflects modest merit and sponsor-only evidence.

### Run a separate burner or secondary social account for performance-style, product-benefit content, so the main brand page keeps its lifestyle aesthetic while sales-heavy angles are tested. — 4/10

**Why it works:** The main page stays a coherent brand 'world'. The secondary account lets the algorithm find people who respond to direct product-benefit messaging without diluting the main feed.

**Evidence:** Assertion only.

**Fit:** The mechanism is real and one person can run it, but merit for RSC is limited. RSC is not protecting a lifestyle-aesthetic feed, and a second account splits a small audience and doubles the posting load. It is most useful as a sandbox for blunt benefit-led hooks that would feel off-brand on the main account.
