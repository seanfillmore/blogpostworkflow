## Adopted

### When the prospect knows the product but does not yet want it, the headline has one of six specific jobs: reinforce the desire, sharpen his image of how the product satisfies it, extend where and when it satisfies it, introduce new proof or documentation, announce a new mechanism, or change the image of the mechanism so the product escapes comparison with everything else claiming the same thing. — 6/10

**Why it works:** At this awareness level the reader needs no education about the problem or the category, so the only remaining variables are how vividly he pictures the satisfaction, how widely he believes it applies, how much he believes it at all, and whether he sees your version as comparable to the others. Naming the six moves turns 'write a better headline' into a choice among six concrete jobs.

**Evidence:** Enumerated list of six tasks presented as the options facing a headline at this awareness level. Assertion only, no examples worked through, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (positioning), so the 1966 date is irrelevant. Close match for this catalogue's actual reader: virtually every natural-deodorant prospect already knows aluminium-free sticks exist and most have tried one that quit by noon, so they are product-aware and unconvinced. The existing awareness skill says match the message to that level but never says what a message at that level can do — this supplies six named jobs, the generator needed for two meaningfully different primary texts and two headlines inside the 3-2-2 flexible ad at $30/day, plus PDP hero variants and Amazon bullets. Free, purely editorial. Held at 6 because two of the six are already owned by the sophistication ladder and the compare-and-contrast mechanism rule, 'introduce new proof' is owned by marketing-review-mining, and at ~54 orders/month the choice will be judged rather than measured.

### The physical product does not sell, but the physical detail has exactly four jobs — justifying the price, proving the product can do what you claim, assuring the performance will continue over time, and sharpening the reader's mental picture of that performance — so keep spec copy only where it is doing one of those four. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Spec detail is not persuasive on its own and reads as documentation, but each of these four jobs is a real objection the performance claim cannot answer by itself: why it costs this much, why it is believable, why it will keep working, and what it will actually feel like. Naming the four jobs gives a test for whether a given ingredient, material or construction fact earns its place on the page.

**Evidence:** Enumerated four-item list stated as the use of the physical product. Assertion only, no example, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy structure), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. Closes a real gap next to the performance inventory already recorded: that rule says what the product does and which desire it maps to, but nothing says where the ingredient panel, the cardboard tube, the small-batch provenance and the made-in-USA fact belong or why. Each has an honest slot here — shea and arrowroot justify $12 against a supermarket stick, the baking-soda-free formulation proves the no-burn claim, the format assures it holds through a full shift, texture description sharpens the picture. It is also the constructive counterpart to the 'cut the root-cause education block' rule. Held at 6 because it is a placement test rather than a revenue mechanism, it partly overlaps the identification-material audit already recorded, and every spec used as proof must clear the body-care claim rules.

### Once you have the claim, generate headline variants by holding the claim fixed and running it through a checklist of verbalization transforms — measure its size, measure its speed, compare it, metaphorize it, sensitize it, demonstrate it, dramatize it, state it as a paradox, remove a limitation from it, state it as a question, offer information about how to accomplish it, tie authority to it, put it before-and-after, name the problem it solves, turn it into a case history, or warn about the pitfall of not acting. — 6/10

**Why it works:** The claim decides whether the ad can work at all; the verbalization decides whether the claim gets read. Because the transforms are mechanical, they turn 'write another headline' from a blank-page problem into a checklist, and each transform binds a different secondary image to the same underlying claim — so you get variety of expression without drifting onto a different, weaker claim.

**Evidence:** A list of roughly two dozen transforms, each with one or two real 1950s–60s headlines as examples ('I am 61 pounds lighter', 'Six times whiter washes!', 'Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery!', 'Who else wants a whiter wash, with no hard work?'). Illustrative only, no performance data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (claim construction), age irrelevant, free and purely editorial. Directly useful this week: the 3-2-2 flexible ad at $30/day needs two meaningfully different headlines and two primary texts, and this generates them from one already-chosen claim — which also makes them a real comparison rather than two rewordings. Same use for email subject lines and Amazon bullet leads. The claim/expression split is the additive part: the existing skill owns many individual transforms but nothing owns the discipline of fixing the claim and varying only its expression. Held at 6 because that overlap is substantial and because the register of the examples is a live hazard — 'melts away ugly fat' is exactly the phrasing that becomes an unsupportable efficacy claim in body care, so every output must clear the specificity and body-care claim rules.

### Carry one dominant idea through the whole piece and intensify it by re-presenting it in successive fresh settings rather than repeating the same phrasing — and drop any additional perspective that is not different or dramatic enough to renew interest. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A vague desire has no purchase-forcing power; concrete images give it weight, and the reader leaves with one dominant image no matter how many you present, so each additional legitimate setting reinforces rather than repeats, building emotional weight and making the price feel smaller. Repeating the same phraseology bores a sophisticated reader, so a fresh perspective is the only way to say the same thing twice; a perspective that is not genuinely fresh adds reading cost with no return.

**Evidence:** Assertion framed as the opening of the 'thirteen ways to strengthen desire' section, with a stated stopping rule ('as long as each additional fresh perspective continues to build the dominant desire... use it'). No test data, no worked examples.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy structure/craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Nothing here governs how a long PDP body, an Amazon A+ sequence or a multi-block email holds one idea across several sections. Clean form for this catalogue: one promise — you stop checking your shirt — carried through four distinct settings (the 3pm meeting, the twelve-hour shift, the gym bag on Friday, the hug at the door), each drawn verbatim from the review corpus rather than invented. The stopping rule is the load-bearing half for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which will happily generate ten near-identical restatements. The price-salience argument is directly relevant to a $12 stick sitting next to Native. Held at 6 because it overlaps the specificity rule, transformation-first ordering and the re-entry rule, it must be bounded by the three-job length test, and it will never be measurable at ~54 orders/month.

### Demonstrate the product verbally in stages: describe the product or result bluntly first, then put the product in action showing how it works, then put the reader inside that scene on the first day of ownership, then stretch the same demonstration out across weeks and months. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A static description gives the reader nothing to picture themselves doing. Showing the product working, then placing the reader inside the demonstration, converts an abstract benefit into a rehearsed experience; extending it over time turns a single result into a continuous flow of benefits the reader can see themselves receiving.

**Evidence:** Numbered process instruction (items 1–5 of the intensification list). No worked example in this excerpt, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, editorial. The time-extension step maps unusually well here because the category's defining experience is a timeline rather than a moment: day one, week two of the transition, month three. The friction-audit skill already owns the dated week-by-week timeline as an artifact; this is the second-person narrative version for the PDP hero section and post-purchase emails — 'the first morning you use it… by the second week…' — which is where the 18–22.5% repeat rate is decided. Held at 6 for that partial overlap and because every stage must stay within what the product actually does under the body-care claim rules.

### Order your claims by how readily each will be believed, not by how strong each is — open with a statement the reader accepts immediately, build a chain of successive acceptances, and delay the biggest claim until the groundwork makes it credible. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Every statement's power comes from two sources: its own content and the preparation made for it. A claim landing before the reader is ready is disbelieved, and disbelief contaminates everything after it, so the strongest claim is not always the strongest headline. One fully-believed promise outsells ten partially-believed ones, so sequencing to protect belief is worth more than intensifying the claim itself.

**Evidence:** Assertion, with four named devices for carrying acceptance forward into a new statement (paragraph parallelism, lead 'and', 'most important' tie-ins, echoing an earlier subhead). No test data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (persuasion sequencing), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Additive next to what is recorded: claim-calibration governs how strong a claim should be, the agreement-opener governs the first line, the price-position rule governs where price sits — but nothing governs the running order of the claims. Concrete form for this catalogue, where the hardest claim to land is 'a natural deodorant that actually holds' against a burned market: state the accepted facts first (baking soda is what burns, the transition is real), then the claim. Applies to PDP body copy, A+ blocks and non-buyer nurture emails. Held at 6 because it partly overlaps the market-image bridge rule, and the ordering will be judged, never measured.

### Read your own draft as the prospect and answer, at the exact point each arises, the three demands a reader keeps making: 'tell me more' (more information, image, desire), 'oh yeah, who says so?' (proof), and 'how does it work?' (mechanism). — 6/10

**Why it works:** A reader runs a silent commentary while reading and progresses through predictable reactions; each unanswered demand is where they stop, regardless of what the rest of the piece contains. Because the three demands fire at different moments, answering them where they occur — rather than dumping all proof at the end or all mechanism at the top — keeps the reader moving, and knowing which demand a passage triggers tells you whether the next block supplies desire, proof, or mechanism.

**Evidence:** Assertion only. The three demand classes are enumerated with the reader's implied question for each, and the writer is told to develop 'a foolproof sensitivity' to when they arrive. No worked examples, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy craft and draft review), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial, runnable as a literal review pass over PDP body copy, Amazon A+ blocks, non-buyer nurture emails and the Meta primary text on the live $30/day campaign. Additive to the audits already recorded: the mechanism-depth dial says how much mechanism to supply and the five-needs audit checks emotional coverage, but nothing owns whether proof and mechanism appear at the moment the reader silently asks for them. That matters because a solo operator drafting with an LLM gets a tidy features → mechanism → review-block structure rather than a piece that answers each demand in place. Held at 6 because it is a review discipline rather than a revenue mechanism, the source offers assertion only, and no repositioned proof block will be measurably better at ~54 orders/month. Note the proof answer is constrained: with dozens rather than thousands of reviews, 'who says so' is often answered by the extreme-case customer account rule.

### Escape the hard-sell stereotype with understatement: simplicity, few colour words, fewer adjectives than the reader expects, no superlatives, and short sentences that fall rather than rise in tone. — 6/10

**Why it works:** The reader has a trained ear for sales language, and superlatives and stacked adjectives trip it, causing the whole piece to be discounted. Copy pitched below the expected intensity reads as reporting rather than selling, so the claims inside it are evaluated on their content instead of being pre-filtered as hype.

**Evidence:** Assertion only, given as a list of stylistic constraints. No examples, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy register), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial, and a better fit than the source could have known: a body-care catalogue is already gated by the specificity and claim rules, and 'no superlatives, fewer adjectives' is the cheapest mechanical way to keep an LLM draft from producing unsupportable efficacy language — the failure mode is compliance as well as tone. Pairs directly with claim-calibration. Additive next to the spoken peer register rule, which governs conversational tone but says nothing about intensity — a draft can be chatty and still stacked with superlatives. Applies to PDP body copy, Amazon bullets, post-purchase emails and live Meta primary text. Held at 6 because it is a craft constraint judged at draft review, never measured at ~54 orders/month. Scope limit: the Amazon title still needs its keyword and claim terms stated plainly.

### Do not write headlines by taking a successful one, pulling out the original product name and substituting your own — the result is an echo ad that reminds the reader of the other product. — 5/10

**Why it works:** A headline's power comes from a unique product-market-timing relationship, not from its words. Swapping the product name keeps the words and discards the relationship, so a reader who has seen the original recognises it and the ad works for the competitor rather than for you.

**Evidence:** Assertion, presented as the lowest of three levels of creativity, with the concession that copying a similar product in the same market at the same time can work. No examples, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, and it lands on an activity this business actively runs: the teardown skill instructs pulling Native, Dr. Squatch, Every Man Jack and Harry's ads from the Ad Library and briefing an LLM off a survivor — and an LLM handed a source text drifts toward its wording unless stopped. That skill already prohibits lifting phrasing on legal and credibility grounds; the additive part is a performance reason, a more persuasive stop for a solo operator in a hurry. Capped at 5 because it is a narrow refinement of an existing prohibition, it is a decision not to act, and nothing about it is measurable at ~54 orders/month.

### A prevention claim only sells when the imagined sufferer is someone the buyer cares about rather than the buyer himself — decay-preventing toothpaste sold when the ads focused the decay on the children, not the parent, which is also why life insurance sells. — 5/10

**Why it works:** People will not act on a harm they might suffer themselves at some indefinite future point, but they will readily imagine that harm happening to a child or spouse, and the protective motive is felt now even though the harm is not. So a prevention benefit that is inert when pointed at the buyer becomes urgent when pointed at a dependent.

**Evidence:** Two examples asserted — decay-preventing toothpaste ads aimed at children, and life insurance. No figures, no test.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (desire selection), age irrelevant, free and editorial. Additive because it is a stated exception to a rule already recorded: the felt-today gate keeps aluminium-free out of the hero precisely because it is prevention of something later, and this names the one configuration where a prevention claim carries urgency — a parent choosing an aluminium-free stick for a teenager, or natural oral care for a child. Held at 5 because it is unclear the 12 SKUs address that buyer, the shared-center rule argues against splintering a demographic slice, and any health-flavoured prevention claim aimed at children must clear the body-care claim rules — so the usable version is 'what you put on your kid's skin' as ingredient transparency, never an implied health warning. Test as an email subject line or Meta primary text first.

### Copy length is determined by three jobs, not by a word target: how much you need to build desire to its greatest strength, how much you need to make the buyer feel comfortable and complimented owning the product, and how much you need to make him believe what you said. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Desires, identifications and beliefs are separate mental dimensions, so a page that has finished one but not the others is under-length regardless of its word count, and a page repeating work already done on one dimension is over-length regardless of how short it is.

**Evidence:** Assertion with the three-dimension framework laid out. No worked example, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy structure), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Supplies a test the friction audit lacks: that skill says to reduce information fatigue and order by decision-relevance but never says which sections are load-bearing. This gives three questions to run against a PDP or A+ stack — is desire built with concrete scenes, is identification served, is belief served — and anything serving none of the three gets collapsed. Capped at 5 because it is a length heuristic rather than a revenue mechanism, it overlaps the decision-relevance ordering already recorded, and page length will be judged rather than measured at ~54 orders/month.

### Interlace each new promise with logical connective language — 'the reason for this is', 'here's why', 'simply', 'therefore', 'as an example' — and use syllogistic chains so each claim reads as following inevitably from what was already proved. — 5/10

**Why it works:** The connective signals to the reader that the new statement is a consequence of material they have already accepted rather than a fresh assertion requiring fresh scrutiny, so belief carries forward instead of resetting at each claim.

**Evidence:** A list of connective phrases plus one syllogistic example ('the larger the spark, the more powerful the explosion; the more powerful the explosion, the more power you get from your gasoline'). Assertion only.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (copy craft), age irrelevant, free, seconds to apply. It is the sentence-level implementation of the claim-ordering rule — that one decides sequence, this decides the joins — and it is a real catch for a solo operator drafting with an LLM, which tends to produce lists of independent assertions rather than an argument. Homes: PDP body copy and the mechanism paragraph on the baking-soda-free deodorant, where 'here's why' is exactly the transition into the compare-and-contrast sentence. Capped at 5 because it is micro-craft inside a rule already recorded, and the connectives must not bridge into a claim the formulation cannot support — a logical-sounding chain is not evidence.

### Close by summarizing — a bare condensed catalog of every performance, benefit and application with no description or elaboration — and turn the guarantee into the climax, restating it against each individual claim ('this must do X, or your money back'). — 5/10

**Why it works:** By the close the reader has already had each point dramatized, so a stripped list re-presents the whole case in one glance without costing reading time. Attaching the guarantee to each named performance converts a general reassurance into a specific, repeated promise, so the last thing the reader holds is the full set of claims with the risk removed from each one.

**Evidence:** Named as the two conventional summary devices, with the repeated-guarantee construction written out. Assertion, no data.

### Cut a price objection by redefinition rather than by discounting — switch the comparison standard so the price is measured against something more expensive than the products it is normally compared with. — 5/10

**Why it works:** A price only 'costs too much' relative to the class it is being compared against. Moving the comparison to a different, costlier standard changes the denominator, so the same number reads as smaller without the seller giving up any margin.

**Evidence:** Stated as one of three redefinition moves, with the reasoning that the price seems high only because it is being ranked against other products in the same field. No worked example, no data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (pricing psychology), age irrelevant, free, editorial. Additive next to marketing-offer-construction, which owns how to state savings, where price sits, and how to step a price upward — but not re-anchoring the comparison itself. One of the few price levers costing no margin, which matters at a $50.46 AOV with real COGS and postage. Honest forms: a stick priced against the shirts it saves, or a multipack priced per day of use. Capped at 5 because the anchor must be truthful, an implied dermatology comparison drifts toward a medical claim, and Native and Dr. Squatch set a visible in-category price the buyer re-anchors to on Amazon anyway.

### Remove structural buying objections by redefining the product before the prospect voices them: simplify anything that looks complicated or hard to use, and escalate a product whose appeal looks too narrow by showing the many everyday situations it pays off in. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Some resistance comes from the product's perceived category rather than from any specific doubt — it looks like work, or it looks like it only matters occasionally. Arguing against that impression fails; restating what the product *is* (simple, or broadly useful) removes the roadblock before the prospect has articulated it.

**Evidence:** Stated as two of three named drawback categories with prescribed fixes. No examples worked through in this excerpt, no data.

### Attack the alternatives in an alternating 'bad… good… bad… good' rhythm — name what competing products do badly, immediately follow with what yours does instead, and choose weak-sounding words for their side and powerful ones for yours. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Concentration is not just criticism: pointing out a weakness and then, in the same breath, proving your product delivers the same want without it makes the reader draw the superiority conclusion themselves. Alternating the two keeps each contrast adjacent so the comparison is unavoidable, and deliberate word selection widens the felt gap without adding a new claim.

**Evidence:** Assertion with one illustrative structure ('competitor's product does this badly… mine however does this') and a compressed single-phrase example, 'SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY'. No test data.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (positioning), age irrelevant, free, purely editorial. The additive part is narrow but real: the existing contrast rule supplies four axes and the compare-and-contrast mechanism sentence supplies a one-line form — neither owns the alternating rhythm or the contrastive-vocabulary instruction. Natural fit for a PDP section or A+ block where 'baking soda burns / ours does not' is the whole argument. Capped at 5 because it overlaps three existing rules and because the hard constraint here inverts the source: the target must stay the product class the buyer already owns, never a named competitor, and every 'bad' half must be a defensible formulation fact rather than a chosen adjective.

### Join two claims or emotional images into a single linked statement so each amplifies the other, rather than making them separately — Avis's 'we try harder' becomes far stronger when welded to 'we're only second'. — 5/10

**Why it works:** A benefit claim on its own is asserted and gets discounted; a limitation on its own is just a limitation. Fused, the limitation supplies the reason the benefit must be true, so the pair carries far more weight than either half and the reader assembles the conclusion themselves.

**Evidence:** The Avis campaign cited as the illustration, with the claim that the right pairing can have ten times the impact of either image alone. Single historical example, no figures, and the multiplier is asserted.
