# How to Make Better Offers and Lead Magnets

**Creator:** Hormozi Highlights  
**Video:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7qY7gBMWOB4  
**Published:** 2026-02-22  
**Inferred era cues:** References his Money Models book being out, in-person L1/L2/L3 workshops in Vegas, a 'hooks playbook' and 'goated ads playbook', and running ads to a Scaling Roadmap lead magnet. Published 2026-02-22. No ad-platform mechanics, placement names, or attribution windows that could date the material.  

A whiteboard-style troubleshooting session on two things: how to de-commoditize an offer, and how to build a front-end lead magnet worth opting in for. On offers, Hormozi argues you must win on at least one of three value vectors — speed, risk, or ease — and fund the premium with price rather than margin; he then layers on 'attraction offer' wrappers (giveaway, win-your-money-back, decoy, buy-X-get-Y-free where the free units outnumber the paid ones). On lead magnets, he contrasts direct-purchase ads (higher immediate ROAS, tiny addressable market) with lead-magnet ads (higher long-horizon ROAS, far more reach), then gives three lead-magnet archetypes — reveal a problem, trial of the solution, one step of a multi-step 'splinter' offer — and insists discounts be deep enough (80–90%) to actually change behavior. Almost everything here is durable offer-construction and pricing logic; the only platform-specific content is an anecdote about his own ad account.

Found 14 tactics: 9 adopted, 5 rejected.

## Adopted

### Use buy-X-get-Y-free where the free units outnumber the paid ones (buy four get eight, not buy one get one), and raise the unit price so the total stays where you need it. — 8/10

**Why it works:** The headline ratio reads as an extraordinary deal and changes behavior, while the arithmetic keeps total revenue per transaction intact — you are repackaging the same total price into a structure that feels radically more generous.

**Evidence:** Worked arithmetic example ($100/mo × 12 = $1,200 restructured as buy-four-get-eight at $300 each); assertion, no performance data. Includes a caveat to check regional advertising law.

**Fit:** Durable pricing logic, no decay. This is the single best-fit tactic in the video: deodorant is a consumable, the catalog is 12 SKUs, AOV is $50.46, and repeat purchase is the binding constraint. A multi-unit or mixed-SKU pack sold at an adjusted unit price simultaneously lifts AOV and forward-buys months of supply, which suppresses churn without needing a single new visitor. Executable solo on Shopify today and as a multipack ASIN on Amazon.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### If your category is easy for buyers to comparison-shop, win on at least one of three value vectors — faster, less risky, or easier — and hammer that one rather than trying to do all three. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Commoditization happens when nothing distinguishes you on the dimensions a buyer can actually feel. Speed, risk, and ease are the three axes buyers price against across every category, so owning one converts you from an interchangeable option into a premium one.

**Evidence:** Assertion from consulting practice across home services (roofing, concrete, kitchen remodeling); no numbers.

**Fit:** Durable principle class — offer construction, age irrelevant. Natural deodorant is one of the most comparison-shopped categories on Amazon, and Offer/AOV is an explicit stage in the gated sequence, reachable without spend. Two of the three vectors are solo-executable at zero cost: risk (a transition-period guarantee) and ease (refill/bundle/subscription that removes decisions across 12 SKUs). Not higher because speed is essentially out of a solo operator's hands when fulfillment runs through FBA and Shopify.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (create)

### Reverse risk by naming the buyer's single biggest fear and guaranteeing, warrantying, or literally insuring against it — then build that cost into the price. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The unspoken fear is what stops the purchase; explicitly covering it removes the reason to hesitate, and the actual cost of covering it is usually far lower than sellers assume because most buyers never claim.

**Evidence:** Practitioner claim that he has implemented insurance-backed guarantees with 'plenty of businesses'; no figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. The biggest fear in natural deodorant is precise and well-known — 'it won't work and I'll find out in public during the transition period.' A stated 30-day guarantee costs nothing to write, sits on the PDP and in the Amazon listing, and is pure CRO/Offer-stage work with no spend or traffic required. Redemption cost is bounded at a $50.46 AOV. Capped at 7 because Amazon's own return policy partly duplicates the promise on the channel producing ~$1,800/mo.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Fund the premium (speed, guarantee, white-glove ease) by raising your price to reflect it, not by absorbing it into margin — a de-commoditized product can carry a higher price. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Once you are no longer directly comparable, the price anchor moves with you; buyers who value the vector will pay for it, so the added cost is recovered at the point of sale rather than eaten.

**Evidence:** Assertion, plus the fallback logic that if the market won't pay, you move up market.

**Fit:** Durable pricing logic, age irrelevant, and revenue is the prime directive — with roughly 54 orders/month, price is one of only two multiplicands available without new traffic. Scored mid rather than high because order volume this low makes a clean price test slow to read, so it should be applied as the funding mechanism for a specific added guarantee or bundle rather than as a standalone across-the-board increase.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Improve ease by doing pre-work for the customer — intake, onboarding, and preparation done on your side before they have to act — because almost nobody does it and customers notice. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Perceived effort is a purchase and retention cost; removing steps the buyer would otherwise have to figure out makes the experience feel premium without changing the core product.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus his own workshop operations (staff knowing attendees' names and businesses on arrival).

**Fit:** Durable principle class. For a consumable with an 18–22.5% repeat rate, the ease vector has a concrete form: pre-selected refill bundles and a post-purchase transition-period walkthrough that removes the guesswork that causes churn. Zero cost, solo-executable, and aimed squarely at the binding constraint. Scored 6 rather than higher because the copy layer of onboarding is already covered by the 'how to start' stem in the awareness-level skill — the additive part here is the offer/structure layer (what the refill bundle is), not the wording.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Never let the ask be a bare 'request a quote' — give something in exchange for contact information, and describe it as a dream outcome in the buyer's own words. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Nobody wants the transaction step itself; they want an outcome. Trading a concretely desirable thing for an email raises opt-in rate because the exchange finally feels favorable to the visitor.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus a title example ('11 frameworks we use to build wildly profitable gyms'); no data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class. The Shopify email capture is a live, zero-cost surface, and list growth feeds directly into the retention constraint that is binding here. Directly actionable: replace a generic '10% off' popup with a named, specific incentive tied to what the buyer actually wants. Scored 6 rather than higher because the 'in their own language' half overlaps with the existing review-mining skill — the additive part is choosing the incentive, not phrasing it.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### There are three lead-magnet types: reveal a problem, give a trial of the solution, or hand over one step of a multi-step process. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Each gives the prospect real value before any commitment while leaving the rest of the job undone, so the opt-in is genuinely worth taking and the remaining gap is what you sell next.

**Evidence:** Examples for each: 'Seven mistakes homeowners make when fixing their HVAC'; a local-SEO firm that ranks a business top-three free and calls in seven days ('they're crushing it'); a fashion consultant who automated a color-palette assessment. Anecdotal.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and it gives a concrete menu for a surface that is already live at zero cost. All three have real forms here: reveal-a-problem as a short guide on why a natural deodorant switch fails, trial-of-solution as a travel-size or sample, one-step as a scent/SKU match quiz across the 12-SKU catalog. Scored 6 rather than higher because trial-of-solution carries real COGS and shipping cost at a $50.46 AOV, so only the zero-COGS variants are safe to run first.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Splinter one low-cost component out of your full offer, sell it at 80–90% off or free, and upsell the rest — because a 10–20% discount changes nothing while an 80–90% discount changes behavior. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Shallow discounts are absorbed as noise; a discount large enough to feel irrational forces a decision. You can afford it only on a component that costs you little, which is why the splinter step comes first — the deep discount buys the relationship and the remaining components carry the margin.

**Evidence:** Whiteboard A/B/C/D decomposition of a $2,000 offer; assertion plus the rebuttal that 'everything I do comes together' is always false (you can always paint, seal, or measure part of it). No data.

**Fit:** Durable pricing psychology, no decay, and it has a direct catalog form: lip balm is already the natural low-COGS entry SKU, and the discount-depth rule is an immediate corrective to the reflexive 10%-off popup. Held at 6 because at ~$2,700/mo with solo-funded shipping, an 80–90% off physical entry product can go cash-negative per order once postage is counted — the principle transfers, but only to items where landed cost is genuinely trivial, and the upsell path must exist before the discount ships.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Give away something other people charge for — the more real the lead magnet's cost, the better it converts. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Perceived value tracks demonstrable market price. If the thing is visibly something with a price tag elsewhere, the opt-in feels like a genuine win rather than a marketing wrapper.

**Evidence:** Stated as his personal preference; assertion only.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and a useful selection filter for a business whose products are physical and priced — a real travel-size or a full lip balm has an unmistakable market price, unlike a PDF. Scored 6 rather than higher because the same real cost that makes it convert is real cash out of a $2,700/mo operation, so it should be gated to a paid-order threshold (free item with purchase, or with a bundle) rather than given for a bare email.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

## Rejected

### Run a giveaway as a front-end attraction offer, then call every non-winner and give them a 'second place' consolation discount. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Requires distribution reach the business does not have, and is a traffic/lead-acquisition tactic that is premature behind the tracking → CRO → offer sequence.

**Fit reasoning:** Depends on scale that does not exist. A giveaway only produces meaningful entries with existing reach or paid distribution, and the follow-up mechanic is a phone call to high-ticket home-services leads — nothing about it maps to a $2,700/mo body care catalog with a small list. It is also a lead-acquisition play, which sits at the traffic stage behind the Tracking → CRO → Offer gate.

### Use a decoy offer for transactional businesses, and 'win your money back' for service businesses that require customer effort. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Named in passing with no stated mechanism or construction detail — not actionable or testable as presented.

**Fit reasoning:** Decoy pricing is a real durable mechanic, but the creator supplies no mechanism, no construction rules, and no worked example — there is nothing here testable enough to encode. 'Win your money back' additionally requires a customer-effort milestone that a shelf product has no way to verify.

### Lead-magnet ads beat direct-purchase ads on total ROAS over a longer time horizon and scale far better, because a direct 'buy now' ask only reaches the most-aware sliver of the market. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Assumes an ad budget and long-horizon attribution that do not exist; paid traffic is gated behind tracking, CRO, and offer work.

**Fit reasoning:** The entire claim is a comparison between two paid campaigns judged on long-window ROAS — it presumes both an ad budget materially above current spend and working multi-touch attribution, which is exactly what the Tracking → CRO → Offer/AOV → Traffic gate defers. The awareness-reach half of the argument is already covered by the existing awareness-level messaging skill.

### If your market won't pay the premium your improved offer requires, move up market to wealthier buyers who value speed, risk reduction, and ease. — 3/10

**Rejected because:** Strategic hand-wave with no stated mechanism for reaching the new segment; presumes repositioning capacity and channel access a solo operator at $2,700/mo does not have.

**Fit reasoning:** Durable in principle but non-actionable at this scale: repositioning to a higher-income segment means new channels, new packaging, and new creative, none of which a solo operator can execute without either budget or a distribution route. Amazon, the channel producing ~$1,800/mo, does not let you select buyer income. No mechanism given for how to reach the new segment.

### Instead of saying 'we can't afford that', ask the literal question 'what would it take to deliver this outcome in one-third the time?' with your team, then price the answer. — 2/10

**Rejected because:** Framed as a team exercise, and the speed vector it serves is not controllable by a solo operator whose fulfillment is handled by Amazon and Shopify.

**Fit reasoning:** The exercise is framed explicitly as a team workshop, and the speed vector has almost no surface area for a solo operator selling shelf-stable body care through Amazon FBA and Shopify — delivery speed is set by the platform, not the seller. The generalizable half is already captured by the value-vectors tactic above.
