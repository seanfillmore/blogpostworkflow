## Adopted

### Build the scroll-stopping frame from two parts: a visual the viewer instantly recognises to halt the scroll, and the unfamiliar thing the content is actually about to spike curiosity — never the unfamiliar thing alone. — 8/10

**Why it works:** A stranger scrolling gives the frame a split second. An unrecognisable object (a weird fishing lure on a table) gives the eye nothing to latch onto, so it never registers; the same lure held next to a big fish borrows the fish's instant recognisability for the stop and then hands the eye something novel, so the dots connect themselves and curiosity does the clicking.

**Evidence:** Worked example (fishing lure on a table vs. lure in one hand and a fish in the other) plus his own top-performing lighting-guide thumbnail, where lights surround him purely to make the subject readable in a glance.

**Fit:** Directly governs the frames RSC actually ships: the Amazon main image, the Shopify gallery opener, Meta statics, and the cover frame on his own short-form video. A jar of natural deodorant on white is the unrecognisable-lure failure; the same jar beside the raised-arm moment or the drugstore stick it replaces is the fish-plus-lure version. Durable composition principle, so age is irrelevant, and one person with a phone and an image model can execute it today.

### Allow exactly one primary subject in the frame and at most one secondary subject, and include the secondary only when it is needed to connect the dots or to open curiosity. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Too many competing elements either overwhelm the viewer or flatten the frame so nothing individually stands out, and a frame where nothing stands out stops nobody. Forcing a single dominant subject guarantees one thing wins the glance; the single permitted secondary element supplies the context that tells the viewer what the piece is about.

**Evidence:** Two documented before/after swaps: a busy phone-comparison thumbnail (emoji, tripods obscuring both phones, blurred face behind a gradient — 'visual vomit') flopped and started performing once replaced with a clean two-phone frame; a student's busy NFL frame went from 0.5% to 4.1% click-through after being cut to one recognisable face plus one short screenshot. Also a split test where the simpler of two versions took 44.6% of watch time vs 29%.

**Fit:** RSC's frames have to work at phone size for a stranger with no brand context, which is exactly the condition this rule is written for. It is a concrete subject-count constraint that sharpens the existing 1-second comprehension test rather than restating it, and it is the fastest fix available to a solo operator rebuilding a listing image set or an ad static. Durable composition principle.

### Read the finished script out loud, ideally several times, before hitting record — and rewrite any sentence you trip over so it is easier to say. — 8/10

**Why it works:** A line that is hard to speak is what makes teleprompter delivery sound read rather than spoken; rewriting to the mouth removes the stumble at source, and the read-through builds enough muscle memory that you anticipate the next line instead of reacting to it, which is what reads as natural on camera.

**Evidence:** Assertion, offered as his own named 'cheat code' for why his teleprompter delivery sounds unscripted.

**Fit:** The operator films, scripts and delivers his own short-form video — this is the exact craft problem he hits on every piece, and the existing marketing-creator-content-sourcing skill already notes that a creator whose cadence reveals they are reading a script is disqualifying. marketing-short-form-video-production records word-for-word teleprompter scripting but no read-aloud rewrite pass. ### Aim for a frame with no overlay text at all; where text is unavoidable, cap it at four words. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The frame's job is to communicate visually in a split second, and a viewer scrolling will not read. Every word added subtracts from the impact of the image, and a wall of small text (an article screenshot, a long episode title) is simply never read, so it contributes nothing while consuming the whole frame.

**Evidence:** Claims most of the most viral videos on YouTube carry no thumbnail text; concedes his own channel mostly uses text but that its biggest hits do not. Counter-evidenced honestly by the iPhone test where adding 'yikes' beat no text — so the rule is few words, not zero at any cost. Both critiqued student frames failed on unreadable small text.

**Fit:** A real constraint on RSC's frames, which have to survive phone-size viewing by a stranger. It also names the cost of the no-text ideal honestly — a wordless frame needs far more storytelling in the visual — so the operator can choose deliberately rather than defaulting to a headline. Complements the recorded headline-as-focal-point rule by capping length; does not replace it. Durable principle.
