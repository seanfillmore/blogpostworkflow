### Assume every subpage is an entry page for cold traffic — the credibility, social proof and offer that sit on the homepage must be repeated on the deep pages people actually land on, and proof belongs on the product or service pages rather than on a dedicated case-studies or reviews page nobody visits. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Organic and paid visitors land directly on deep pages with zero brand context and nobody navigates down from the homepage, so any trust element living only on the homepage is invisible to the majority of buyers. Proof only works where the decision is made — and a buyer who never clicks through still absorbs credibility from seeing the logo or headline sitting next to the offer.

**Evidence:** Live demonstration: a law firm's Trustpilot stars and award badges appear on the homepage but vanish on the commercial-contracts subpage that would actually rank in Google; plus screenshots of case studies embedded on a service page with client logos, and the assertion that case-study pages are among the least-visited on any site.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and an unusually exact match: an Amazon listing and a Shopify PDP are both pure cold-traffic entry points with no homepage visit preceding them, and Amazon is the larger channel at ~$1,800/mo. Zero cost, solo-executable, no traffic or attribution required — it means the review quotes, third-party proof, transition-period guarantee and 'what this is' framing must each appear on every PDP and in the A+ content rather than once on the homepage or a policy page. It sharpens existing proof-placement work by naming which pages need the proof, which nothing currently owns.

### Score every conversion page against a fixed six-item rubric — credibility, lure (what do I get and is it attractive at this price), objection handling, social proof, ease of use / application instructions, and result (is it clear what they get when they act) — and fix whichever items are missing or weak. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Conversion failure is usually a missing element rather than bad design; each item corresponds to a separate reason a visitor stalls, so walking a named list forces you to identify the actual leak instead of polishing whichever element you happen to notice.

**Evidence:** Presented as the course's 'qualified customer closer' roadmap, then applied live to three lead-gen pages and to a real screen-protector product page and homepage, item by item — including the observation that a £60/mo LeadPages page beat a £20,000 site on five of six elements, and two concrete failures found (shipping/currency inconsistency, undemonstrated result). Practitioner-derived rubric, no test data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class — a diagnostic rubric, age irrelevant. Free, solo-executable, needs no traffic or attribution, and applies to the Shopify PDP, collection pages, and the Amazon listing/A+ block where two-thirds of revenue sits. Held at 7 because marketing-conversion-friction-audit already owns roughly half the items (risk, proof, returns visibility, the four-term value equation); the additive value is the named checklist ordering plus the 'lure' and 'result' items — making explicit what happens after add-to-cart or after an email signup — so it belongs as an additional pass inside that skill rather than a new one, and at ~54 orders/month fixes are applied by judgement rather than tested.

### Select or build proof whose 'before' state is one the target buyer recognises as their situation today and whose 'after' is the outcome they actually want — and remember the format can be a testimonial, case study or video, not only a photo pair. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Identification is what makes proof persuasive: a viewer who sees themselves in the before state accepts the after state as available to them, whereas a before state that does not match their situation reads as evidence about someone else. A single quote that names the prospect's current state does both the resonance and the proof job that generic praise cannot.

**Evidence:** Bill Phillips fitness camp before/after pages, plus the AV company example where an events director's testimonial ('the best AV company I've ever dealt with') functions as an implicit before/after for another events director. Practitioner analysis, no conversion data.

**Fit:** Durable persuasion principle, age irrelevant, and free to apply on Shopify PDP imagery/copy and Amazon secondary image and A+ slots — the surfaces carrying ~$1,800/mo of Amazon revenue. It works on dozens of reviews rather than thousands: RSC's reviews naturally contain the before ('every natural deodorant I tried quit by noon') and the after ('made it through a 12-hour shift'). The non-photo permission matters because honest photographic before/afters are hard to produce solo for body care. Not higher because the existing review-mining and product-image-stack skills already own extraction and the transformation format — this only supplies the selection rule.

### Load proof with 'meaningful specifics' — exact figures (21% to 9% body fat, 30 lbs) plus seemingly irrelevant identifying detail such as age, occupation and location — because the irrelevant detail is what makes the claim feel real. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Concrete, checkable-sounding detail signals a real person and a real event rather than a marketing construction; a specific figure is harder to dismiss than 'lost loads of fat', and biographical detail with no bearing on the result ('a fireman in California') signals an ordinary person rather than a paid guru.

**Evidence:** Line-by-line teardown of a Bill Phillips testimonial explaining what each detail is doing, plus AV company testimonials that name both the company and the job title of the person quoted. Assertion with worked examples.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, zero cost, no traffic or attribution required — CRO-stage work. It extends the specificity rule already in marketing-conversion-copy-angles in a genuinely additive direction: that rule covers numbers in claim copy, this covers attribution detail on borrowed proof. Directly executable by pulling existing Shopify and Amazon reviews and displaying them with the reviewer's stated context ('switched off aluminium three years ago', 'works through 12-hour shifts') rather than a bare anonymous star quote. Not higher because the number half overlaps the existing rule and RSC can only display detail reviewers actually volunteered — inventing it is dishonest and an Amazon policy risk.

### Spread your displayed proof across different ages, genders and situations, and include cases that pre-empt the specific belief that would disqualify a reader ('at age 50...' kills 'I'm too old for this'). — 7/10

**Why it works:** Every visitor silently asks whether the result applies to someone like them; a proof set covering multiple identities means each reader finds their own case, and a case that leads with the disqualifying attribute neutralises the objection before it forms.

**Evidence:** Points out the fitness page alternates male and female subjects and opens one testimonial with 'at age 50'; explains what each choice defuses. Practitioner analysis, no data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, zero cost, and unusually well matched to the category's real disqualifiers — 'natural deodorant never works for someone who sweats as much as me', 'baking soda burns my skin', 'I gave up during the transition'. Those are exactly the beliefs blocking first purchase and reorder, and retention is the binding constraint. Executable today by curating existing Shopify and Amazon reviews into a proof set organised by objection rather than star rating, then placing them on the PDP and in the A+ block. Held at 7 because with a small corpus some objection slots will be empty — the gap becomes a prompt for the post-purchase review request, not something to fabricate.

### Inventory the credibility and social-proof assets you actually have (star rating and review count, awards, certifications, third-party testing, client or press logos, safe-checkout and payment badges, volume claims) and place at least one directly at every decision point — above the fold and immediately beside the button you expect people to click — instead of leaving them on an About or reviews page. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Nobody wants to be the first person to take an action, and everywhere you ask for trust you must supply a reason to trust at the moment of the ask. Credibility markers are an instant shortcut requiring no reading, but a marker the visitor has to hunt for is never counted, and proof only removes the objection if it is physically present when the objection fires.

**Evidence:** Client example where NHS, Deloitte, Lafarge and BBC logos were buried on an About page and moved into the hero and headline copy; Exposure Ninja's own awards and Google review display beside CTAs; National Accident Helpline teardown (Trustpilot in the hero, trust score and reviews in the sidebar beside the form, testimonials underneath); an ecommerce PDP with an above-the-fold review widget, secure-checkout logos and a '500k sold' badge; and an email popup reading '42,000 other people get our tips'. Assertions and teardowns, no conversion figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (proof psychology and placement), age irrelevant. Purely editorial, zero cost, no traffic or attribution needed — CRO-stage work on the ~$875/mo Shopify PDP and the Amazon A+ block: move star rating and one verbatim quote adjacent to add-to-cart, add payment/secure-checkout badges above the fold, and put proof in the email capture popup. Held at 7 because RSC has no enterprise logos or press coverage and the volume-based variants ('500k sold', '42,000 subscribers') depend on scale it does not have — displaying a small review count or list size can undercut credibility, so the marker set must be chosen honestly (third-party testing, cruelty-free/made-in-USA certification, small-batch) rather than padded; Amazon also controls star placement on the larger channel.

### There is a second class of objection — objections to taking the conversion action itself, not to the product — so enumerate the visitor's top three fears about clicking your CTA and answer each one in the copy immediately around it. — 7/10

**Why it works:** A visitor holds desire for the outcome on one side and fear, doubt and uncertainty on the other. Until the specific red flags (will this be low value, will someone pitch me, will I be embarrassed, what am I committing to) are answered, they will not act — and almost nobody will ask; they just leave and go somewhere they feel more assured. Web objections are driven by uncertainty about what happens next rather than by price.

**Evidence:** Worked examples: a construction firm's 'request a free quote' with no service-area or next-step info; a claims firm's 'start my claim'; a kitchen firm's copy that explicitly makes the home survey optional; plus the creator's own three fears about their free website review (it'll be rubbish, it's nothing new, someone will pitch me) and the exact copy used to defuse each. Practitioner assertion, no conversion figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, age irrelevant. CRO-stage work requiring no spend, no traffic, no attribution. Distinct from the existing problem/solution inventory (problems with the product) and from the friction audit's returns/guarantee coverage: this is about fears attached to the specific act of clicking. Live surfaces are the Shopify add-to-cart, the email capture popup, and the subscribe-and-save toggle — 'will I get locked in', 'will you spam me', 'what if the scent is wrong', 'will the discount code fail'. Capped at 7 because Amazon (~$1,800/mo, the larger channel) controls its own buy box, so the gain is confined to the ~$875/mo Shopify side and the popup.

### Make the conversion action the single most visible element above the fold — a bordered box with a high-contrast button and white space, placed where buyers already expect it (Amazon's top-right buy box) — never buried at the bottom of the page. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Visitors are unfamiliar, busy and distracted, so they do not hunt; a buyer who has to scroll for the way to act mostly does not act. Amazon has already trained every shopper where the buy action lives, so matching that position makes comprehension instant and removes a step that offers the buyer nothing.

**Evidence:** Three site walkthroughs with claimed rate bands (form buried at the bottom 0–1%, above-the-fold solicitor form 2–3%, strong lure above the fold 10–15%, one test site 33.7%), plus the Amazon product page cited as the convention-setter, a client 'get a quick quote' box, and the thought experiment of Amazon replacing its buy box with a phone number at the page bottom.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (layout convention and attention), not a fast-decaying platform mechanic — the Amazon buy-box convention has held for over a decade. Free and solo-executable: one theme pass to get add-to-cart, price, and the email-capture incentive above the fold and made the visual focal point on the Shopify PDP and homepage. Capped at 7 because the quoted UK lead-gen rate bands do not transfer to a $50.46-AOV consumable, at ~54 orders/month no placement change will be measurable, and it partly overlaps the existing rule about moving the guarantee above the fold next to add-to-cart — so treat it as one do-it-and-move-on layout pass.

### Word the call to action for the lowest perceived pain, not the highest perceived value — 'get a free quote' over 'book a free survey', 'try our 30-second claim checker' over 'start my claim' — and never ask for a step further down the buyer journey than the buyer currently needs. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Every ask carries an implied cost: 'book a survey' implies a salesperson in your house, 'start my claim' implies deciding now with embarrassment or cost attached if you turn out to be wrong. A low-commitment verb reframes the click as investigation rather than decision, so the visitor can proceed without having settled anything — even though the underlying offer is identical.

**Evidence:** ADT split test where 'get a free quote' beat 'book a free survey' by a claimed 60% uplift (audience had already established need and only wanted a price), plus a side-by-side of three claims-industry CTAs with 'try our 30-second claim checker' called 'perfect'. One split test, otherwise assertion.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (persuasion / perceived risk), so the older test date doesn't matter. Directly actionable at zero cost on the email-capture button, the secondary PDP CTAs ('find your scent' as entry to a SKU-match quiz across 12 SKUs), and especially the subscription CTA where 'Subscribe' implies lock-in while 'Send a refill every 8 weeks — change or cancel anytime' does not. Since retention is the binding constraint and subscribe-and-save is the adopted retention lever, reducing implied commitment on that button is a real lever. Not higher because the primary ecommerce CTA is add-to-cart and cannot be softened, Amazon button copy is not editable, and at ~54 orders/month RSC cannot run the split test that produced the 60% figure.

### Form length is a dial in both directions: conversion falls steadily from three to ten fields, but one or two fields also converts worse because a bare name-and-email form signals 'you are about to be pitched' rather than 'you will receive a diagnosis' — and where fulfilment is costly, extra fields are a deliberate filter against unserious requests. — 6/10
