## Adopted

### In the first 24–48 hours after purchase, make as many small specific promises as you can and keep every one — tell them exactly what happens next and when ('in the next 30 minutes you'll get an email, then this happens') — because cancellations come from unset expectations, not from the product. — 7/10

**Why it works:** A buyer left with a vague sense of what happens next fills the gap with their own timeline; when reality misses that invented timeline they conclude the seller is not on top of it and reverse the decision. A dense sequence of small promises kept in the window immediately after payment reinforces the purchase instead, converting buyer's remorse into confidence — and confidence is what makes them buy again.

**Evidence:** Practitioner assertion from onboarding thousands of gym and agency customers: 'the reason a lot of customers cancel is because you said someone's going to reach out and they thought it was going to take 30 minutes, you didn't set the expectation, it takes two days.' No cancellation or retention figures.

**Fit:** Durable retention principle, age irrelevant, and it names a mechanism no existing entry owns: marketing-post-purchase-onboarding covers what to teach (transition period, routine, early win) but nothing covers the expectation-setting schedule in the hours right after checkout. Direct fit because retention is the binding constraint and repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue — the honest ecommerce translation is an order-confirmation and pre-arrival sequence that states exactly when the order ships, when it arrives, when the first email lands and what week two will feel like, and then actually delivers each. Free, solo-executable in existing Klaviyo flows, no traffic or attribution needed. Not higher because the promises must be genuinely keepable by one person with FBA and third-party shipping, and at ~54 orders/month the effect on repeat rate will never be cleanly measurable.

### Audit any product against eight distinct levers for making a customer worth more — raise the price, get them to buy again over time, get them to buy more today, sell a higher-quality version, cut your own cost per unit, cross-sell a complement, sell a smaller quantity as a downsell, sell a lower-quality economy version — and as a beginner work the three that require nothing new from you: charge more, sell more today, get them to buy again. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Lifetime value is not one number but a set of independently movable inputs. Three of the eight (price, quantity today, repurchase) require no new product, no new supplier and no new operational surface, so they convert into revenue immediately; the other five all add product or negotiation work and are therefore later moves. Naming all eight prevents the default reflex of inventing a new product when an existing one has unexploited configurations.

**Evidence:** Taxonomy asserted and then applied twice as a worked demonstration — gum priced up in 20% steps, and a whiteboard walked through all eight levers (more whiteboards over time, five today, wood instead of metal, no logo, cheaper manufacturing, paper rolls, one instead of five, flimsier metal). No conversion or revenue data.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (offer and pricing logic), so age is irrelevant despite the 2024 recording. Genuinely useful here as a prioritization filter rather than a new mechanism: with ~54 orders/month, no ad budget and no team, the three no-new-product levers are exactly what the Offer/AOV stage of the gated sequence can reach, and the enumeration is a direct corrective to launching SKU 13. It overlaps 'don't add products, add ways to offer' in marketing-offer-construction, which is why it belongs there as an eight-item checklist under that existing rule rather than as its own skill; the additive part is that price and repurchase frequency sit in the same list as bundling, so a price change is considered alongside a bundle instead of forgotten. Held at 7 because five of the eight levers are already owned elsewhere (cross-sell and downsell by marketing-upsell-offer-design, repurchase cadence by marketing-retention-offers) or are operational rather than marketing (cost per unit).

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Walk the buyer through what they have already tried and how it went — then explicitly associate the parts they liked with your solution and the parts they disliked with the specific reason it failed and why yours is different. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Prior failed attempts are where the buyer's frustration already lives, so surfacing them widens the felt gap between where they are and where they want to be, which is the motivation to act. Splitting the prior attempt into 'the part that was right' and 'the part that broke' lets you inherit the credibility of the category while attributing the failure to a named cause your product removes — so you are not asking them to abandon the belief that got them here, only the product that let them down.

**Evidence:** Described as the 'pain cycle' step of his CLOSER framework, refined over 4,000+ consults: 'what have you done before, how'd that work out for you' repeated to keep expanding the gap. Assertion, no data.

**Fit:** Durable persuasion principle, age irrelevant, and it has an unusually exact form here: the natural-deodorant buyer has almost always tried another natural deodorant that quit by noon or burned with baking soda. Copy that says 'you were right to get off aluminium — the problem was X, and here is what we do instead' does both jobs at once and is free to write on the PDP, in Amazon bullets and in the non-buyer nurture flow. In a saturated category this is also the natural vehicle for the named mechanism the sophistication rule demands. Held at 6 because it partially overlaps negative framing and transformation-first ordering already in marketing-conversion-copy-angles, and because attributing a competitor's failure to a specific cause in body care must stay to defensible formulation facts, not invented ones.

### Pitch exactly three differentiating pillars, each stated in one or two sentences and then explained with an analogy or metaphor comparing something the buyer already understands to the thing they don't — and keep the pitch about their destination, not the product's internals. — 6/10

**Why it works:** A long monologue about product detail loses the sale because the buyer cares about their own problem, not your mechanism. Capping it at three pillars keeps the argument holdable, and an analogy transfers understanding by mapping the unfamiliar onto something familiar, so a mechanism claim becomes believable without a technical explanation the buyer has to work through.

**Evidence:** Worked examples — SEO explained as a paycheck versus an investment account that compounds, and a mortgage-broker lead script built on exactly three pillars (exclusive, timely, qualified). Practitioner assertion from his own scripts, no conversion data.

**Fit:** Durable copy-construction principle, age irrelevant, and free to apply to PDP body copy, Amazon A+ blocks and the non-buyer nurture emails. The three-pillar cap fits how much a shopper actually reads and lines up with the existing 'cap the us-vs-them graphic at three or four attributes you win on' rule, so it reinforces rather than contradicts. The analogy device is the additive part — nothing in the existing copy skills covers explaining a mechanism by comparison, and natural deodorant needs exactly that for the transition period and for why an aluminium-free formula works differently. Held at 6 because analogies in body care slide easily into implied physiological claims, so each one has to be paired with the existing specificity rule and kept descriptive, and at ~54 orders/month the choice of three pillars is judged rather than tested.

### Test price upward in small committed increments — for every five sales that close without resistance, raise the price 20% and keep going until buying stops, then pick the price where gross profit times conversion rate is highest — and never drop the price to save a sale mid-test. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Most sellers guess a price once and never revisit it, usually low. Stepping upward in fixed increments against a fixed sample size turns pricing into a series of cheap, bounded experiments where the downside per step is five orders, and evaluating on gross profit times conversion rate (not units sold) stops the operator from mistaking volume for money. Committing to the stated sample matters because discounting mid-test destroys the only signal the test was supposed to produce and trains buyers to wait.

**Evidence:** Worked arithmetic only — $5 gum stepped to $6, then $7.20, with the instruction to keep going and 'do the math'. Practitioner assertion, no case data, no mention of how conversion rate is actually observed at small volumes.

**Fit:** Durable pricing logic, so age is not the limiter. Unusually workable at this scale: five Shopify orders is a matter of days at ~54 orders/month, so the sample unit is small enough to actually run, and revenue is the prime directive with price as one of only two multiplicands available without new traffic. It also reinforces the existing 'fund the premium by raising price' rule with a concrete procedure. Capped at 6 for two real limits: the 20% step is far too coarse for a $12 deodorant in a saturated category where Native and Dr. Squatch set the visible comparison price, so steps here should be small; and on Amazon (~$1,800/mo, the larger channel) price moves interact with Buy Box and ranking, so the escalation belongs on Shopify first. The 'never discount to save a sale' half also partly restates the anti-discount framing already in this skill.

### Evaluate any product against four properties — unique (nobody else can sell it), expensive relative to cost, sticky (bought again and again), and 'air' (cheap to deliver) — because that combination is what produces gross profit. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Uniqueness removes price comparison, high price relative to cost creates margin per unit, stickiness multiplies that margin across repeat purchases, and low delivery cost keeps the margin intact — so the four together are the arithmetic of gross profit rather than four separate nice-to-haves.

**Evidence:** Coca-Cola worked as an illustration (secret recipe, pennies of cost, lifelong repeat consumption) plus the claim that a one-person service business also satisfies all four. Assertion with one canonical brand example, no figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (offer/product economics), so age is irrelevant. It cannot be used as intended — marketing-offer-construction explicitly gates adding SKUs, and a solo operator at $2,700/mo should not be launching product 13 — but it translates honestly as a scoring lens on the 12 SKUs that already exist: which item is stickiest (deodorant and oral care are true consumables), which has the lowest landed cost (lip balm), which is genuinely differentiated (baking-soda-free variant). That scoring decides which SKU anchors a named bundle, which becomes the low-COGS entry/attraction offer, and which carries the subscribe-and-save cadence. Free, no traffic, no attribution. Capped at 5 because the 'unique' criterion is largely unachievable in a saturated natural-deodorant category, and the low-COGS-entry insight is already half-covered by the splinter and lead-magnet rules in offer-construction — the additive part is only the four-way scoring frame.

### There are only four things one person can do to make people aware of what they sell — warm outreach, cold outreach, content, and paid ads — and until you are at seven figures you should run just one or two, ideally content plus warm outreach because they are the cheapest. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Nobody can buy from you unless they know you exist, and the four channels exhaust the ways a single operator can create that awareness; concentrating on the cheapest one or two avoids spreading a solo operator's finite hours across eight half-run channels.

**Evidence:** His own sequence as a beginner: a year of warm outreach producing ~13 customers, then his first content post ('the free training project') publishing those results, then later paid ads and cold outbound. Anecdotal, no figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (channel taxonomy and focus discipline), so age is irrelevant. Its real value here is the focus rule, not the taxonomy: paid is explicitly gated behind Tracking → CRO → Offer/AOV, and retention — not traffic — is the binding constraint, so the correct read is that organic content plus warm outreach to people who already know the brand are the only two acquisition channels worth any hours at $2,700/mo. Capped at 5 because cold outbound has no honest form for a $12 consumer deodorant, warm outreach for a solo operator is a handful of people rather than 25 customers at $10k/yr, and the whole worked example is a high-ticket service model. It is a coverage-and-focus checklist, not a revenue mechanism.

### Strike a reciprocal deal with a non-competing business that already has your customers, so each of you routes buyers to the other (his example: a gym and a chiropractor trading referrals). — 5/10

**Why it works:** Another business has already paid to acquire the exact people you want and has their trust; a reciprocal arrangement borrows that audience at no media cost, and because it is mutual the partner has a standing incentive to keep sending people.

**Evidence:** Stated as one of the four 'lead getters' with the gym/chiropractor example. Assertion only, no partnership economics or conversion figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class (distribution partnership), age irrelevant, and it is one of the very few acquisition moves that needs no ad budget, no media buyer and no attribution — a solo operator can cold-email ten small non-competing brands (natural soap, sensitive-skin, zero-waste) and propose a co-marketing bundle or an insert swap. Honest translation exists for a physical catalog, unlike the agency and employee variants in the same list. Held to 5 because RSC brings very little to a swap: a sub-1,000 subscriber list and ~54 orders/month make it a weak trading partner, the outcome depends entirely on someone else agreeing, and none of the referred revenue will be attributable at this volume.

### Never make a customer repeat themselves — take notes on every conversation, pass them forward, and open the next contact by referencing what they already told you. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Recapping a customer's own stated situation back to them signals that the business is organised and paying attention, which raises satisfaction and the odds they buy again; making them re-explain signals the opposite and costs goodwill at the moment they are already frustrated.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus a described handoff chain (setter → sales → onboarding 1 → onboarding 2), with the explicit note that a beginner is taking all the calls and should at least keep and review their own notes. No data.

**Fit:** Durable retention/service principle, free, and the solo-operator version is exactly the one the creator names: keep a note per customer and open every support reply referencing their prior order, scent, and the issue they raised last time. At ~54 orders/month a single person can genuinely do this, and repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue, so goodwill in a support thread lands directly on the binding constraint. It fits alongside the existing hand-cultivation of top repeat buyers in marketing-retention-offers. Capped at 5 because it is a service habit rather than a revenue mechanism, it has no effect on the ~$1,800/mo Amazon channel where buyer identity is largely hidden, and the multi-person handoff half of the tactic is irrelevant with no team.

### Create a higher-quality version of what you already sell to upsell — stronger dose, better materials, premium ingredients, faster or priority service, exclusivity touches — because every component of an offer sits on a quality dimension that can be raised. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Buyers differ in willingness to pay, not just in what they want. A visibly better version captures the ones who would have paid more, so each raised attribute becomes a reason the same buyer pays more for essentially the same job to be done.

**Evidence:** Illustrative only: 2mg versus 4mg nicotine gum, plastic versus Kevlar or metal iPhone cases, wood instead of metal whiteboards, and service equivalents (priority support, extended hours, senior reps). No data.

**Fit:** Durable pricing/offer logic, age irrelevant, and it does add a lever the existing offer skill does not name — that skill covers quantity bundles, buy-X-get-Y, and how to name tiers, but presupposes the tiers exist rather than telling you to create them along a quality axis. Honest forms exist here without new sourcing: a larger-size or extra-strength deodorant as the premium tier. Capped at 5 because it sits in real tension with the strongest rule already in marketing-offer-construction — 'don't add products, add ways to offer the products you already have' — a genuinely new premium formulation means new inventory and cash a $2,700/mo solo operation should not spend, and the neighbouring rules ('win on faster / less risky / easier' and 'fund the premium by raising your price') already cover much of it. Use the size and configuration axis, not new formulations, and treat SKU 13 as off-limits.

### Run a common factors analysis: put your top 10% and bottom 10% of comparable assets side by side — content pieces, ads, customers, sales call recordings — and name the discrepancies between them, because those discrepancies are the transferable skill. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Doing volume produces data; the data only becomes skill when you close the feedback loop by contrasting winners against losers. The differences between the best and worst performers isolate the variable that is actually doing the work, so you can deliberately make more of what wins and less of what doesn't, instead of averaging across everything.

**Evidence:** Assertion from personal practice — he says it is how he learns things nobody teaches, and applies it to content, ads, customer avatars, and sales scripts. No worked example, no numbers, no controlled comparison.

**Fit:** Durable-principle class (a learning/feedback loop, not a platform mechanic), so age is irrelevant. Executable today at zero cost on two surfaces a solo operator actually has: Klaviyo campaign history (which sends earned opens and clicks vs. which died) and Shopify order history (what the repeat buyers who drove 45–52% of revenue have in common versus one-and-done buyers). Capped at 5 because the deciles are tiny here — ~54 orders/month, 12 SKUs, a small list, and no working attribution — so every conclusion is directional judgement rather than a finding, and the ad-and-content version of the exercise needs volume this business does not have. It also partly overlaps the top-customer identification already in marketing-retention-offers and the winner-dissection rule parked in marketing-paid-acquisition-scaling; the additive part is the explicit bottom-decile contrast, which neither owns.
