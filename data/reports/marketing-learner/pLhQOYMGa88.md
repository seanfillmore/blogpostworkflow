## Adopted

### Deliberately write the preview/preheader text instead of letting the client auto-fill the first ~150 characters, pulling the most valuable and curiosity-inducing nugget forward — never ship an email with a defaulted preheader. — 8/10

**Why it works:** The preview line is the second thing a reader sees after the subject and functions like a video thumbnail or YouTube hover — it either extends or wastes the subject line's promise and materially affects the open decision. Left to default it shows filler ('Hey John, blah blah'), which burns the slot.

**Evidence:** Claims a 24% increase for his own sends and cites reporting that one in four recipients read the preview before opening; notes it takes 10 seconds. No underlying data shown.

**Fit:** Email is the one owned retention surface and retention is the binding constraint. Zero cost, one field per send, no traffic, no attribution, no budget — a solo operator can do this on every Klaviyo campaign and flow email today. The existing copy-angles skill mentions preview text only in passing under curiosity loops; nothing owns the rule 'never ship an email with a defaulted preheader'.

**Target skill:** `marketing-email-design-production` (edit)

### Reward the reader in the first glance — open the email with a self-contained payoff (a quote, a one-line insight) so the act of opening is immediately reinforced, and make sure every send delivers one usable thing so it 'pays for itself'. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Behavior recurs because it was rewarded afterward, not because of what preceded it. If opening reliably produces value, open rate compounds; a single useless send can break the habit and push you into the promotions tab, which is inbox death.

**Evidence:** Behavioral-reinforcement reasoning applied consistently across two teardowns of his own sends, plus the observation that unopened mail eventually lands in the promo tab. No open-rate data.

**Fit:** Durable retention psychology, age irrelevant, and it fills a real gap: existing skills cover what an email says and how it is built but nothing covers why a subscriber opens the next one. With retention binding and repeat buyers at 45–52% of revenue, the education emails Real Skin Care needs — transition period, storage, oral-care usage — are exactly the 'one usable thing per send' format. Not higher because the effect is only measurable as open rate on a small list.

**Evidence:** Practitioner observation from his own sends ('if we put more money stuff in an email it tends to get a higher percentage in the promo tab') plus a stat that 16.99% of B2B marketing emails never reach the inbox at all. No controlled test.

### Make the new subscriber reply 'yes' to receive the promised lead magnet instead of auto-delivering it. — 7/10

**Why it works:** A reply is the strongest engagement signal a recipient can send, which teaches the provider that your mail belongs in the primary inbox; it also filters for people who actually want ongoing communication rather than just the freebie.

**Evidence:** Describes his own live opt-in email doing exactly this and explains the algorithmic reasoning; no open, reply, or revenue figures.

**Fit:** Zero cost, solo-executable, and it operates on a live surface — the Shopify popup-to-welcome-email handoff — where retention begins. On a small list, a few genuine replies are a meaningful reputation signal, so this works better at Real Skin Care's scale than at Hormozi's. Held at 7 because fulfilment falls on one person and gating a discount code behind a reply adds checkout friction, so it fits a content or guide incentive better than a coupon.

### Use the opt-in confirmation email to sell the subscriber on staying subscribed — tease what's coming and what else they'll get — and deliver the promised incentive near the end rather than leading with it. — 7/10

### If you aren't emailing at all, commit to a low cadence (once or twice a month), then batch-write the entire year's emails in a single sitting — 24 emails took him half a day — and treat anything beyond that as bonus. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The barrier to email is perceived ongoing effort, not difficulty. Writing a year of sends in one block removes the recurring decision and reveals how little total time the channel actually costs, so the channel finally gets turned on.

**Evidence:** Personal account: wrote 24 emails in half a day; says waiting years to start emailing was one of the biggest mistakes of his career; cites a 40% conversion lift when School added email follow-up. Anecdotal.

**Fit:** Durable production principle, no decay, and specifically shaped for a solo operator with no team — the constraint it removes (recurring effort) is exactly the one that stops one person from running an email program. Zero cost, needs no traffic or attribution, and campaign email is the cheapest lever against the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Adaptation: the 3x/week cadence he calls ideal is for a pure-value newsletter to a huge list and would burn a small ecommerce list, so only the start-monthly-and-batch half transfers.

### Elicit and select pain-based testimonial hooks: stop asking 'what was life like before you started' and instead ask 'what was your worst moment', then choose the testimonials that open on that pain rather than on the result. — 7/10

**Why it works:** An open-ended 'before' question produces a meandering story with no extractable opening line; asking for a single worst moment forces a specific, visceral scene, and that scene is the hook. A testimonial that opens on the customer's pain lets the reader identify before any claim is made, whereas one that opens on the result reads as a brag and gets skipped. Specificity ('barely making enough to survive') is what makes it land versus a vague 'it was hard.'

**Evidence:** He reviewed roughly 2,500 Gym Launch testimonials and reports the top four hooks of all time were all pain-based specific moments, two independently landing on '$0 of profit.' No conversion or spend figures attached.

**Fit:** Durable persuasion / customer-language elicitation principle, age irrelevant, free and solo-executable, and it works at Real Skin Care's review volume — you need a handful of quotes, not thousands. Additive to the existing review-mining skill, which covers how to extract golden-nugget quotes but not which question produces them or which ones to pick. Natural deodorant has an unusually clean 'worst moment' ('I kept my arms down in a meeting', 'my shirt was ruined by noon'), feeding PDP proof blocks, Amazon A+ and email subject lines. Not higher because Amazon forbids directed review solicitation, so only the Shopify-side request is safe, and it partly overlaps the specificity rule in conversion-copy-angles.

### Make unsubscribing easy and visible rather than hard, because captive-but-dormant subscribers damage long-term domain deliverability more than unsubscribes do. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Continuing to send to an uninterested segment drives down engagement signals and domain reputation, which reduces the odds that the people who do want your email actually receive it in the primary inbox rather than the promotions tab.

**Evidence:** Practitioner reasoning plus his own numbers: ~1%+ unsubscribe on the first send, now ~0.42% per email. No controlled comparison.

**Fit:** Durable principle with a deliverability mechanism that does not depend on scale — in fact a small list is more fragile, since a handful of spam complaints on a sub-1,000-subscriber list can meaningfully hurt inbox placement for the reorder and transition-period emails that attack the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Zero cost, one-time setup for a solo operator. Not higher because the revenue effect is invisible at this volume and Shopify/Klaviyo already include a compliant unsubscribe link by default; the additive work is pruning non-openers and not burying the link.

### Expect an abnormally high unsubscribe rate on the first send after a long dormant stretch — it is pulled-forward churn ('shaking the tree'), not a signal to stop emailing. — 6/10

**Why it works:** Subscribers who would have churned across several months churn all at once when contact resumes, so the spike is a one-time cleanup rather than a recurring cost; refusing to send to avoid it just keeps a dead list dead.

**Evidence:** His own first send had his highest-ever unsubscribe rate (just over 1%), which settled to ~0.42% per email thereafter. Single-case anecdote.

**Fit:** Durable retention psychology, age irrelevant, and specifically useful for a solo operator who has almost certainly under-mailed a list where 45–52% of revenue comes from repeat buyers. It removes the main reason the send does not happen and sets the correct expectation so a scary first-send metric does not stop the flow build. Capped at 6 because it is an interpretive rule for a number rather than a mechanism that itself produces revenue.

**Fit:** Durable asset-structure principle, free to apply. Its real value here is reconciliation: the existing email-design skill carries both 'at least three CTAs' and 'exactly one offer or ask per email', which conflict; one ask per objective with one or two destinations max resolves it. ### Use one repeating value-email skeleton — open with something that rewards the reader for opening, put the contextual CTA in the middle, close with something that rewards them for reading — and send it at least once a month. — 5/10

**Why it works:** Front-loading a payoff makes the open feel worth it, a second payoff at the end trains the reader to finish, and burying the ask in the middle means the CTA rides on attention the content already earned rather than competing with it. A fixed skeleton removes per-send decisions, which is why a low cadence is still sustainable.

**Evidence:** Presented as the format of his own newsletter, with a walkthrough of one send that 'did really well' and a claim that people screenshotted it. No open, click, or revenue figures; the 'you won't make less money' framing is explicitly hedged.

**Fit:** Durable asset-structure principle, so age is not the limiter. It fills a real gap: the adopted email skeleton is a promo layout (hero image plus three CTAs) that does not fit an education send, and this is exactly an education-send skeleton with the ask placed mid-body. Free and solo-executable at a once-a-month cadence, on the retention surface carrying 45–52% of revenue. Capped at 5 because Hormozi's 'reward' is a B2B business insight; here it must be usage/education content, and a monthly broadcast is a weaker retention lever than the triggered post-purchase and reorder flows.

## Rejected

### Hold each email to a single concept and roughly 200 words (a one-minute read), cutting anything that introduces a second idea. — 4/10

**Fit reasoning:** Durable in class, but 'exactly one offer or ask per email' and 'short copy instead of paragraphs' are already codified, and the noted exception is exactly the case that matters here — the transition-period education email cannot be a 200-word single-concept send.

### Give subscribers platform-exclusive bonuses — e.g. good book chapters peeled out and offered only to the email list — so people have a reason to be on that channel specifically. — 4/10

**Fit reasoning:** The underlying move — choosing a lead-magnet incentive with real perceived value — is already covered by marketing-offer-construction, and the asset class (surplus book chapters) does not exist for a body-care brand.

The 35–45x figure also presumes a list size and acquisition machine that a $2,700/mo solo operation does not have.
