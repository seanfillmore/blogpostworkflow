### The Classic Upsell: your core offer solves one problem and immediately creates the next one, so sell the solution to that next problem at the moment of purchase ('you can't have X without Y') — the free storage unit creates the need for a lock, boxes, tape, a dolly and insurance. — 8/10

**Why it works:** The purchase itself creates immediate, obvious follow-on needs the buyer hasn't thought about yet, and you are the only seller present at the moment the need appears. Buyers are at peak trust and peak awareness of the adjacent gap right after buying, so the next offer reads as completion rather than a new sale, and the attach revenue is captured at zero acquisition cost.

**Evidence:** Itemized storage anecdote from a 27-location operator (a free month yields $127: $47 lock, boxes/tape/markers, equipment rental, $10/mo insurance upgrade, size upgrade); rental car, bicycle-and-helmet, dog-food-and-toys examples; burger-shop arithmetic showing profit going from $0.25 to $3.00 across three upsells. Anecdote plus illustrative math, no cohort data.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction principle, age irrelevant, and it fits the catalog exactly: buying natural deodorant creates real named next problems (the transition period, residue on shirts, a body wash that doesn't fight the pit microbiome), and RSC already stocks adjacent body, oral and lip SKUs. Zero cost and solo-executable as a cart cross-sell, a Shopify post-purchase offer, and a frequently-bought-together pairing on Amazon. Lifts AOV above $50.46 with no new visitors, which is the Offer/AOV stage of the gated sequence. Held below 9 because the ~$1,800/mo Amazon channel gives no control over a post-purchase upsell surface, and the 'free front end' half carries real COGS at this revenue level.

### Explain to the buyer why the small or cheap option physically cannot produce the outcome they want — using a concrete analogy — so the larger premium option becomes the obvious solution. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Buyers underestimate the time and quantity required for a result. Once you show them the real dose-and-duration math (the 'turkey talk': you can't cook a three-hour turkey in ninety minutes without burning it), the bigger package stops looking expensive and starts looking like the only version that works.

**Evidence:** The $5 five-day tanning pass story — five sessions never gets anyone as tan as they want, so the pass is credited toward a $19.99 unlimited membership 'easy as that' — plus the gym version where 8 of 10 leads took the $399 premium. Anecdotal, single-operator claims.

**Fit:** Durable offer/pricing logic, age irrelevant, and the single best-fit tactic in its excerpt. Natural deodorant has a genuinely true version of the turkey talk: one stick does not span the transition period, so a single-unit purchase is the version most likely to fail. Saying that plainly on the PDP and in the Amazon A+ block simultaneously lifts AOV from $50.46 and forward-buys enough supply to survive the churn window — hitting both the Offer/AOV stage and the binding retention constraint at zero cost, solo, with no traffic or attribution. It is the persuasion layer that makes the already-adopted multi-unit pack actually sell, and it is distinct from the published-results-timeline tactic (that documents what happens; this uses the timeline to justify quantity).

### The same economics can be stated many ways — percentage off, absolute dollar amount, a free portion, or a total package price, and a free bonus versus an equivalent discount ('get the $1,000 program free when you join' vs 'get it for $1 when you join') — so choose the framing deliberately and use both adding good stuff and removing cost. — 8/10

**Why it works:** The arithmetic is identical but the framings are not psychologically equivalent: free items and discounts trigger different decision heuristics (gain-framing vs loss-avoidance), so buyers respond to the presentation of savings, and you can move conversion without moving price at all.

**Evidence:** Worked example: a $1,200/yr item sold for $900 stated as '25% off' vs '$300 off' vs '3 months free' vs 'one year for $900 ($1,200)', plus paired free-bonus/$1 examples. Assertion that they mean the same thing and should be tested; no data.

**Fit:** Durable pricing/framing logic, no decay, and the highest-value framing item available: bundle and multipack pricing is already the adopted AOV lever, and this makes the label on that bundle a free variable ('save 25%' vs 'save $18' vs 'third stick free' vs '3 for $99, a $132 value'; 'lip balm free with your first refill' vs 'add the lip balm for $1'). The $1 variant even recovers some landed cost at a $50.46 AOV. Applies identically on Shopify bundle widgets, Amazon multipack ASINs and email subject lines, with no spend and no traffic requirement. Held at 8 only because at ~54 orders/month a clean A/B read is impossible — pick by judgment (free-portion framing, per the source's own argument that 'free' beats an equivalent discount).

### Replace yes/no asks with A-or-B choices — quantity (one bottle or two?), start date, cadence, flavour, size, delivery speed — and always attach a nudge line naming your own pick ('this is my favourite', 'X is usually a safe bet', 'most people start here'), nudging harder on whatever you want to move. — 8/10

**Why it works:** When 'don't buy' is one of the presented options, some share of buyers take it; framing the decision as a preference between two purchases presupposes the sale and removes the no-purchase branch from the choice set. Inexperienced buyers also lack the basis to choose between similar options and stall, so a one-line endorsement supplies the missing decision criterion and converts hesitation into a pick.

**Evidence:** Personal discovery anecdote — 19 consecutive nutrition consults with zero sales, then 'chocolate or vanilla?' and 'kiwi or strawberry lemonade?' sold the next twenty customers in a row and cleared $5,000 of shelf inventory — plus thousands of in-person supplement sales, a list of A/B dimensions, and example nudge one-liners. Single-operator anecdote, no controlled comparison.

**Fit:** Durable persuasion and choice-architecture principle, age irrelevant, and unusually well-matched: a scented body-care catalog is natively an A/B choice, and a first-time natural-deodorant buyer has no basis for picking a scent or strength. Translates directly and free into cart and post-purchase copy ('lavender or cedar?'), a default two-pack quantity selector, refill cadence framed as 'every 4 weeks or every 8 weeks' rather than subscribe yes/no, and a 'most people start here' badge on one PDP variant — all zero-cost CRO/Offer-stage work with no ad budget or attribution. Held at 8 because a webpage cannot actually remove the no-purchase option the way a salesperson can, at ~54 orders/month it is applied by judgment rather than measured, and the nudge half partly implements the existing decision-fatigue rule about leading with one recommended pack.

### Prescription upselling: write out detailed, personalized step-by-step instructions for how to use the additional products alongside what the customer already bought — what to use, how much, when — and let the routine itself sell the products, telling rather than asking. — 8/10

**Why it works:** A written regimen makes each product a required step in a plan the buyer has already committed to, so the purchase decision collapses into 'follow the plan' rather than 'evaluate nine items', and the yes/no decision point disappears. Vague suggestions leave every item optional.

**Evidence:** Anecdote: writing per-item dosing instructions on the order form led the customer to buy everything without being asked, followed by 'do you want to just use the card on file?'; he claims 30-day profits 'skyrocketed' after adopting it. No figures.

**Fit:** Durable retention/persuasion principle, age irrelevant, and the artifact already has a home: the packaging insert and post-purchase email sequence. Turning first-use instructions into a named multi-SKU routine (morning: deodorant plus this bar; night: this oral care step, in a stated order and cadence) does double duty — it removes the misuse that causes transition-period churn, the binding constraint, and cross-sells the adjacent SKUs of a 12-SKU catalog. Costs a print run and some copy, no traffic or attribution needed. The additive claim over existing onboarding coverage is that the instruction sheet is the upsell vehicle, not just education; held below 9 because the AOV lift here is a suggestion on a page rather than a live human removing the option to decline.

### Bundle multiple upsells into one named package and name every tier deliberately — the top tier an aspirational status name ('Fastest Results', 'Transformation Package'), the floor tier something like 'The Minimum' — so one ask sells many items. — 8/10

**Why it works:** It is easier to sell one thing than nine; a name framed around the outcome or buyer type lets the customer choose an identity or result rather than evaluate each component. Names also do positioning work price columns cannot: an aspirational label attaches identity to the expensive option, and calling the floor 'the minimum' frames it as the baseline so buying nothing feels deficient. The package can later be peeled apart as a downsell.

**Evidence:** Assertion from practice with example package names and the airline analogy (First / Business / Economy); claims it boosts upsells-per-person. No conversion data.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction logic, no decay, and it extends the buy-X-get-Y multipack already adopted with a naming layer nothing else covers. A 12-SKU catalog at a $50.46 AOV is exactly the case where a named 'Natural Switch Starter' or 'Full Routine' is a single ask that lifts AOV and forward-buys months of a consumable, suppressing churn with no new traffic. Zero cost, solo-executable on Shopify bundle and collection pages today and as a multipack ASIN on Amazon. Held at 8 because naming adds no value by itself and at ~54 orders/month the effect will never be measurable — apply by judgment and move on.

### Run a win-back campaign to customers who haven't purchased in 6+ months: look at what they previously paid, decide how much of it you're willing to credit back, and offer that credit personally as the reason to return — 'I want to give you $500 of your money back as credit toward…' — redeemable only against a new, usually larger purchase. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Past customers have already cleared the trust and first-purchase hurdles, so reactivating them is far cheaper than acquiring strangers. Framing the incentive as their own money returned reads as restitution and recognition rather than a markdown, so it does not devalue the product or train the list to wait for sales, and because it is redeemable only against a new purchase it converts a dormant customer into an order.

**Evidence:** Anecdote with numbers: 200 personalized videos to past customers offering $4,000 credit, ~20% conversion, one day of recording producing ~$1.9M annual revenue; plus the origin anecdote of a peer adding 'another hundred members' of recurring revenue via rollover and a scripted chiropractor winback. High-ticket coaching context; the claim that it 'gets way more people to take it' is assertion.

**Fit:** Durable offer construction and retention logic, so the 2014-era anecdotes don't decay. This is the closest fit to the binding constraint: repeat rate is 18–22.5% while repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue, meaning the largest identifiable revenue pool is people who bought once and never came back. A win-back email crediting their last order's value toward a multi-unit or refill pack costs nothing to send, needs no traffic, no ad budget and no working attribution — redemption is measured directly by discount code — and the list is small enough that the personalized-video version is genuinely feasible. It also gives the operator a non-discount framing for the same dollars, fixing the discount reflex. Not 9 because the credit is real margin at a $50.46 AOV so it must be gated to a larger or recurring purchase, absolute dollars recovered will be modest, and Amazon buyers (~$1,800/mo) cannot be contacted this way at all.

### Convert one-time buyers into a recurring subscription, because a lower per-period price converts many more people, each still pays the same total over time, and you end up with a much larger base to upsell later. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Splitting a large price into recurring payments lowers the entry barrier so a bigger share of the same audience buys; retained periods rebuild the total revenue, and every extra subscriber is also an extra person available for later upsells. You sell once and get paid repeatedly.

**Evidence:** Worked arithmetic (100 prospects: 10 buyers at $1,000 = $10,000 versus 40 buyers at $50/mo held 20 months = $42,000 and 4x the upsell base). Illustrative math, not measured results.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, age irrelevant, and the single best-matched idea available: deodorant, body care and oral care are consumables, repeat rate is 18–22.5%, repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue, and retention is explicitly the binding constraint. A Shopify subscribe-and-save / refill cadence is solo-executable at near-zero cost and needs no ad budget, attribution or new traffic — it converts existing buyers into scheduled revenue. Not 9 or 10 because the arithmetic comes from high-ticket services; at a $50.46 AOV the per-order economics differ and enrollment rates on low-ticket consumable subscriptions are far lower than the 40% in the example.

### Advertise a permanently lower rate that the customer earns by staying past your single most common churn point (or by committing to a longer term), and remind them as the milestone approaches. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Churn clusters at a predictable point. Placing a permanent reward just beyond it gives the wavering customer a concrete reason to push through the exact moment they would otherwise quit, and once they are past it the habit is formed. A rate they can never get again also makes cancelling feel like losing an asset, so commitment length rises and churn falls while the discount is recovered over a longer lifetime.

**Evidence:** A named real-world example — a rice company selling a one-time price, 5% off subscription, and 15% off if you stayed subscribed five straight months, which he infers was set just past their typical cancellation point — plus the newsletter lifetime-rate example and the Micro Gyms Stage III offer. Observational, no data from his own accounts.

**Fit:** Durable principle class and the best-fit continuity tactic available. RSC already knows its churn point precisely: the natural-deodorant transition period, which is exactly when a first-time buyer concludes the product failed and is why repeat sits at 18–22.5%. A stated 'stay through order three and your price drops permanently' costs nothing to configure as a founding-subscriber rate, needs no traffic or attribution, and pairs with the transition-period education already in the post-purchase flow. The rice-company precedent is a consumable CPG rather than a high-ticket service, so it transfers cleanly. Held below 9 only because the margin give is permanent and must be sized from real landed cost rather than a percentage-off reflex, and it overlaps the prepay/lifetime-rate framing already adopted.

### Simple scales, fancy fails — don't add products, add ways to offer the products you already have; it's less about 100 products and more about 100 ways to offer one product. — 8/10

**Why it works:** Every new product adds sourcing, inventory, listing and support load, while a new configuration of an existing product (different quantity, cadence, pairing, or payment structure) adds revenue with no new operational surface. Revenue growth comes from offer permutations, not SKU count.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus the personal-training example (one, two, three, four sessions per week turns one product into many offers) and a summary restatement.

**Fit:** Durable principle class, age irrelevant, and the most load-bearing framing available for this business. A solo operator with 12 SKUs at $2,700/mo whose constraint is retention has no business launching SKU 13; the leverage is in quantity packs, refill cadences, mixed-SKU routines, prepay tiers and subscription configurations built from what already exists. Zero cost, no team, no traffic, no attribution needed, and it is a direct corrective to the most common growth reflex at this size.

### Treat the business as a sequence of offers rather than one sale — if you only sell one thing you don't have a business, you have a front end. Anticipate each problem the customer will hit, make a separate small ask at the moment they feel it, frame each as removing a specific worry or hassle rather than as an upgrade, and move on gracefully when one is declined. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Each ask is an independent yes/no rather than one intimidating bundle, so a decline on one doesn't kill the next; 'so you don't have to worry about X' makes the add-on read as relief from a named annoyance instead of a spend increase. Because the thing you sell most is rarely the thing you profit most on, a sequence of small well-timed offers extracts far more revenue per customer than a single product-page ask, with no additional customers acquired.

**Evidence:** The rental car narrative: a $19/day booking became $100/day across five sequenced offers (roomier vehicle, late return to avoid late fees, premium insurance, minimum-insurance downsell, prepaid fuel above market to avoid a pre-flight fill-up) — a 5x lift; plus the claim that businesses told to add a second offer '5x'd' months later, and the McDonald's fries-and-soda framing. Anecdote, no rates.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction and persuasion framing, so the 2013 setting doesn't decay it. RSC has 12 SKUs but no structured second ask anywhere in the purchase path — no cart cross-sell, no post-purchase offer. Writing cart/checkout add-on copy with hassle-removal framing (a travel size so you don't repack, a two-pack so you don't run out mid-transition) is free and solo-executable on Shopify at the Offer/AOV stage. Capped at 7 because Amazon (~$1,800/mo, the larger channel) offers almost no sequenced-offer surface, at ~54 orders/month the lift will never be statistically readable, and the rental-counter version relies on a human reading the customer live.

### The Rollover Upsell: immediately after a purchase, credit some or all of what the customer just paid toward a longer or larger commitment — 'get your $600 back as credit if you sign up for a year', or apply a $600 first purchase as $50/month off a 12-month membership. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The buyer has already spent the money and has just demonstrated intent, so crediting it costs no new cash out of their pocket and reframes the bigger commitment as recovering a sunk cost rather than incurring a new one. Spread across the term, the credit also makes the longer commitment look cheaper per period than the one-off they just bought, and declining feels like forfeiting money already paid.

**Evidence:** Personal narrative: two-thirds of $600 sign-ups converted into memberships, producing $20,000/mo recurring per gym off a $3,000 lease deposit; plus worked $600 → $50/month examples and the Micro Gyms breakdown. Single-business anecdote.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction principle, age irrelevant, and it attacks the stated binding constraint directly: repeat rate is 18–22.5% while repeat customers are 45–52% of revenue, so converting a first order into a standing refill commitment is worth more than new traffic. Executable solo on Shopify by crediting the first order's value against a refill subscription or prepaid multi-month pack (on Shopify the credit has to be faked with a fixed-amount discount code and tracked by hand). Held at 7 because the credit is real margin given away at a $50.46 AOV on a $2,700/mo operation — it must be funded by a higher total commitment rather than absorbed — and Amazon (~$1,800/mo) cannot run it at all.

### There are exactly four offer types — Attraction (turn strangers into customers), Upsell (get people to spend more), Downsell (turn a no into a yes), Continuity (keep them buying) — and you should audit which ones your business is missing. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Each type fixes a different leak: no attraction offer means too few customers, no upsell means too little cash per customer, no downsell means the no's walk away, no continuity means revenue must be re-won every month. Missing types are literal holes in the money engine.

**Evidence:** Assertion plus the author's claim that his most profitable businesses used all four, with the rental car sequence as the illustrating case. No figures.

**Fit:** Durable principle class; age irrelevant. Useful precisely because it is a coverage checklist a solo operator can run in an hour at zero cost, and it maps cleanly onto a 12-SKU consumable catalog: attraction = the low-COGS lip balm or travel size, upsell = multi-unit or mixed-SKU pack above the $50.46 AOV, downsell = single unit when the bundle is declined, continuity = a refill cadence. Continuity in particular attacks the 18–22.5% repeat rate directly. Not higher because it is a taxonomy, not a mechanism — it tells you what is missing, not what to build, and the existing offer-construction skill already owns the bundle and lead-magnet halves.

### Never sell the same thing for less — when the buyer declines or the price is the objection, lower the price by lowering what they get (fewer units, lower quality, fewer components or support features, shorter duration, or a different item entirely), recombining only products you already carry, and order the removals from highest value to lowest. — 7/10

**Why it works:** A bare discount cuts price while holding value constant, which trains buyers that the original price was fake and permanently resets the anchor. Changing the contents moves price and value together, so the cheaper option is a genuinely different offer rather than an admission of overcharging. Because people weigh money saved against value lost, stripping the single most-valued component first makes the loss feel larger than the savings and pushes many buyers back up to the full offer; limiting downsells to existing inventory gives you a hundred ways to present one catalog instead of a hundred products to stock.

**Evidence:** Dinner-conversation anecdote: a high-ticket seller went from 25/100 closing to 35 buying the main thing plus 40 taking the downsell — tripling close rate — by cutting the money-back guarantee instead of discounting, with buyers saying 'screw it, I'd rather keep the guarantee.' Plus the rental counter's minimum-insurance downsell, the fast-food soda ladder, service examples (drop support calls, keep chat and email), and the warning that inventing new products to downsell creates 'a hundred businesses' worth of problems.'

**Fit:** Durable offer and pricing construction, age irrelevant, free and solo-executable, and it sits in the Offer/AOV stage reachable without ad spend. It is directly corrective for the default reflex of stacking deeper discount codes on the identical SKU in exit-intent popups and abandoned-cart emails: instead, change what's in the cart — single stick instead of the 3-pack, travel size instead of full size, two-SKU pack instead of the full routine, a different scent at the same terms. A 12-SKU catalog gives a large recombination space and the 'use only what you already have' constraint is load-bearing for a solo operator. Capped at 7 because the source's execution is a live objection-handling turn, so on Shopify it becomes a static price ladder plus abandonment emails; at ~54 orders/month no configuration will be cleanly measurable; and offer-construction already establishes 'offer ≠ discount' — the additive piece is the specific move of trading removed units or features for the lower price.

### When refunding, rewarding, or rescuing an unhappy customer, pay it back as store credit toward another — preferably larger or better-fitting — offer rather than as cash: testing showed identical take-up for credit and cash, and the money stays in the business. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Buyers respond to the size of the promised return, not its form, so the conversion effect is the same while the redemption becomes a future order instead of an outflow. A cash refund ends the relationship and books the loss; the same amount credited toward the thing that would actually solve their problem preserves revenue, reframes the failure as insufficient dose or duration rather than a bad product, and often produces a larger total order. Someone asking for a refund has usually rejected one specific outcome, not the whole category.

**Evidence:** Direct claim of A/B experience — 'my testing showed offering store credit and cash back got the same number of customers' — plus that adding an unconditional guarantee on top 'never materially affected' refund requests, the scripted 'act like they won' close crediting the entire deposit, a dentist example ($200 cleaning that didn't whiten credited toward a whitening package), and 'this has saved me tons of customers and cash.' No figures or sample sizes.

**Fit:** Durable pricing and retention logic, no decay, and it is the single detail that makes incentives affordable at $2,700/mo: every credit redeemed is a second transaction landing on the 18–22.5% repeat rate rather than cash out the door. Exceptionally well matched to the known churn cause — a first-time buyer concludes natural deodorant failed during the transition period and asks for their money back; a saved reply that credits their order toward the correct formula, a different scent, or a two-month supply, paired with the transition timeline, converts a refund into a second purchase. Free to implement as a Shopify store-credit/gift-card policy plus a canned response. Not higher because the claim rests on unquantified personal testing, the credit must be offered as a choice rather than a substitute for a legally required refund, the 'free' framing needs a compliance sanity check, and Amazon refunds (~$1,800/mo channel) are platform-controlled so this only fully applies to the ~$875/mo Shopify side.

### Spread earned credit or a discount across the longest commitment the customer will accept — as a per-period or per-order reduction — instead of handing over free product or free months up front, and choose deliberately where the free portion lands (back-loaded, or after the first one to two payments). When giving free time, extend the term rather than eating into it (pay 12 get 15, not pay 9 get 12), and let billing revert to normal rates when the period ends. — 7/10

**Why it works:** The placement of the giveaway determines what behaviour it pays for. A gift given before any payment buys a sign-up and nothing else — customers who pay nothing disengage and churn before ever paying out of pocket. A standing per-period discount, or a gift the customer must make payments to reach, keeps them transacting and makes leaving early forfeit accrued value. The same discount dollars therefore buy far more retained months when stretched over a term, and auto-reverting to full price means the incentive doesn't permanently reprice the customer.

**Evidence:** Origin story: gym members who won $600 back consumed three free months and churned before their first payment, while a peer spreading the same $600 as $50/month off a 12-month membership retained them. Plus four named structures with worked arithmetic ($200/mo with three months free spread as $50/mo) and the assertion that 'people fall off if they don't pay something.' No comparative churn data.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction and retention logic, age irrelevant, and it directly reframes the default 'X% off your first order' into 'your third shipment is free' or '$10 off each of your next three reorders' — the version that pays for exactly the behaviour RSC needs, since the first-to-second order gap is the leak and repeat buyers are 45–52% of revenue. Free to configure as stacked Shopify discount codes or a subscription setting. Held at 7 because the up-front variant explicitly requires enforceable contracts and is unusable here, tracking multi-order credit balances by hand is fiddly at ~54 orders/month, the source context is monthly-billed services so transfer to a physical consumable is by analogy, and any auto-revert must be disclosed plainly to avoid chargebacks at a $50.46 AOV.

### Make the guarantee conditional on the buyer actually doing the thing that produces the result, and tie it to a clear, measurable yes/no outcome you can deliver inside the stated window — rather than offering an unconditional refund. — 7/10

**Why it works:** An unconditional guarantee pays out to people who never used the product; a conditional one both filters those claims and, more importantly, pushes the buyer to complete the behaviour that makes the product work — so fewer people fail, fewer claim, and more reorder. A vague promise invites disputes; a binary one does not. Customers who get results stay, buy again, and tell others, so the offer pays for itself on the back end.

**Evidence:** Speed-reading example where attendance is required to qualify for cancellation, the shoulder-pain 1–10 rating illustration, the rule 'make the criteria what people do to get the most value out of the product', and summary points ('you get loads of up front cash... you get massive results for customers... they advertise your offer'). Assertion, no figures.

**Fit:** Durable offer-construction principle, age irrelevant, and it materially upgrades the guarantee already adopted in offer-construction, which covers naming the fear and placing the promise but not how to condition it. The natural-deodorant version writes itself: 'use it daily through the full 30-day transition period — if you're not drier than your old deodorant by day 30, we refund you.' The condition is exactly the behaviour that causes success, so the guarantee doubles as onboarding pressure against the transition-period churn that drives the 18–22.5% repeat rate. Free to write, lives on the PDP and in an Amazon bullet, needs no traffic or spend. Not higher because Amazon's own return policy overrides any condition on the ~$1,800/mo channel, verifying compliance is manual labour, and the condition must stay honest enough not to read as a refund dodge.

### Advertise a lesser or deliberately stripped-down 'decoy' version of your offer (fewer components, older model, no guarantee), then present the decoy and a maximally loaded premium option side by side with the contrast as large as reasonable, and emphasize the premium — you close the buyer either way. — 7/10

**Why it works:** Perceived value of the premium option is created by comparison, not in isolation. A basic option with visibly fewer components and no guarantee makes the premium read as obviously the better deal, so more buyers self-select up; the cheap version also gets attention and inbound interest cheaply, and buyers who take it are still customers you can upgrade later, so nobody walks.

**Evidence:** The $5 tanning pass to $19.99 membership story; a gym offer where a free one-session-a-week option sat next to a $399 'Ultimate' with a results guarantee, with a reported take rate of 8 of 10 on the $399; worked lemonade, float tank and gym examples; and Gym Launch's Stage I free DIY decoy versus $16,000 done-with-you licensing. Single-business anecdotes, no spend or cohort detail.
