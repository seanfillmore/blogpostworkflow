## Adopted

### Set the free-shipping threshold so the buyer has to add about three average-priced items to reach it (the 'rule of three'), e.g. $75 when the average item is $30. Test shipping cost, because add-to-cart-without-checkout is usually a shipping-cost problem. — 7/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** Amazon has trained shoppers to expect free shipping, so a shipping charge revealed at checkout kills initiated checkouts. A threshold placed a multi-item basket above the typical single-item price turns that expectation into a reason to add units. That lifts AOV instead of giving shipping away on one-item orders.

**Evidence:** Assertion only. It is presented as a standing rule the agency applies.

**Fit:** This sits directly on the Offer/AOV leg. Shipping is what crushes deodorant, soap, toothpaste and lip balm to $2–5 contribution, so a roughly three-item threshold turns those SKUs into basket-builders on a skin-line anchor. RSC's Amazon presence means its buyers carry Prime expectations. Set the number from RSC's own item prices, per-order shipping cost and order-value distribution (mean AOV $54.73, median far lower), not a copied $75. Check add-to-cart → initiated-checkout drop-off first. Runnable today in Shopify settings.

### Announce the free-shipping threshold in a sticky banner at the very top of every page. Shoppers who do not know free shipping exists will not add to cart or start checkout. — 7/10 · parked until `cro`

**Why it works:** Unknown shipping cost is a hidden price objection. Stating the threshold before the shopper reaches the cart removes the fear of a surprise charge and turns the threshold into a visible goal.

**Evidence:** Assertion only ('we say it all the time').

**Fit:** Conversion rate is the binding constraint, and this is a near-zero-cost CRO fix one person can install today. A threshold nobody sees cannot lift AOV. It is not covered by marketing-conversion-friction-audit. Verify the banner on mobile so it does not push the PDP's above-the-fold proof down.

### Send a regular email campaign at least once a week from the start as the baseline. Increase frequency toward daily only as the list grows and revenue warrants more volume. — 7/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** Campaigns are active revenue that flows do not replace. A fixed weekly send keeps the list warm and produces recurring revenue from subscribers and buyers. The habit is easier to build early, and tying frequency increases to revenue avoids burning the list.

**Evidence:** Assertion with open-rate arithmetic (~20% open rate means subscribers see only a fraction of sends). Brands entering their coaching programme often send none. Regular campaigns were among the foundations in the client turnaround.

**Fit:** Repeat customers are 45–52% of RSC revenue, and a standing weekly campaign is the cheapest lever on that revenue. One person can run it today, at any list size. No existing skill records a cadence floor. The open-rate argument is weaker than claimed, because the same engaged minority opens repeatedly. Read frequency increases against unsubscribe and complaint rates per send rather than assuming they are safe.

### Graduate test winners into a separate validation campaign (Advantage+ Sales) at 2x target CPA with a $50/day minimum, and require validation to be profitable before anything moves to production. — 7/10 · parked until `traffic`

**Why it works:** A $3-per-ad test result is too shallow to trust. Spending about two acquisitions' worth per day shows whether the asset 'has legs' at depth. The profitability requirement stops unproven assets from absorbing production budget.

**Evidence:** An example client running two $50/day validation campaigns at $12 CPA and 4.5 ROAS.

**Fit:** This supplies RSC's missing graduation rule from the $50/day test campaign. It is runnable today: a skin-line target CPA of about $25–40 gives $50–80/day. Re-check the existing claim that Advantage+ Shopping needs about $100/day and about 20 creatives against the renamed Advantage+ Sales. Do not run cold-paid validation on the $2–5 contribution SKUs.

### Size the production campaign budget at a minimum of 10x target CPA per day ($250/day floor), because Meta wants about 50 conversions in a 7-day window to optimise. — 7/10 · parked until `traffic`

**Why it works:** A budget that funds 10 target-CPA conversions a day gives about 70 a week, which clears learning-phase exit with margin. Accounts that jump an unproven ad straight to $200–2,000/day 'crash and burn' because they skipped the earlier tiers.

**Evidence:** Agency experience; some accounts run 50–100x target CPA.

**Fit:** This is directly on the $1M path. At a ~$30 target CPA, 10x is about $300/day. Milestones: $60/day now, $100/day once a creative clears validation, then $250–300/day once validation runs profitably at 2x CPA. Existing skills cover the learning-phase freeze but not sizing from CPA × 10.

### Run a 'promo sprint' every four weeks: a 1–3 day promotion to the email list tied to a holiday, product launch or other occasion, to turn non-buyers into buyers and get existing buyers to buy again. — 7/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** A short, dated, occasion-tied deal produces a predictable monthly cash injection from the owned list at near-zero acquisition cost. That revenue pulls ad spend as a share of revenue back down while paid scales.

**Evidence:** Part of the client turnaround: a monthly promo was among the foundations credited with moving the brand to $8–10K profit months. Not isolated per tactic.

**Fit:** This hits retention and the funding problem at once: owned-list cash offsets live Meta spend. One person can run it today. The new parts beyond marketing-scarcity-urgency-framing are the fixed four-week cadence, the 1–3 day window and the ad-spend offset job. Build offers around skin-line items or sets, not deep cuts on $2–5 contribution SKUs.

### Govern paid spend as a percentage of total revenue. When that ratio is too high, pull ad spend back and install the conversion and retention foundations before scaling again, even if top-line revenue drops ('go slow to go fast'). — 7/10 · parked until `scale`

**Why it works:** Total ad spend over total revenue shows whether the whole business profits from its paid spend, which campaign-level ROAS hides. Cutting to a sustainable ratio stops the bleed. The foundations then raise the revenue each ad dollar produces, so later scaling compounds profit instead of loss.

**Evidence:** Client case: $200K/month at about a $20K loss with ad spend at 50% of revenue. They pulled back to $50K with $8K profit, then to $36K with about $10K profit at 30% spend, projected at $80–100K on the rebuilt foundation.

**Fit:** A whole-business spend-ratio governor the $1M plan needs. It is not recorded in marketing-paid-media-measurement, and it complements the per-customer breakeven-first-purchase gate. It is runnable today as a monthly computation. RSC sits at roughly 67% today ($60/day against about $2,700/mo). Read that as the signal for which foundations to raise next, not as a cap. Set RSC's own ceiling from contribution margin rather than copying 30%.

### Install a dedicated cart drawer app (Monster Cart or similar) in place of the stock Shopify theme cart. Use it to carry upsells, order-value bumps and a stacked spend-to-unlock ladder: free shipping first, then a bonus or discount tier, shown as an 'add X to unlock' prompt. — 6/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** Stock theme carts are weak and cannot carry offers. In the cart, the buyer has already committed. A visible next-tier reward with the gap to reach it gives a concrete reason to add one more item, which raises AOV without a new page and before paid traffic is scaled.

**Evidence:** Part of a client turnaround case: installing the Monster Cart drawer was one of the foundations credited with moving a brand from a loss to profit. Results were not isolated per tactic. The hosts openly favour Monster Cart, so vet the vendor recommendation for motive.

**Fit:** AOV lift is the second leg of RSC's engine, and conversion is its binding constraint. A cart drawer with an unlock ladder is a solo, one-afternoon install. It is also where the free-shipping progress bar and the $2–5 contribution retention SKUs (lip balm, soap) belong, as basket-builders rather than cold sells. Part of this is already covered: marketing-upsell-offer-design owns what to offer in the cart, and marketing-offer-construction has a threshold free gift. The new piece is a cart surface that can carry a stacked ladder at all. Before paying for an app, check whether RSC's current theme cart already supports a threshold bar and add-ons. Margin caution: prefer a free low-cost gift or skin-line sample as the unlock over a percentage tier, which can erase margin on a basket of low-contribution SKUs.

**Target skill:** `marketing-upsell-offer-design` (edit)

### Offer 15% off in the email-capture popup rather than 10%. The popup offer is a priority foundation, because one tweak to it can add thousands in revenue. — 6/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** A 10% incentive is too small to move a first-time visitor to hand over an email and then buy. 15% clears the 'worth acting on' threshold and produces far more downstream revenue through the welcome flow.

**Evidence:** The creator's own test: about 3x more revenue from 15% than from 10%, with no sample size, margin or timeframe given. A separate anecdote: one consulting client added $5,000 in one day from an unnamed popup tweak.

**Fit:** The popup is a live capture surface, so this is a one-person setting edit that is runnable today. It also feeds the pre-purchase nurture that retention depends on. The evidence is weak: a single unreported test from apparel. A deeper discount costs real contribution on the low-margin catalogue, so aim the offer at skin-line or set purchases where $15–25 contribution absorbs it. It is also in tension with marketing-offer-construction's note that 10–20% discounts change little. Record 15% as the default popup depth with that caveat.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Run the entry product with no offer first. Layer an acquisition offer only when the best product's no-offer return lands around 2–2.5x ROAS. If it already returns strongly at full price (their example: 4.5x), skip the offer entirely. — 6/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** A discount spends margin. If the product converts profitably at full price, an offer only gives margin away. Offers are the lever for when the no-offer baseline is too weak on volume or CPA.

**Evidence:** Anecdote: their own t-shirt brand reached 4.5 ROAS on day five at $100/day with zero offer. The 2–2.5x trigger is assertion only.

**Fit:** A durable offer-construction principle that fits RSC's route. The skin line carries $15–25 contribution, so it can be judged at full price first, which makes the offer a deliberate response rather than a default. Two corrections apply. First, measure the trigger against breakeven-on-first-purchase CAC, not a bare ROAS multiple. Second, a single week's ROAS at ~16 orders/month is directional only. It is runnable now as a written decision rule on the live creative-test campaign.

**Target skill:** `marketing-offer-construction` (edit)

### Run low-fidelity Meta tests as an ordered dependency chain: product first, then offer, then creative, then message/USP. Each later test's creative carries the earlier winners. Add the message test only when the product needs education or a USP callout. Visual commodities like t-shirts need only creative testing. — 6/10 · parked until `traffic`

**Why it works:** Each layer constrains the next. Creative built before the winning product and offer are known tests the wrong thing. Cheap tests at about $3 per ad sort many options before real money is spent, and every later test inherits the proven layers beneath it. For products that require education, the differentiator decides the sale, so messaging becomes the variable to test.

**Evidence:** Their agency practice and a t-shirt worked example: designs, quirky overlay text, PDP description as primary text, product name as headline.

**Fit:** Natural deodorant and skin care are exactly the 'needs education / call out the USPs' case. Aluminium-free and ingredient differentiation are the message layer. The ordering fits RSC's route: skin line (product), then AOV offer, then creative, then message. It is runnable inside the existing $50/day creative-test campaign. The existing skill covers messaging pillars and 3-2-2, but not this ordering.

### Before layering offers or creative tests, run low-fidelity product testing on Meta across the most distinctive SKUs to find the single entry product to hammer. — 5/10 · parked until `scale`

**Why it works:** You cannot build an offer or creative programme until you know where traffic goes. A quick multi-product test surfaces the SKU that converts profitably with no help, and that SKU becomes the entry point.

**Evidence:** Anecdote: their own t-shirt brand at $100/day found a winner returning 4.5 ROAS by day five. The 'low fidelity campaign' and 'ad lab' method is sold as training and not specified.

**Fit:** A durable principle, but the creator's selector (front-end ROAS) conflicts with marketing-offer-construction, which picks by first-time-buyer repeat rate. Test only skin-line entry SKUs, screen first on front-end CPA against breakeven, and let repeat rate make the final call. Parked behind scale: the trigger is enough daily budget to give each candidate SKU roughly 10+ purchases in its cell (or about $100/day).

### Run a sunset flow. After about 120 days of no engagement, send a short re-engagement series with a one-click unsubscribe, then remove anyone who still does not engage. — 5/10 · parked until `offer-aov`

**Why it works:** Dead subscribers inflate the ESP's subscriber-based bill, so pruning them keeps the paid list to engaged people. A last-chance series rescues some first.

**Evidence:** Assertion only. The only rationale given is the cost of the email tool.

**Fit:** Durable list hygiene. It is directly relevant to the 1,447 giveaway leads that produced no buyers, and it complements marketing-email-list-health. Deliverability is the stronger reason, though the source does not argue it. Key inactivity on clicks, site visits and orders, not opens, because Apple MPP inflates opens. Never sunset past purchasers on email inactivity alone; route them to win-back. Runnable today.

### Score each workstream on resource cost and profit impact, and do high-impact, low-resource work first (website, then email flows, then ads). Spend the idle time ads create, and all remaining time once foundations are built, on slow-compounding organic social and audience building. — 5/10 · parked until `traffic`

**Why it works:** Ads front-load effort, then need near-zero upkeep, but traffic stops when spend stops (a faucet). Organic never gets cheaper to produce, but its impact compounds with audience size (a trench). Filling the review-wait windows with organic builds an owned audience without starving the predictable channel.

**Evidence:** Their own business grown on ads and email; clients adding about $20K/month from social once ads ran hands-off.

**Fit:** A useful prioritisation rule for a solo operator budgeting his own hours. RSC already posts organic daily, so the takeaway is to schedule organic production into the windows when ads are frozen for learning, not to defer it. acquisition-channel-selection has no impact-versus-resource scoring.
