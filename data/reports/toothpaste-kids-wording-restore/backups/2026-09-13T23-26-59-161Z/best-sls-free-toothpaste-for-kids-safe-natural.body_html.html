What Is SLS and Why Does It Matter for Kids?

Sodium lauryl sulfate is a surfactant — a cleansing and foaming agent borrowed from the detergent industry and added to toothpaste to create lather. That foam feels satisfying. But foam has nothing to do with how well a toothpaste cleans. The cleaning comes from brushing mechanics, mild abrasives, and active ingredients like fluoride or hydroxyapatite. SLS is just along for the ride.

Kids are more vulnerable to SLS than adults for a few reasons. Their oral mucosa — the soft tissue lining the mouth — is thinner and more sensitive. They also swallow more toothpaste than adults, especially toddlers. And they are more prone to aphthous ulcers, the painful canker sores that many parents just assume are normal.

They may not be. A frequently cited clinical study by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) in Acta Odontologica Scandinavica found that participants using SLS-containing toothpaste experienced significantly more aphthous ulcers than those using SLS-free formulas. When they switched to toothpaste without SLS, canker sore frequency dropped. For kids who already get mouth sores, that finding matters a lot.

There is another issue worth knowing: SLS temporarily disrupts taste perception by breaking down phospholipids on the tongue that suppress bitter taste. That is why orange juice tastes awful right after brushing. For kids, this effect can make toothpaste taste unpleasant and create a negative association with the whole routine. What to Look for in a Kids' SLS-Free Toothpaste

Before we get to specific picks, here is your label-reading cheat sheet. These are the five things to check on any kids' toothpaste — natural or otherwise.

  • Cleaning agents: SLS is out. But what replaces it? The gentlest option is no surfactant at all — just mild abrasives (like baking soda) and brushing action. If a formula does use a surfactant, look for sodium cocoyl glutamate or cocamidopropyl betaine, which are far gentler on oral tissue.
  • Fluoride vs. fluoride-free: This is a separate decision from SLS-free. The American Dental Association recommends fluoride toothpaste for cavity prevention starting at first tooth eruption. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises consulting a dentist about fluoride use under age 2. If you prefer fluoride-free, nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) is a strong alternative — a 2019 systematic review found it effective at remineralizing enamel and comparable to fluoride in cavity prevention for early lesions. When in doubt, ask your pediatric dentist.
  • Sweeteners: Xylitol is the gold standard. A Cochrane review found xylitol may reduce cavity-causing bacteria. Stevia is a natural, safe alternative. Avoid saccharin and other artificial sweeteners.
  • Flavors and dyes: Natural fruit flavors derived from real extracts are fine. Artificial dyes and synthetic flavor compounds are not — they add nothing useful and some kids react to them.
  • Other ingredients to skip: Parabens, triclosan, and artificial colors have no place in a kids' toothpaste. How to Make the Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste for Your Kids

The transition is easier than most parents expect. Here is what to know going in:

  • Expect less foam: This is the biggest adjustment. Kids (and parents) have been trained to equate bubbles with clean. They are not. Remind your child that the brushing is doing the work, not the lather.
  • Let them pick the flavor: Autonomy increases compliance. If they chose it, they are more likely to use it.
  • Replace the whole tube at once: Do not alternate or mix. Taste adaptation happens faster with consistency.
  • Use the right amount: Rice-grain size for under 3. Pea-sized for ages 3 and up. More is not better — it just gets swallowed.
  • Keep the brushing basics: Soft bristles, two minutes, twice a day. The toothpaste is one part of the equation.

Frequently Asked Questions About SLS-Free Toothpaste for Kids

Is SLS in toothpaste harmful to children?

SLS is not acutely toxic, but it is associated with oral mucosal irritation and an increased frequency of canker sores in susceptible individuals, including children. It provides no therapeutic cleaning benefit — the Herlofson & Barkvoll study found a clear link between SLS exposure and aphthous ulcer frequency. For kids with sensitive mouths, the case for going SLS-free is strong.

What is the difference between SLS-free and fluoride-free toothpaste?

These are completely separate attributes. SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate foaming agent. Fluoride-free means no fluoride. A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride — or it can be both SLS-free and fluoride-free. Real Skin Care's formula is both. If you specifically need fluoride-free options, that is a different filter to apply when shopping.

At what age should kids start using SLS-free toothpaste?

From the very first tooth. Most natural toothpastes skip SLS by default. There is no reason to wait — and given that toddlers swallow toothpaste regularly, starting with the cleanest possible formula is simply the more cautious choice.

Does SLS-free toothpaste still clean teeth effectively?

Yes. SLS creates foam, but it does not clean teeth. Cleaning happens through mechanical brushing, mild abrasives like baking soda, and active ingredients like fluoride or hydroxyapatite. An SLS-free toothpaste with those components cleans just as well — without the irritation.

Can SLS cause canker sores in kids?

Research suggests it can trigger or worsen them in susceptible individuals. The 1994 Herlofson & Barkvoll study remains a key reference point. Switching to toothpaste without SLS is frequently the first recommendation for children who get frequent mouth sores.

Is coconut oil toothpaste safe for kids?

Organic coconut oil is a gentle base ingredient in Real Skin Care's formula. For young children, ask your pediatric dentist which toothpaste suits their age. It does not foam, does not irritate, and supports a healthy oral environment without synthetic additives.
