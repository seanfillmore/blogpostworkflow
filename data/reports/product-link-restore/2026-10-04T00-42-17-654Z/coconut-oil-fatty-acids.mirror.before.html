Coconut oil's fatty acid profile is what makes it one of the most studied and widely used oils in both traditional medicine and modern clean beauty. Specifically, coconut oil is one of the richest natural sources of medium-chain fatty acids — a class of fats with a unique molecular structure that behaves differently from the long-chain fats found in most other plant oils. Nearly half of coconut oil's fat content is lauric acid alone, a medium-chain fatty acid with well-documented properties that make it especially interesting for skin and personal care.

This guide breaks down every major fatty acid in coconut oil, explains what chain length actually means for your skin, and shows how this science connects to real personal care applications. What Are Fatty Acids and Why Does Chain Length Matter?

Fatty acids are the building blocks of fats. Every fat — whether it's coconut oil, olive oil, or the fat in your skin's own barrier — is made up of chains of carbon atoms with hydrogen atoms attached. The number of carbons in that chain is what scientists call "chain length," and it matters a lot for how a fat behaves.

Think of fatty acids as molecular keys. A short key fits certain locks; a long key fits others. Short-chain fatty acids (fewer than 6 carbons) are water-soluble and volatile. Long-chain fatty acids (more than 12 carbons) are heavier and slower to absorb. Medium-chain fatty acids — the 6 to 12 carbon range — sit in a sweet spot: small enough to absorb relatively quickly, large enough to form a useful film on skin.

Beyond chain length, fats are described as either saturated or unsaturated. A saturated fatty acid has no double bonds between its carbon atoms — every carbon is "saturated" with hydrogen. This makes the molecule straighter and more stable. An unsaturated fatty acid has one or more double bonds, giving it a bent shape and making it more prone to oxidation (going rancid).

Coconut oil is approximately 91% saturated fatty acids (Spiazzi et al., 2023). That number surprises some people. But for topical use, that high saturation is actually an asset — it gives coconut oil remarkable shelf stability and a skin-compatible feel. The cardiovascular concerns you may have heard about saturated fat are tied to dietary intake, not skin application. Those are different conversations entirely.

MCT oil — medium-chain triglycerides — is a closely related concept. It refers specifically to the medium-chain fraction of coconut oil, and we'll come back to it when we discuss fractionated coconut oil below.

The Full Fatty Acid Profile of Coconut Oil

Most articles mention lauric acid and stop there. Here's the complete picture, drawn from peer-reviewed research by Spiazzi et al. (2023) and Boateng et al. (2016):

Lauric acid (C12:0) Saturated Medium ~46–47%
Myristic acid (C14:0) Saturated Long ~18–19%
Palmitic acid (C16:0) Saturated Long ~8–9%
Caprylic acid (C8:0) Saturated Medium ~7–8%
Oleic acid (C18:1) Unsaturated Long ~5–8%
Capric acid (C10:0) Saturated Medium ~5–7%
Stearic acid (C18:0) Saturated Long ~2–3%

What stands out immediately: over 50% of coconut oil's fats are medium-chain (Boateng et al., 2016). That's unusually high. Avocado oil, sesame oil, and grapeseed oil are predominantly long-chain — their fatty acid profiles look very different. Canola oil and vegetable oil are largely long-chain and unsaturated. Coconut oil sits in a category of its own.

Lauric Acid — The Star Fatty Acid in Coconut Oil

Lauric acid (C12:0) makes up approximately 46–47% of coconut oil (Boateng et al., 2016) — and coconut oil is the highest natural source of it. Palm kernel oil comes close, but no common plant oil matches coconut oil's lauric acid concentration.

Its molecular structure is worth understanding. Lauric acid is a 12-carbon chain — medium-length, fully saturated, solid at room temperature. That solid-at-room-temp behavior is what gives virgin coconut oil its familiar white, waxy appearance in the jar.

Lauric acid has been studied extensively for antimicrobial properties. A foundational review by Kabara et al. (1972) identified lauric acid as having notable activity against certain bacteria and viruses among the fatty acids tested. More recent research has continued to explore this property. To be clear: the research describes activity in controlled settings — we're not saying coconut oil treats infections.

Lauric acid also converts to a compound called monolaurin when it contacts certain enzymes. Research published in the Journal of Nutritional Biochemistry (Dayrit, 2015) has explored monolaurin's properties, though the full picture is still being studied. What this means for skin and personal care: these studied properties are part of why lauric acid is an interesting ingredient in formulations like natural deodorant, where odor-causing bacteria are the target, and in oral care contexts like oil pulling.

Oil pulling — swishing oil in your mouth — has a long history in traditional medicine and has been studied in oral hygiene research. The lauric acid content is thought to be a key reason coconut oil is used in this practice.

Caprylic and Capric Acids — The Medium-Chain Workhorses

Caprylic acid (C8:0) and capric acid (C10:0) together make up roughly 12–15% of coconut oil. They're shorter than lauric acid — 8 and 10 carbons respectively — which gives them distinctly different properties. source

These two fatty acids are liquid at room temperature. They absorb faster into skin, leave a lighter feel, and are less likely to sit on the surface than heavier long-chain oils. That lighter molecular weight makes them especially useful in leave-on skin care formulations.

These fatty acids also appear in other carrier oils, but they're uniquely concentrated in coconut-derived oils. That's one reason coconut oil has become such a workhorse ingredient in clean beauty formulations.

Myristic and Palmitic Acids — The Supporting Cast

Myristic acid (C14:0) makes up about 18–19% of coconut oil — a significant portion. It's a longer-chain saturated fatty acid, which means it contributes to coconut oil's rich, occlusive texture. "Occlusive" just means it forms a light seal on skin that slows water loss. That's a useful quality in a moisturizer. source

Palmitic acid (C16:0) sits at around 8–9%. It's one of the most common fatty acids in human skin itself, which is part of why it's generally well-tolerated. It contributes to the emollient, softening feel of coconut oil on skin. source

One honest note: the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health flags myristic and palmitic acids as dietary saturated fats associated with raising LDL cholesterol. That's worth knowing if you eat coconut oil. But it has no bearing on topical use — fatty acids applied to skin are not absorbed systemically in a way that raises blood lipids during normal cosmetic use. These are genuinely two separate conversations, and conflating them doesn't serve anyone.

How Coconut Oil Fatty Acids Benefit the Skin

The science here comes down to three mechanisms.

Emollient effect: Fatty acids physically fill the tiny gaps between skin cells in the outermost skin layer (the stratum corneum). When that layer is intact, skin feels soft and smooth. Coconut oil's mix of medium- and long-chain saturated fats does this job well.

Occlusive barrier: The saturated fat structure creates a light film that reduces transepidermal water loss — that's just the medical term for water evaporating off your skin's surface. Less water loss equals more hydrated skin. This is especially relevant for dry or compromised skin.

Studied antimicrobial properties: As covered above, lauric acid's researched activity against certain microorganisms may play a role in how skin tolerates coconut oil, though this mechanism is still being studied in dermatological contexts.

Research has explored coconut oil's use for dry skin conditions. A peer-reviewed clinical study by Evangelista et al. (2014) examined the effect of topical virgin coconut oil on SCORAD scores in patients with atopic dermatitis — a dry, inflamed skin condition. The results were favorable. We're not saying coconut oil treats eczema. But the science does support its use as a moisturizing ingredient for dry skin.

Fractionated vs. Whole Coconut Oil — Does the Fatty Acid Profile Change?

Yes, significantly. This is one of the most misunderstood distinctions in natural skin care.

Fractionated coconut oil (FCO) has been processed — through hydrolysis and steam distillation — to remove the long-chain fatty acids. What's left is primarily caprylic acid (C8) and capric acid (C10). Because those are liquid at room temperature, FCO stays liquid even in the fridge. It's lighter, absorbs quickly, and has a nearly indefinite shelf life.

Here's the key trade-off: fractionated coconut oil contains virtually no lauric acid. That means you lose the fatty acid most studied for antimicrobial properties. FCO and MCT oil are essentially the same thing — both are C8/C10 dominant.

Whole coconut oil retains the full spectrum: lauric acid, myristic, palmitic, and all the rest. That's why it's solid at room temperature (lauric acid has a higher melting point). It also has a higher smoke point, which matters in cooking — but not in topical use, where heat isn't a factor.

For skin care, the choice depends on what you want. Fractionated absorbs faster and feels lighter. Whole coconut oil delivers more lauric acid. How Coconut Oil Compares to Other Natural Oils

Context helps here. Most plant oils look nothing like coconut oil at the fatty acid level.

Avocado oil is dominated by oleic acid (C18:1) — a long-chain monounsaturated fat. It's heavier, slower to absorb, and has a very different skin feel. Excellent for dry skin, but a completely different fatty acid category.

Sesame oil is a mix of oleic and linoleic acids — both long-chain, both unsaturated. Rich in antioxidants, but again, no meaningful medium-chain content.

Grapeseed oil is high in linoleic acid (C18:2), a polyunsaturated long-chain fat. Lightweight and dry-feeling, but its fatty acid profile is essentially the opposite of coconut oil.

Pumpkin seed oil is similarly polyunsaturated — mostly linoleic and oleic acids.

The bottom line: no common plant oil comes close to coconut oil's lauric acid content or its concentration of medium-chain saturated fatty acids. That's not a value judgment — each oil has its own useful properties. But it does explain why coconut oil occupies a unique place in natural skin care formulations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What fatty acids are in coconut oil?

Coconut oil's main fatty acids are lauric acid (~46–47%), myristic acid (~18–19%), palmitic acid (~8–9%), caprylic acid (~7–8%), and capric acid (~5–7%), plus smaller amounts of oleic acid and stearic acid. Together, over 50% of its fats are medium-chain — unusually high for a plant oil.

Is lauric acid in coconut oil good for skin?

Research suggests lauric acid has useful properties for skin care. It has been studied for antimicrobial activity and contributes to coconut oil's emollient feel. A clinical study by Evangelista et al. (2014) found virgin coconut oil beneficial for dry, sensitive skin. It doesn't treat any skin condition — but the research on its properties is solid.

What is the difference between coconut oil and MCT oil?

MCT oil is fractionated coconut oil — it's been processed to keep only the medium-chain caprylic (C8) and capric (C10) acids, removing the long-chain fats and almost all of the lauric acid. Regular (whole) coconut oil retains the full fatty acid profile, including the lauric acid that makes up nearly half its composition. MCT oil is liquid at room temperature and lighter in feel; whole coconut oil is solid and richer.

Is coconut oil high in saturated fat?

Yes — approximately 91% of coconut oil's fatty acids are saturated (Spiazzi et al., 2023). In a dietary context, that's a legitimate concern worth discussing with your doctor. In a topical context, that high saturation gives coconut oil stability, shelf life, and a skin-compatible texture — the cardiovascular implications of dietary saturated fat don't apply to what you put on your skin.

Can coconut oil fatty acids clog pores?

Coconut oil is generally considered moderately to highly comedogenic — meaning it has a meaningful potential to clog pores in some people, particularly those with acne-prone or oily skin. This is widely cited in cosmetic dermatology literature and varies by individual skin type and formulation. If your skin breaks out easily, patch-test coconut oil products on a small area before applying them to your face. Body skin is typically less reactive.
