What Is SLS and Why Does It Cause Sensitivity?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — also called SLS — is a synthetic surfactant added to toothpaste to create foam. That's it. The foam doesn't clean your teeth any better. It's a cosmetic effect, and many people have been conditioned to associate foam with clean. For a deeper look at this ingredient, see our post on what sodium lauryl sulfate is and why to avoid it.

The sensitivity issue comes from what SLS does to your mouth's protective lining. The oral epithelium — the thin mucosal layer coating the inside of your cheeks, gums, and lips — acts as a barrier between your tissues and the outside world. SLS disrupts this barrier by stripping away the protective proteins that hold it together. Once that lining is compromised, underlying tissue is exposed to irritants, bacteria, and friction.

The result? Canker sores (clinically called recurrent aphthous ulcers), tissue peeling, burning sensations, and general mouth soreness. Research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine found a statistically significant reduction in canker sore frequency when participants switched from SLS-containing toothpaste to an SLS-free formula — a finding that points clearly to SLS as a contributing trigger.

It's worth distinguishing between SLS sensitivity and a true SLS allergy. Sensitivity is an irritant response — your tissue reacts because SLS is chemically disruptive. Allergy is immune-mediated, involving your body's immune system identifying SLS as a threat. Both are real. Both are good reasons to switch. But most people experiencing oral discomfort from SLS are dealing with sensitivity, not allergy.

Signs You May Have SLS Sensitivity

These symptoms often go unnoticed because people don't think to connect them to toothpaste. But if you recognize several of these, SLS may be the culprit.

  • Recurring canker sores or mouth ulcers: Small, painful sores on the inner cheeks, lips, or under the tongue that keep coming back.
  • Peeling or sloughing tissue: The inner cheek or gum tissue appears to shed thin layers, especially after brushing.
  • Burning sensation when brushing: A stinging or burning feeling during or right after you use toothpaste.
  • Soreness that improves on toothpaste-free days: Skipping toothpaste or using plain water leaves your mouth feeling noticeably better.
  • Taste disturbances: Food tastes metallic or bitter after brushing — SLS is known to temporarily suppress sweetness receptors, which is why orange juice tastes wrong after brushing.
  • Dry or irritated lips: Persistent lip dryness or chapping that doesn't have an obvious cause.
  • General oral sensitivity: A heightened reaction to hot, cold, or acidic foods even when teeth appear healthy.

Many people live with these symptoms for years without realizing their toothpaste is the source. Switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is consistently the first step dentists and oral health professionals recommend when patients present with unexplained recurring mouth sores.

What to Look for in a Toothpaste for SLS Sensitivity

Reading a toothpaste label isn't always straightforward. SLS appears under several names — look for sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or sodium laurilsulfate in the ingredients list. If you see any of those, the product contains SLS.

Beyond avoiding SLS, here's what makes a toothpaste genuinely good for a sensitive mouth:

  • SLS-free formula: The baseline. No SLS or its variants anywhere in the ingredient list.
  • Gentle abrasive system: High-abrasion formulas (measured by a Relative Dentin Abrasivity score, or RDA) can worsen sensitivity. Look for mild abrasives like baking soda, which cleans effectively without excessive scrubbing action.
  • Soothing or remineralizing actives: Baking soda supports a balanced oral pH and gently buffers acid. Nano hydroxyapatite toothpaste — a newer fluoride-free remineralization option — uses synthetic calcium phosphate to rebuild enamel at a microscopic level, and it's gaining clinical support as a gentle alternative.
  • Free from artificial dyes and synthetic flavors: These are secondary irritants for sensitive mouths. Natural flavors from essential oils (like peppermint or spearmint) are better tolerated by most people.
  • Fluoride or fluoride-free, per your preference: Both options exist SLS-free. Some people prefer the reassurance of fluoride; others choose a fluoride-free toothpaste for personal or lifestyle reasons. Neither choice is wrong, and SLS-free versions of both exist.

Frequently Asked Questions About SLS Sensitivity and Toothpaste

Is SLS in toothpaste safe?

For most people, SLS causes no noticeable problems. But for those with sensitivity, it disrupts the oral epithelium — the mucosal lining of the mouth — which can trigger canker sores, tissue peeling, and burning. Safe for the general population doesn't mean safe for everyone.

What is the difference between SLS sensitivity and a toothpaste allergy?

SLS sensitivity is an irritant response — the chemical directly disrupts mucosal tissue in a dose-dependent way. A true SLS allergy is immune-mediated, meaning your immune system mounts a reaction to SLS as if it were a threat. Both are valid reasons to switch to an SLS-free toothpaste.

Can SLS toothpaste cause canker sores?

Yes. Multiple studies point to a link between SLS and recurrent aphthous ulcers. Research in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine found that switching to SLS-free toothpaste significantly reduced canker sore frequency. If you get frequent mouth ulcers, SLS is worth investigating as a cause.

Is fluoride-free toothpaste okay for sensitive teeth?

Yes. Nano hydroxyapatite toothpaste is a fluoride-free remineralization option with growing clinical support. It uses calcium phosphate — the same mineral already in your enamel — to help repair surface damage. Whether you go fluoride or fluoride-free is a personal choice. Both options exist SLS-free. How quickly will symptoms improve after switching to SLS-free toothpaste?

Most people notice improvement within one to three weeks. The oral mucosa regenerates fairly quickly once the irritant is gone. Canker sore frequency usually drops first, followed by reduced burning and general sensitivity. Results vary, but many people feel a difference within the first week.
