What Makes a Toothpaste Truly "All Natural"?

Here's the honest answer: there's no legal definition for "natural" on a toothpaste label. The FDA doesn't regulate the term in personal care products, and neither does any single body with binding authority. That means a brand can call its toothpaste "natural" even if it contains synthetic detergents or artificial flavors.

So what should you actually look for? Genuinely natural toothpaste is built on plant-derived or mineral-based ingredients, contains no synthetic surfactants (more on SLS in a moment), skips artificial colors and flavors, and ideally carries a third-party certification. The most credible ones include EWG Verified, USDA Organic, and NATRUE. Those certifications require documented ingredient sourcing — they're not just marketing badges.

It helps to know the spectrum. "Natural" is the broadest claim. "Organic" means the ingredients were grown without synthetic pesticides. "Clean" is a values-based term that usually means free from a specific list of flagged chemicals. And "vegan toothpaste" simply means no animal-derived ingredients — which most natural formulas already avoid. Ingredients to Look For in the Best Natural Toothpaste

Think of this as your label-reading checklist. These are the ingredients that earn their place in a high-quality natural formula:

  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate): A mild, mineral-based abrasive that helps lift surface stains and neutralize acids in the mouth. Gentle enough for daily use and well-studied for plaque control.
  • Xylitol: A plant-derived sweetener that does more than sweeten. Research published on PubMed indicates xylitol inhibits the growth of Streptococcus mutans — the primary bacteria linked to tooth decay.
  • Coconut oil: A staple in coconut oil toothpaste formulas for its natural antimicrobial properties. The active component (lauric acid) has been shown in studies to disrupt bacterial cell membranes, supporting a cleaner oral environment.
  • Nano-hydroxyapatite: A naturally occurring mineral that makes up most of your tooth enamel. Emerging clinical research suggests it may help support enamel health — a 2019 systematic review found it comparable to fluoride in some remineralization measures.
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, clove, cinnamon): Natural breath fresheners with antimicrobial properties. Clove oil in particular contains eugenol, which has documented antibacterial activity in oral health research.
  • Myrrh: A wildcrafted resin used historically for oral hygiene, myrrh has demonstrated antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties in several studies — making it a genuinely functional natural ingredient, not just a feel-good add-on.

Ingredients to Avoid (Even in "Natural" Brands)

Some ingredients show up in products marketed as natural. It's worth knowing what they are before you buy:

  • Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS): The synthetic detergent that creates foam. SLS has no cleaning function — it's purely cosmetic. A peer-reviewed study in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine found that SLS-containing toothpastes were associated with higher rates of recurrent mouth ulcers in sensitive individuals. SLS-free toothpaste simply skips it — and your mouth won't miss it.
  • Artificial sweeteners (saccharin, aspartame): Common in conventional formulas to improve taste without sugar. Neither belongs in a toothpaste with natural ingredients.
  • Triclosan: An antimicrobial agent the FDA ruled against for use in consumer antiseptic washes. It was voluntarily removed from most toothpastes, but worth confirming it's absent from any formula you're considering.
  • Carrageenan: A seaweed-derived thickener with mixed research on gut inflammation. It's controversial enough that many clean-brand formulas skip it entirely.
  • Titanium dioxide: Used as a whitening agent. The European Food Safety Authority flagged concerns about nano-grade titanium dioxide in food products — and while toothpaste isn't swallowed, cautious formulators leave it out.

The Fluoride Question: What Natural Toothpaste Users Need to Know

Fluoride is the most debated ingredient in oral care. Here's a fair read of the evidence. The American Dental Association endorses fluoride as an effective cavity-prevention mineral with decades of research behind it. The CDC includes community water fluoridation among the 10 great public health achievements of the 20th century.

At the same time, many people choosing natural toothpaste simply prefer to avoid fluoride — for a variety of personal reasons. That's a valid choice, and it doesn't mean your teeth are left unprotected. Research suggests that ingredients like xylitol and nano-hydroxyapatite can support enamel health in fluoride-free formulas. But if you have a history of cavities or high decay risk, it's worth talking to your dentist before making the switch to a natural fluoride-free toothpaste. source

5. Best Organic Toothpaste (USDA / Certified Organic Options)

"Natural" and "certified organic" are not the same thing. USDA Organic certification requires that at least 95% of the ingredients were grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers — and the certification process is rigorous and documented. Truly USDA-certified organic toothpaste is rare, largely because some necessary ingredients (like water or mineral-based abrasives) don't fall cleanly into organic certification categories. NATRUE certification is another credible benchmark to look for in natural organic toothpaste. When evaluating any organic toothpaste without fluoride, check whether the certification applies to the full formula or just select ingredients — the label should tell you which. source

6. Best Vegan Natural Toothpaste

Most all natural toothpaste is already vegan, but it's not automatic. The ingredient to watch is glycerin — it can be derived from either plant sources or animal tallow, and most labels don't specify. Look for "vegetable glycerin" on the label, or confirm with the brand directly. Carmine (a red dye from crushed beetles) occasionally appears in flavored dental products, so it's worth checking if you're avoiding animal-derived ingredients entirely. Cruelty-free certification (Leaping Bunny or PETA's Beauty Without Bunnies) is a separate but related standard that confirms no animal testing took place anywhere in the supply chain. A vegan toothpaste should clearly state its glycerin source and carry cruelty-free documentation if that matters to you.

How to Switch to Natural Toothpaste Without Hating the Experience

The most common complaint from people trying natural toothpaste for the first time: it doesn't foam like they expected. That's not a sign it's not working. SLS is the ingredient responsible for foam in conventional toothpaste — it has no actual cleaning function. It's purely there to make the experience feel familiar. When you remove it, the toothpaste still cleans your teeth. It just doesn't lather.

Here are four things that make the transition easier:

  • Give it two weeks: Your taste expectations will reset. Most people adjust completely within 10–14 days.
  • Focus on brushing technique: Natural toothpaste works best with proper brushing — two minutes, gentle circular motion, full coverage. The paste does less of the heavy lifting; technique matters more.
  • Pair it with a natural mouthwash: If you're missing the "clean" sensation, a simple diluted essential oil rinse can fill that gap while you adjust.
  • Store it properly: Some natural formulas can separate over time. Give the squeeze bottle a quick shake if you notice the texture changing — it's normal and not a sign of spoilage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is all natural toothpaste as effective as conventional toothpaste?

Yes — when it's formulated with proven ingredients. Baking soda is well-documented for plaque control. Xylitol inhibits cavity-causing bacteria. Coconut oil and myrrh have antimicrobial properties supported by research. The key is looking past the "natural" label and reading the actual ingredients. A well-formulated all natural toothpaste can clean your teeth effectively without synthetic detergents or artificial additives.

What is the healthiest toothpaste you can use?

The healthiest toothpaste is one free of SLS, artificial sweeteners, and triclosan — and built on ingredients that actually support oral health. Can I use fluoride-free natural toothpaste and still prevent cavities?

For many people, yes — especially when paired with good brushing habits, xylitol, and a low-sugar diet. That said, if you have active cavities or high decay risk, talk to your dentist before going fluoride-free. Fluoride has a strong evidence base for cavity prevention, and your dentist can help you weigh the options for your specific situation.

What does SLS-free toothpaste mean?

It means the formula contains no sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic detergent that creates foam in most conventional toothpastes. SLS-free toothpaste, also called sulfate-free toothpaste or toothpaste without SLS, is generally gentler on soft tissue. Research suggests SLS may aggravate mouth ulcers in sensitive individuals. Removing it doesn't reduce cleaning effectiveness at all — it just changes the texture and lather. source

Is natural toothpaste vegan?

Most natural toothpastes are vegan, but not always by default. The main thing to check is the source of glycerin — it can come from plant or animal fats. Look for "vegetable glycerin" on the label, or contact the brand to confirm. If cruelty-free matters to you, look for Leaping Bunny or a similar certification alongside the vegan claim.
