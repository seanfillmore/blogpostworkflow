What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate in Toothpaste?

SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate) is a synthetic surfactant — a foaming agent added to toothpaste to create lather and help the paste spread across your teeth. Its job is cosmetic. It makes brushing feel more thorough, but it doesn't do the cleaning itself.

You'll find it in most mainstream toothpastes, plus shampoos, body washes, and facial cleansers. On ingredient labels, it hides under a few names:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate — the most common listing
  • Sodium dodecyl sulfate — the technical chemical name
  • SDS — abbreviation for sodium dodecyl sulfate
  • LSL — occasionally seen in abbreviated ingredient lists

If any of those appear in the ingredient list, the formula contains SLS — regardless of how natural the packaging looks.

Does Toothpaste Have Sodium Lauryl Sulfate? Which Brands Contain It?

Most popular toothpaste brands do contain SLS. Here's a quick breakdown of the most-searched ones:

Does Colgate contain sodium lauryl sulfate?

Yes. Most Colgate formulas — including Colgate Total, Colgate Cavity Protection, and Colgate Whitening — list sodium lauryl sulfate as an ingredient. A small number of specialty Colgate lines are formulated without it, so check the label on each specific product.

Does Crest contain sodium lauryl sulfate?

Yes. Crest's standard lines, including Crest 3D White and Crest Cavity Protection, contain SLS. As with Colgate, individual formulas can vary — always read the full ingredient list rather than relying on the front-of-pack claims.

Does Sensodyne contain sodium lauryl sulfate?

Some Sensodyne formulas do, and some don't. Sensodyne Pronamel and certain Sensodyne Repair & Protect varieties are marketed as SLS-free. But not all Sensodyne products are — the Sensodyne Whitening line, for example, contains SLS. Check the back label for "sodium lauryl sulfate" before assuming a Sensodyne product is sulfate-free.

Does Close Up toothpaste contain sodium lauryl sulfate?

Yes. Close Up is one of the classic SLS-containing toothpastes — the heavy foam it produces is largely a result of its SLS content.

What toothpaste brands avoid sodium lauryl sulfate?

Many natural and clean toothpaste brands have formulated products without SLS. You'll find several options marketed as SLS-free in the natural personal care section. Always check the ingredient label to confirm, since formulas can change over time.

What about Oral-B Densify?

Oral-B Densify toothpaste does contain sodium lauryl sulfate. If you're looking for an SLS-free option from a major brand, Densify is not it.

How to Identify SLS in a Toothpaste Ingredient List

Reading ingredient labels is straightforward once you know what to look for. Here's the short version:

  1. Flip the tube or box to the ingredient panel — not the front claims.
  2. Scan for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium dodecyl sulfate," "SDS," or "LSL."
  3. If any of those appear, the product contains SLS.
  4. Front-of-pack language like "natural" or "gentle" does not mean SLS-free. Check the list.

One common point of confusion: sodium lauroyl sarcosinate is not the same as sodium lauryl sulfate. It's a different surfactant — milder, with a better safety profile and less irritation potential. The EWG rates it with low concern, and it's considered safe in oral care applications. If you see it in an SLS-free toothpaste, it's not a red flag.

Similarly, lauryl glucoside is sometimes used in toothpaste as a gentler alternative to SLS. It's a mild, plant-derived surfactant with a strong oral care safety record — not an SLS alias. Lauryl glucoside in toothpaste is generally considered a cleaner choice than SLS, not a substitute to avoid.

Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate Bad in Toothpaste? What the Research Says

At the low concentrations used in toothpaste, regulatory bodies including the FDA consider SLS generally safe for most people. But "safe at this dose" and "ideal for everyone" are different things.

A peer-reviewed review published in PMC (Sabri et al., PMC10506142) examined the clinical evidence on SLS and oral health. Three findings stand out: source

1. Canker sores

SLS is associated with more frequent and more severe canker sores (recurrent aphthous stomatitis). The likely reason: SLS strips away the thin protective protein layer — called the mucin layer — that lines your mouth. Without it, tissue becomes more vulnerable to ulceration. For people who already get canker sores regularly, this is the most important reason to switch.

2. Bitter taste after brushing

That metallic or bitter taste you get after brushing and then drinking juice? SLS is the most likely cause. It disrupts phospholipids on the surface of your taste buds — specifically the ones responsible for perceiving sweetness. The effect is temporary, but it's chemistry, not imagination. SLS-free formulas don't cause it.

3. Dry mouth irritation

SLS can dehydrate the mucin layer, which makes it worse for people who already have dry mouth. If your mouth regularly feels dry or your gums feel raw after brushing, SLS is worth eliminating first.

SLS does have some antimicrobial activity and has been studied for plaque reduction. It isn't entirely without function. But for people with canker sores, oral sensitivity, or taste disruption, the evidence points clearly toward SLS-free formulas.

These are separate choices. A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride, or it can be both — like Real Skin Care's coconut oil toothpaste, which contains neither. If you want to eliminate both, check the label for each ingredient separately.

Yes. Sodium lauroyl sarcosinate is a different surfactant from SLS — milder and with a better irritation profile. The EWG rates it with low concern. It's a legitimate SLS alternative in toothpaste, not a hidden alias for it.
