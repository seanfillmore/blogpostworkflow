The toothpaste ingredients to avoid most urgently include sodium lauryl sulfate, triclosan, parabens, and titanium dioxide — all of which have been flagged by researchers and regulatory bodies for concerns ranging from oral tissue irritation to hormonal disruption. Why Your Toothpaste Ingredient Label Deserves a Closer Look

The mouth has a highly permeable mucous membrane. That means chemicals are absorbed faster and more directly than through skin — and they don't pass through the digestive system the same way food does. They enter the bloodstream more readily.

This matters when you consider how much toothpaste is swallowed over a lifetime — especially by children. Some ingredients have documented toxicity thresholds, and the question of whether toothpaste is toxic isn't hypothetical. It depends entirely on what's in the tube. Knowing which bad ingredients in toothpaste to avoid is the first step to choosing something genuinely safer.

The Toothpaste Ingredients to Avoid (and Why)

Here's a breakdown of the most concerning ingredients found in conventional toothpastes, what the evidence says about each, and what to look for instead.

Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)

Sodium lauryl sulfate is a synthetic foaming agent added to create lather. The foam feels like it's doing something — but it contributes nothing to cleaning your teeth. It's just there for sensation.

The problems are real, though. SLS is a known oral tissue irritant, and studies have linked it to increased frequency of canker sores in people who are prone to them. It can also disrupt the oral microbiome by stripping away the protective mucous layer in your mouth. Worth noting: SLS is the same compound used in industrial degreasers. If you deal with canker sores or mouth sensitivity, switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is one of the most straightforward changes you can make.

Triclosan

Triclosan is an antibacterial agent that was used in toothpaste to fight gum disease. The FDA banned it from hand soaps in 2016 over safety concerns — but it has continued to appear in some dental products.

The concerns are significant. Triclosan is a suspected endocrine disruptor, meaning it may interfere with hormone signaling. Studies have detected it in human breast milk and urine. There's also evidence it contributes to antibiotic resistance. Check labels carefully — it may still appear in some conventional formulations. It's one of the more dangerous ingredients in toothpaste precisely because it was marketed as a health benefit.

Fluoride — What You Should Know

Fluoride is endorsed by dental associations worldwide for its role in helping with cavities, and that position is backed by decades of research. It's not something to dismiss outright.

That said, legitimate concerns exist. Fluoride is a neurotoxin at high doses, and the side effects of fluoride in toothpaste for young children include dental fluorosis — white spots on developing teeth — when toothpaste is swallowed regularly. There's also ongoing scientific debate about cumulative systemic exposure from multiple daily sources: toothpaste, drinking water, certain foods. Many parents and health-conscious adults choose fluoride-free options as a precautionary measure, particularly for young children. If that's you, our fluoride-free toothpaste collection offers transparent, plant-based alternatives worth exploring.

Parabens

Parabens — including methylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben — are synthetic preservatives used to extend shelf life. They're absorbed through mucous membranes and have been detected in human tissue samples.

Parabens are classified as suspected endocrine disruptors and have faced regulatory scrutiny in the EU. CHEM Trust's research on endocrine-disrupting chemicals in personal care products has highlighted parabens as a category of concern. Natural formulations can achieve adequate preservation without them — so there's no good reason to accept them in a product going into your mouth twice daily.

Propylene Glycol

Propylene glycol is a synthetic humectant — it keeps toothpaste moist and spreadable. It's also found in industrial products, though cosmetic-grade and industrial-grade do differ. Still, even at cosmetic concentrations, it can irritate mucous membranes. At higher exposure levels it has been associated with liver and kidney effects in animal studies. Given how readily the mouth absorbs compounds, natural humectants like vegetable glycerin are a cleaner choice.

Artificial Sweeteners (Saccharin and Aspartame)

These are added purely to make toothpaste taste better. Saccharin has a long history of cancer concern — current consensus puts the risk low at typical exposure, but the concern isn't invented. Aspartame breaks down into compounds including methanol and phenylalanine during digestion.

Neither is necessary. Stevia, a natural plant-derived sweetener, does the same job without the baggage. Our natural toothpaste uses stevia instead. Xylitol is another clean option worth looking for — it actually inhibits the cavity-causing bacteria Streptococcus mutans, making it a functional ingredient, not just a flavor fix.

Titanium Dioxide

Titanium dioxide makes toothpaste look bright white. That's it. It does nothing for your oral health — it's purely cosmetic. The European Food Safety Authority reclassified it as no longer safe as a food additive in 2021, citing genotoxicity concerns with nanoparticle forms. While the picture for topical and oral use is still developing, the precautionary principle applies: if an ingredient serves no function and carries a credible concern, it doesn't belong in your toothpaste.

Diethanolamine (DEA)

DEA is used as a foaming agent and pH adjuster. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified it as a possible human carcinogen. It can also react with other ingredients to form nitrosamines — compounds that are established carcinogens. DEA is already banned or restricted in cosmetics in several countries. It has no place in a clean toothpaste formula.

Artificial Dyes and Colorants

FD&C Blue No. 1, Red No. 40 — these are added for visual appeal only. They have zero oral health benefit. Some synthetic dyes have been linked to hyperactivity in children and are under regulatory review in multiple countries. Toothpaste does not need to be colorful to clean your teeth. Clean formulations skip dyes entirely.

Lead and Heavy Metal Contamination

Lead is not an intentional ingredient — but it has shown up as a contaminant in some toothpastes, particularly in products using inadequately tested mineral ingredients or sourced from manufacturers with less rigorous quality controls. Studies have detected measurable lead levels in some commercial brands. Choose brands that publish third-party testing results and source ingredients transparently. This is a concern most competitor posts overlook entirely.

A Note on Baking Soda Toothpaste

Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is a legitimate natural ingredient with mild abrasive and whitening properties — and it's worth addressing the baking soda toothpaste side effects question directly. Used at appropriate concentrations, it's generally safe and effective. Used too aggressively or too often, it can erode enamel over time because of its abrasive nature.

Baking soda works best as part of a balanced formula — not as a standalone paste used undiluted. Our natural toothpaste includes baking soda alongside coconut oil and essential oils, which helps moderate its abrasiveness while keeping the formula effective.

What Happens When You Use Expired Toothpaste?

Expired toothpaste isn't typically dangerous, but it becomes less effective. Active ingredients — whether fluoride in conventional paste or antibacterial botanicals in natural formulas — lose potency over time. Preservatives can also degrade, which may create conditions for microbial growth. Natural formulations with fewer synthetic preservatives especially benefit from timely use. Check the date and replace it regularly — it's a small habit that actually matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fluoride in toothpaste harmful?

At the concentrations recommended by dental associations, fluoride is considered safe and effective for cavity prevention. The documented concerns involve children swallowing toothpaste regularly — which can cause dental fluorosis, visible as white spots on teeth — and questions about cumulative systemic exposure from multiple daily sources. Many health-conscious adults and parents choose fluoride-free toothpaste as a precautionary approach. The science supports both positions depending on your priorities.

Can toothpaste cause canker sores?

Yes — sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) has been linked to increased canker sore frequency in susceptible individuals. SLS strips the protective mucous lining of the mouth, which may lower the threshold for sore development. Switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is one of the most commonly recommended changes for people who deal with recurring canker sores.

Is there lead in toothpaste?

Not intentionally — but lead contamination has been detected in some toothpaste products, particularly those using inadequately tested mineral ingredients or manufactured with less stringent quality controls. It's not a widespread crisis, but it is a real concern that reinforces the importance of choosing brands with transparent sourcing and third-party testing.
