What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — also listed on labels as sodium dodecyl sulfate or SDS — is a synthetic surfactant. That means it's a foaming and cleansing agent. Originally developed for industrial applications like engine degreasers and floor cleaners, it found its way into personal care products because it's cheap, it lathers well, and consumers associate foam with clean. But foam isn't what cleans your teeth. It's a cosmetic effect, not a functional one.

SLS works by disrupting the lipid bilayer of cells — essentially, it's in the same ingredient class as dish soap. When it contacts the delicate mucosal tissue lining your mouth, it can strip away a protective layer that's supposed to stay intact. For most people, this goes unnoticed. For others, especially those with naturally thinner or more reactive oral tissue, it causes real problems.

It's worth noting that SLS is distinct from SLES — sodium laureth sulfate — though both are worth avoiding in a clean toothpaste formula. SLES is a milder derivative, but it still shares the same synthetic detergent family. For a deeper look at the chemistry, see our explainer on what SLS is and why to avoid it.

What the Research Says About SLS and Oral Health

The research here is more specific than most people realize. This isn't vague "harsh chemical" territory — there are named studies with documented outcomes.

A double-blind crossover study published in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (Herlofson & Barkvoll, 1994) found that participants using SLS-containing toothpaste developed significantly more aphthous ulcers — canker sores — than those using SLS-free toothpaste. The difference was meaningful enough that the researchers concluded SLS-free formulas should be considered for people with recurrent oral ulceration.

Separately, research has shown that SLS temporarily inhibits taste receptor activity — specifically the proteins responsible for detecting sweetness. This is why food can taste bitter or metallic right after brushing. It's not in your head. It's a documented biochemical effect of SLS on your taste buds, and it resolves once the SLS washes out.

The FDA classifies SLS as "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) at low concentrations. That's worth acknowledging honestly: for most healthy adults, low-dose SLS exposure isn't a medical emergency. But "safe at low doses" and "ideal for daily oral use" are two different standards. If you're brushing twice a day, every day, the cumulative contact with mucosal tissue adds up. Who Should Switch to SLS Free Toothpaste?

Switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is a low-effort change with a potentially high payoff for certain people. Here's who tends to benefit most:

  • People with recurrent canker sores: The Herlofson & Barkvoll study showed a direct link between SLS use and aphthous ulcer frequency. If you get canker sores often, this is the first ingredient to remove.
  • People with sensitive gums: If your gums feel sore, irritated, or inflamed after brushing — and your dentist hasn't found another cause — SLS-related mucosal disruption is a likely contributor.
  • Anyone with altered taste after brushing: If breakfast tastes off after you brush, SLS is probably why. Removing it typically resolves the issue within a few weeks.
  • Parents choosing toothpaste for children: Children have thinner, more permeable oral mucosa than adults. An SLS-free formula is often the gentler, more appropriate choice for young brushers.
  • Anyone building a cleaner personal care routine: If you're already reading ingredient labels on your lotion and deodorant, your toothpaste deserves the same scrutiny.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free? Making the Right Call

Many people shopping for SLS-free toothpaste are also deciding on fluoride at the same time. These are two separate ingredient decisions, but they often get made together. Here's an honest breakdown.

Fluoride has decades of clinical evidence supporting its role in cavity prevention. The American Dental Association recommends fluoride-containing toothpaste for most adults, and that position is backed by strong, consistent research. If you have high cavity risk, fluoride is worth discussing with your dentist before switching it out.

That said, some people prefer to go fluoride-free — for personal reasons, for young children where fluorosis (white spotting from excess fluoride in developing teeth) is a concern, or as part of a broader whole-ingredient philosophy. The most researched fluoride-free alternative is nano hydroxyapatite. It's a biomimetic mineral — meaning it mirrors the natural composition of tooth enamel. Research, including work by Limeback and colleagues, has shown that nano hydroxyapatite can support enamel remineralization at appropriate concentrations, with some studies showing comparable results to fluoride formulations. It's not a fringe ingredient — it's been used in Japanese dentistry for decades.

How to Make the Switch to SLS Free Toothpaste

Switching is simple, but a few things are worth knowing upfront so you're not caught off guard.

  • Expect less foam: Foam is cosmetic, not functional. Your teeth can be just as clean — or cleaner — without it. It takes a few days to stop reaching for the lather that isn't coming.
  • Give it 2–4 weeks: If you have existing canker sores or gum irritation, it takes time for mucosal tissue to recover after SLS exposure stops. Don't judge the switch in the first week.
  • Keep your brushing technique consistent: Two minutes, twice daily, soft-bristle brush. The technique matters more than the foam level.
  • Talk to your dentist if you're leaving fluoride behind: Especially if you have active cavities or high decay risk. It's a conversation worth having.
  • Store at room temperature: Coconut oil-based toothpaste may thicken slightly in cold environments. This is completely normal — just let it warm up or hold the squeeze bottle briefly in your hands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for canker sores?

Yes. A double-blind crossover study by Herlofson & Barkvoll, published in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (1994), found that people using SLS-free toothpaste developed significantly fewer canker sores than those using SLS-containing toothpaste. Removing SLS is one of the most evidence-backed steps for people with recurrent oral ulcers.

What does SLS-free mean on toothpaste?

SLS-free means the toothpaste was formulated without sodium lauryl sulfate — a synthetic surfactant used as a foaming agent in most conventional tubes. An SLS-free formula cleans without relying on synthetic detergents, typically using gentler ingredients like coconut oil, mild abrasives, or antimicrobial plant extracts instead.

Can SLS-free toothpaste still clean effectively?

Yes. Cleaning efficacy comes from mild abrasion and antimicrobial action — not foam. Ingredients like organic coconut oil (which contains lauric acid with documented antimicrobial properties), wildcrafted myrrh, and essential oils do the real work. Foam is a cosmetic effect that signals cleaning to consumers but doesn't drive it.

Is SLS-free toothpaste safe for kids?

Yes — and for many children, it's the better choice. Kids have thinner, more permeable oral mucosa than adults, making them more susceptible to the mucosal disruption SLS can cause. A gentle SLS-free formula with natural ingredients is appropriate for daily use and less likely to cause irritation in developing mouths.

What is the difference between SLS-free and fluoride-free toothpaste?

These are two separate ingredient decisions. SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate surfactant. Fluoride-free means no added fluoride compound (such as sodium fluoride or stannous fluoride).
