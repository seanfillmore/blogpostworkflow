What Is Castile Soap? (Simple Definition)

Castile soap is a vegetable oil-based soap — no animal fats, no synthetic surfactants. Surfactants are the foaming and cleaning agents added to many soaps and washes. The harsh ones are often made in a lab. It gets its name from the Castile region of Spain, where craftspeople historically made soap almost entirely from locally abundant olive oil.

Today, the castile soap definition has broadened. It is generally understood as soap made from plant-derived oils rather than animal fats or synthetic surfactants. Modern versions often blend coconut oil, hemp seed oil, or jojoba oil alongside olive oil. The core identity stays the same: plant oils in, synthetic chemicals out.

What it's not: conventional bar soap. Most standard soaps are made with animal-derived tallow or contain harsh foaming agents like sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) and sodium laureth sulfate (SLES). Castile soap skips both.

What Is Castile Soap Made Of?

It starts with just three simple things. Soapmakers combine a vegetable oil, an alkali (a strong base, often called lye), and water. Here's what each one does.

  • Vegetable oil base: Traditionally olive oil. Modern castile-style soaps often use coconut oil for better lather, or blends of hemp seed, jojoba, or other vegetable oils. The oil affects hardness, lather, and skin feel.
  • An alkali (lye): This is a strong base that turns oil into soap. Bar soap uses sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Liquid soap uses potassium hydroxide (KOH). Sodium hydroxide makes a hard bar; potassium hydroxide makes a pourable liquid.
  • Water: Dissolves the alkali and starts the reaction.

The lye concern is common — but unfounded in finished soap. When oil and lye combine through saponification, they transform completely into soap and glycerin. Saponification is just the word for that reaction. No lye remains in the finished product. The glycerin byproduct is what gives the soap its naturally moisturizing quality.

Scented versions add essential oils. Lavender, peppermint, tea tree, and citrus are common examples. The ingredient list should always be short and recognizable.

Our bar is made with coconut oil, not olive oil. Strictly speaking, that makes it a coconut-based, castile-style soap rather than traditional castile. The finished bar contains saponified organic virgin coconut oil, plus organic essential oil of lavender, tea tree, or lemongrass, depending on the scent you pick. The lye and water do their job during soapmaking and don't remain as ingredients. That's it.

What Is the pH of Castile Soap — and Why Does It Matter?

Castile soap has a pH between 8.9 and 10. Your skin sits at pH 4.5 to 5.5. That gap is worth understanding.

Your skin's acid mantle is a thin, slightly acidic protective layer that holds moisture in and keeps bacteria out. Washing with something on the high end of the pH scale (the opposite of acidic) temporarily disrupts it. For most people, the skin bounces back within a few hours. But if your skin is dry, sensitive, or eczema-prone, that disruption can cause tightness, flakiness, or irritation.

Castile Soap Uses and Benefits (Everyday Guide)

One of the best things about castile soap is its versatility. Here's a practical breakdown of the most common uses, with dilution tips where they matter.

  • Hand soap: This is where it shines. Use a bar at the sink, or work up a lather with a wet washcloth. Our coconut oil bar soap is a simple, no-fuss option.
  • Body wash: A few swipes of the bar on a wet washcloth is enough. Don't rub concentrate directly onto dry skin.
  • Face wash: Works best for oily skin. Follow with a gentle moisturizer — the high pH can be drying for sensitive types.
  • Shampoo: Castile cleanses the scalp well, but always follow with an acidic rinse (diluted apple cider vinegar) to restore pH and prevent dryness or tangles.
  • All-purpose cleaner: Mix a small amount with water in a spray bottle. Works on countertops, sinks, and floors.
  • Dish soap: Fine for lightly greasy dishes. Not a match for heavy grease — set that expectation upfront.
  • Plant pest spray: Dilute 1–2 teaspoons per quart of water. Spray directly on soft-bodied insects like aphids. Avoid applying in peak heat.

Benefits of Castile Soap

  • Fully plant-based and vegan: No tallow, no lanolin, no animal by-products.
  • Biodegradable: Breaks down more readily than synthetic detergents — better for camping, travel, and everyday eco-conscious use.
  • No harsh foaming agents: No SLS, no SLES. The lather comes from saponification, not added detergents.
  • Concentrated: A little goes a long way. Fewer purchases, less packaging.
  • Versatile: One product can replace several. That's genuinely useful.

Drawbacks to Know Before You Buy

  • High pH can irritate sensitive skin: The high pH can disrupt the acid mantle. Not a dealbreaker, but real — especially for dry or eczema-prone skin.
  • Hard water problems: Castile soap reacts with calcium and magnesium in hard water, leaving a white film on surfaces, hair, and skin. Rinse thoroughly or use filtered water if this is an issue for you.
  • It doesn't disinfect: Castile soap cleans. It doesn't kill pathogens the way a disinfectant does.
  • Must be diluted: Applying concentrate directly to skin is one of the most common mistakes — and it causes unnecessary irritation.

Bar vs. Liquid Castile Soap — What's the Difference?

Both are made from vegetable oils and an alkali. The difference is which alkali is used.

  • Bar castile soap uses sodium hydroxide (NaOH) → hard, solid bar
  • Liquid castile soap uses potassium hydroxide (KOH) → soft, pourable liquid

Liquid castile is easier to dilute and dispense. Bar castile is more travel-friendly, produces no plastic bottle waste, and is often more economical per wash. Neither is better — it depends on how you use it.

One note: soap made mainly from coconut oil, like our bar, is harder and lathers more than traditional olive oil castile. Coconut oil creates more of the fatty acids responsible for bubbles and cleansing. If you're ingredient-shopping carefully, that distinction matters.

Who Should Use Castile Soap?

Castile soap is a good fit if you:

  • Have oily or combination skin that handles high-pH cleansers without irritation
  • Want to avoid harsh foaming agents like SLS or SLES
  • Care about biodegradable, plant-based ingredients
  • Want one product that can do multiple jobs around the house

It may not be the best fit if you:

  • Have dry, sensitive, or eczema-prone skin (the high pH can cause flare-ups)
  • Have hard tap water (soap scum will be an ongoing issue)
  • Need a daily shampoo replacement (works occasionally, but not ideal for everyday use without an acidic rinse)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is castile soap safe for sensitive skin?

It can be, with the right approach. Dilute it well, use it less frequently, and always follow with a moisturizer. If you have eczema or very dry skin, patch test first — the high pH (8.9–10) can trigger irritation in some people.

Why does castile soap leave a white film?

That's soap scum — a reaction between castile soap and the minerals in hard water. It's harmless but frustrating. Rinsing more thoroughly or switching to filtered water usually solves it.

Can you mix castile soap with other cleaners?

Don't mix it with vinegar or acidic cleaners. Acid neutralizes the soap and breaks down its cleaning ability — you get a cloudy, ineffective mess. Use them separately.
