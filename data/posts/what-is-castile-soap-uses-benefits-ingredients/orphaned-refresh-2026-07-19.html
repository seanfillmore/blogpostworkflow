What Is Castile Soap? (Simple Definition)

Castile soap is a vegetable oil-based soap — no animal fats, no synthetic surfactants. It gets its name from the Castile region of Spain, where craftspeople historically made soap almost entirely from locally abundant olive oil.

Today, the castile soap definition has broadened. Any soap made from 100% plant-derived oils qualifies. Modern versions often blend coconut oil, hemp seed oil, or jojoba oil alongside olive oil. The core identity stays the same: plant oils in, synthetic chemicals out.

What it's not: conventional bar soap. Most standard soaps are made with animal-derived tallow or contain synthetic surfactants like sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). Castile soap skips both.

What Is Castile Soap Made Of?

Three ingredients: a vegetable oil, an alkali, and water.

  • Vegetable oil base: Traditionally olive oil. Modern castile soaps often use coconut oil for better lather, or blends of hemp seed, jojoba, or other vegetable oils. The oil affects hardness, lather, and skin feel.
  • An alkali (lye): Bar castile uses sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Liquid castile uses potassium hydroxide (KOH). The alkali determines the final texture — sodium hydroxide makes a hard bar; potassium hydroxide makes a pourable liquid.
  • Water: Dissolves the alkali and starts the reaction.

The lye concern is common — but unfounded in finished soap. When oil and lye combine through saponification, they transform completely into soap and glycerin. No lye remains in the finished product. The glycerin byproduct is what gives castile soap its naturally moisturizing quality.

Scented versions add essential oils — lavender, peppermint, tea tree, and citrus are common. The ingredient list should always be short and recognizable. Our foaming castile soap, for example, contains saponified organic coconut oil and a blend of organic essential oils. That's it.

What Is the pH of Castile Soap — and Why Does It Matter?

Castile soap has a pH between 8.9 and 10. Your skin sits at pH 4.5 to 5.5. That gap is worth understanding.

Your skin's acid mantle is a thin protective layer that holds moisture in and keeps bacteria out. Washing with something highly alkaline temporarily disrupts it. For most people, the skin bounces back within a few hours. But if your skin is dry, sensitive, or eczema-prone, that disruption can cause tightness, flakiness, or irritation.

This is also why a diluted apple cider vinegar rinse is recommended after using castile soap as shampoo — the acidity helps restore scalp pH after an alkaline wash.

The pH isn't a flaw. It's just context. Knowing it helps you use the product smarter — dilute it, follow with a moisturizer, and don't use it daily on already-dry skin.

Castile Soap Uses and Benefits (Everyday Guide)

One of the best things about castile soap is its versatility. Here's a practical breakdown of the most common castile soap uses, with dilution tips where they matter.

  • Hand soap: This is where it shines. Use a 1:3 or 1:4 soap-to-water ratio in a foaming dispenser. Or skip the mixing — our organic foaming hand soap comes ready to use.
  • Body wash: A few drops on a wet washcloth is enough. Don't apply concentrate directly to skin.
  • Face wash: Works best for oily skin. Follow with a gentle moisturizer — the alkaline pH can be drying for sensitive types.
  • Shampoo: Castile cleanses the scalp well, but always follow with an acidic rinse (diluted apple cider vinegar) to restore pH and prevent dryness or tangles.
  • All-purpose cleaner: Mix a small amount with water in a spray bottle. Works on countertops, sinks, and floors.
  • Dish soap: Fine for lightly greasy dishes. Not a match for heavy grease — set that expectation upfront.
  • Plant pest spray: Dilute 1–2 teaspoons per quart of water. Spray directly on soft-bodied insects like aphids. Avoid applying in peak heat.

Benefits of Castile Soap

  • Fully plant-based and vegan: No tallow, no lanolin, no animal by-products.
  • Biodegradable: Breaks down more readily than synthetic detergents — better for camping, travel, and everyday eco-conscious use.
  • No synthetic surfactants: No SLS, no SLES. The lather comes from saponification, not added detergents.
  • Concentrated: A little goes a long way. Fewer purchases, less packaging.
  • Versatile: One product can replace several. That's genuinely useful.

Drawbacks to Know Before You Buy

  • High pH can irritate sensitive skin: The alkalinity can disrupt the acid mantle. Not a dealbreaker, but real — especially for dry or eczema-prone skin.
  • Hard water problems: Castile soap reacts with calcium and magnesium in hard water, leaving a white film on surfaces, hair, and skin. Rinse thoroughly or use filtered water if this is an issue for you.
  • It doesn't disinfect: Castile soap cleans. It doesn't kill pathogens the way a disinfectant does.
  • Must be diluted: Applying concentrate directly to skin is one of the most common mistakes — and it causes unnecessary irritation.

Bar vs. Liquid Castile Soap — What's the Difference?

Both are made from vegetable oils and an alkali. The difference is which alkali is used.

  • Bar castile soap uses sodium hydroxide (NaOH) → hard, solid bar
  • Liquid castile soap uses potassium hydroxide (KOH) → soft, pourable liquid

Liquid castile is easier to dilute and dispense. Bar castile is more travel-friendly, produces no plastic bottle waste, and is often more economical per wash. Neither is better — it depends on how you use it.

One note: castile made primarily from coconut oil produces a harder bar with more lather than traditional olive oil castile. Coconut oil saponifies differently and creates more of the fatty acids responsible for bubbles and cleansing. If you're ingredient-shopping carefully, that distinction matters.

Who Should Use Castile Soap?

Castile soap is a good fit if you:

  • Have oily or combination skin that handles alkaline cleansers without irritation
  • Want to avoid synthetic surfactants like SLS or SLES
  • Care about biodegradable, plant-based ingredients
  • Want one product that can do multiple jobs around the house

It may not be the best fit if you:

  • Have dry, sensitive, or eczema-prone skin (the high pH can cause flare-ups)
  • Have hard tap water (soap scum will be an ongoing issue)
  • Need a daily shampoo replacement (works occasionally, but not ideal for everyday use without an acidic rinse)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is castile soap safe for sensitive skin?

It can be, with the right approach. Dilute it well, use it less frequently, and always follow with a moisturizer. If you have eczema or very dry skin, patch test first — the alkaline pH (8.9–10) can trigger irritation in some people.

Why does castile soap leave a white film?

That's soap scum — a reaction between castile soap and the minerals in hard water. It's harmless but frustrating. Rinsing more thoroughly or switching to filtered water usually solves it.

Can you mix castile soap with other cleaners?

Don't mix it with vinegar or acidic cleaners. Acid neutralizes the soap and breaks down its cleaning ability — you get a cloudy, ineffective mess. Use them separately.

What's the difference between castile soap and regular soap?

Regular bar soap is often made with animal fat (tallow) or synthetic surfactants like SLS. Castile soap uses only plant-derived oils and an alkali — no animal fats, no synthetic detergents. That's the core castile soap definition.
