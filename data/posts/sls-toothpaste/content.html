SLS toothpaste refers to any toothpaste containing sodium lauryl sulfate, a synthetic foaming agent used in most conventional brands. It's responsible for the thick lather you're used to — but that foam does nothing for cleaning. A growing number of people are switching to toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate because SLS is a known mouth irritant, especially for anyone prone to canker sores, dry mouth, or sensitive gums.

This post explains what SLS is, why brands use it, what the real concerns are, and what to look for in a clean SLS-free formula.

What Is SLS in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is a surfactant — an ingredient that lowers surface tension so liquid spreads and lifts away oil and debris. In toothpaste, it creates foam and helps distribute other ingredients evenly across your teeth.

You'll find it in shampoos, body washes, and face cleansers for the same reason: it's cheap and it lathers well. On a label, look for sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or the abbreviation SLS or SDS.

One important note: SLS is different from SLES (sodium laureth sulfate), which is a related but milder compound. Some brands swap one for the other — that's not the same as going SLS-free. Always check the full ingredient list.

Why Do Brands Put SLS in Toothpaste?

Three reasons: foam sells, it's cheap, and it helps spread active ingredients like fluoride or baking soda more evenly. Consumers associate thick lather with cleaning power — so brands keep using it.

Here's the thing: foam has nothing to do with how well your toothpaste cleans. That's handled by abrasives and active ingredients. The suds are just a sensation we've been conditioned to expect. SLS-free toothpastes foam less or not at all — and they clean just as well.

Is SLS in Toothpaste Actually Bad for You?

SLS in toothpaste is not acutely dangerous for most people. Regulatory bodies in the US and EU have reviewed it and found no evidence of cancer or systemic harm at the concentrations used in toothpaste. Anyone suggesting otherwise is overstating the science.

That said, "not acutely dangerous" isn't the same as "universally harmless." The documented concerns are real — just more mundane than scary headlines suggest.

  • Mouth irritation: SLS can disrupt the mucosal lining of your mouth — the protective layer inside your cheeks, lips, and gums. Daily exposure can gradually make that tissue more raw and reactive.
  • Dry mouth: SLS breaks down the film that helps keep oral tissue lubricated. If you already deal with dry mouth (xerostomia), this makes it worse — and dry mouth raises cavity risk because saliva is a key defense against bacteria.
  • Skin reactions: SLS residue around the lips can trigger perioral dermatitis — a rash-like condition around the mouth — or worsen existing eczema. Some people develop contact sensitivity to SLS that shows up as redness, swelling, or peeling.

Many dentists now recommend SLS-free formulas as a default, especially for patients with recurring oral irritation or chronic dry mouth.

Can SLS Toothpaste Cause Canker Sores?

This is one of the most searched questions about SLS, and the evidence is fairly specific. A 1994 study by Herlofson and Barkvoll found that people using SLS-containing toothpaste got significantly more canker sores than those using an SLS-free formula. The likely reason: SLS strips the protective coating from the inside of your mouth, leaving tissue more exposed and easier to ulcerate. source

Later research broadly supports this. Canker sores have multiple triggers — stress, certain foods, nutritional deficiencies — so SLS isn't the cause for everyone. But it's specific enough that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste for canker sores is one of the simplest, lowest-risk things you can try. Give it four to six weeks. Some people see a real improvement. If nothing changes, SLS probably isn't your main trigger — but you lose nothing by testing it.

Who Should Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste?

Anyone can benefit, but the groups with the most to gain are people with recurrent canker sores, sensitive teeth or gums, dry mouth, perioral dermatitis, known SLS sensitivity, children, and anyone building a natural routine. Here's why each one matters:

  • People with recurrent canker sores — strong enough evidence to make switching worth trying
  • Sensitive teeth or gums — SLS can make gum irritation worse; sensitive toothpaste without SLS is gentler
  • Dry mouth — removing SLS is a simple swap that stops making the problem harder to manage
  • Perioral dermatitis or lip irritation — SLS residue around the mouth is a known trigger; removing it eliminates that variable
  • Known SLS sensitivity or allergy — burning, swelling, or peeling in or around the mouth means this isn't optional
  • Children — smaller mouths, thinner tissue, and kids often swallow some toothpaste; many pediatric dentists recommend SLS-free as a precaution
  • People building a natural personal care routine — skipping SLS is consistent with minimizing synthetic surfactant exposure across the board

How to Shop SLS-Free Without Getting Misled

"Natural" and "gentle" on the front of a package mean nothing legally. Flip the tube. Look for sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or SDS in the ingredient list. If any of those appear, the toothpaste contains SLS — regardless of what the front label claims.

Also check whether a brand has simply swapped SLS for SLES. That's not the same as making a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate. Read the full list, not the marketing copy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is SLS-free toothpaste as effective as regular toothpaste?

Yes. Cleaning is done by the abrasives and active ingredients in toothpaste — not the foam. SLS-free toothpaste cleans just as well; it just lathers less, which can feel different at first.

What's the difference between SLS and SLES in toothpaste?

SLES (sodium laureth sulfate) is a milder relative of SLS. Some brands market SLES-containing products as gentler, and they are — but they're still not the same as a true SLS-free formula. If you're specifically avoiding sulfate surfactants, check that neither appears on the label.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for kids?

Most pediatric dentists recommend it as a precaution. Children have thinner oral tissue and often swallow some toothpaste, so removing an unnecessary irritant makes sense. Our best natural toothpaste for families guide covers kid-friendly options.

Can I get SLS-free toothpaste with fluoride?

Yes — SLS-free and fluoride are not mutually exclusive. Several mainstream and natural brands make fluoride toothpaste without SLS. If you prefer to skip fluoride too, see our fluoride-free toothpaste guide.
