What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is a synthetic foaming agent. It's cheap, reliable, and makes toothpaste lather the way most people expect. That's the entire reason it's there — foam signals "clean" to consumers, even though foam has nothing to do with how well a toothpaste works.

The actual cleaning comes from your brushing motion and mild abrasives like baking soda or silica. SLS just makes bubbles. Manufacturers added it decades ago to meet consumer expectations, and it stuck.

On ingredient labels, SLS appears under several names: sodium dodecyl sulfate, sodium salt of sulfuric acid, and monododecyl ester. When shopping for toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate, check for all three. Also watch for SLES (sodium laureth sulfate) — a close relative with a similar irritation profile that appears in some formulas marketed as "gentle."

What SLS Does to Your Mouth

SLS is a documented irritant at the concentrations used in toothpaste (0.5–2.5%). "Safe by regulatory standards" doesn't mean comfortable or without side effects. Here's what the research shows:

Makes Canker Sores Worse

Multiple clinical studies confirm that switching to SLS‑free toothpaste reduces canker sore frequency, duration, and pain. A widely cited study by Herlofson and Barkvoll (Acta Odontologica Scandinavica, 1994) found that participants averaging roughly 18 sores per three months saw significant improvement on an SLS‑free formula. If you get recurring mouth ulcers, this single swap can make a real difference. See our picks for sensitive mouths →

Damages Your Mouth's Protective Lining

Your mouth has a mucin layer — a natural coating that shields soft tissue from bacteria and acids. SLS degrades this layer, causing the inner cheeks and lips to peel or feel raw. If brushing leaves your mouth feeling stripped, that's why.

Makes Food Taste Wrong

SLS suppresses sweet taste receptors and amplifies bitterness — which is why orange juice tastes terrible right after brushing. This effect can last up to four hours. Switching to a non SLS toothpaste typically eliminates it within days.

Worsens Dry Mouth

SLS strips the natural oils that keep your mouth moist. If you already deal with dry mouth — from medication, aging, or medical conditions — SLS makes it worse. A chronically dry mouth raises your cavity risk, since saliva is your mouth's main defense against decay-causing bacteria.

What SLS Does NOT Do

SLS does not cause cancer. The FDA, the International Agency for Research on Cancer, and the American Cancer Society have all confirmed there is no credible link between SLS and cancer. The real concerns are irritation and oral comfort — not toxicity. Knowing the actual risks helps you make a clear-headed decision.

Does SLS‑Free Toothpaste Actually Clean Your Teeth?

Yes — completely. SLS creates foam; it doesn't clean. Plaque removal comes from the mechanical action of brushing combined with gentle abrasives like baking soda or hydrated silica. Those work exactly the same without SLS.

Good non SLS toothpaste formulas use alternative surfactants — ingredients like sodium cocoyl glutamate, coco-glucoside, and decyl glucoside — derived from coconut or corn. How to Read a Toothpaste Label

"SLS‑free" simply means sodium lauryl sulfate doesn't appear on the ingredient list. But a few things are worth knowing before you trust that claim at face value.

SLS goes by multiple names. Look for: sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, and SDS. If any of these appear, the product contains SLS.

Watch for close relatives. Two related compounds sometimes show up in formulas marketed as SLS‑free:

  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate / sodium lauryl ether sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS, but still a sulfate surfactant. Some sensitive users tolerate it; others don't. If you're switching specifically for irritation relief, avoid this too.
  • SCS (sodium coco sulfate): Made from whole coconut oil rather than isolated lauric acid. Often marketed as "natural," but it still contains lauryl sulfate as a component. People with strong SLS sensitivity may still react to it.

Look for what replaces SLS. The best SLS‑free toothpaste brands use plant-derived cleansers like coco-glucoside or sodium cocoyl glutamate. These clean gently and are well-tolerated by most people. Why glycerin-free matters too →

Who Should Use SLS‑Free Toothpaste?

The best non SLS toothpaste is a smart choice for almost anyone — but it's especially worth making the switch if you:

  • Get canker sores or recurring mouth ulcers
  • Have dry mouth, receding gums, or sensitive oral tissue
  • Are going through orthodontic treatment, implants, or recent dental work
  • Take prescription medications that affect your mouth's soft tissue
  • Notice food tastes off for hours after brushing
  • Want a shorter, cleaner ingredient list for yourself or your kids
  • Have a known sensitivity to sulfate compounds
