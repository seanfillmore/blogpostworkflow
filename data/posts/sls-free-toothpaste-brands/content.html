What Is SLS and Why Do Some People Avoid It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate is a synthetic surfactant — that just means it's a foaming and cleansing agent. Manufacturers add it to toothpaste because it creates the thick lather most of us grew up associating with a "good clean." It's also cheap and helps spread the paste across your teeth and gums quickly.

The problem is how it gets made. SLS starts from lauryl alcohol (often palm or coconut-derived), but it goes through heavy chemical processing that turns it into a harsh detergent — nothing like the gentle oils it originates from. That harshness shows up in your mouth.

Studies have linked SLS to a higher recurrence of aphthous ulcers — the clinical name for canker sores — in susceptible individuals. One commonly cited trial found that participants using SLS-free toothpaste had significantly fewer canker sores than those using a standard SLS formula. Researchers believe SLS strips away a protective mucin layer in the mouth, leaving tissue more exposed to irritants. You can read more about why SLS in toothpaste is worth avoiding in our deeper ingredient breakdown.

One more thing worth knowing: toothpaste doesn't actually clean your teeth because of the foam. Cleaning happens through mild abrasion and active ingredients. The foam is just a sensation. Removing SLS doesn't remove the clean — it removes the irritant.

Who Should Switch to an SLS Free Toothpaste Brand?

Most people do fine with the switch. But for some, going sulfate free is less of a preference and more of a genuine need. Here's who benefits most:

  • People with recurrent canker sores: The clinical link between SLS and aphthous ulcer frequency is the most evidence-backed reason to make the switch. If you get sore mouth ulcers regularly, SLS-free is worth trying first.
  • People with sensitive teeth or gum sensitivity: SLS can strip the oral mucosa and worsen sensitivity, especially near the gumline. A gentler toothpaste for sensitive teeth can make daily brushing more comfortable.
  • People using prescription oral rinses: SLS can interfere with the efficacy of some prescription-strength rinses, including chlorhexidine. Check with your dentist if this applies to you.
  • People with dry mouth: Dry mouth already means less natural protection from saliva. SLS compounds the irritation by further disrupting the mucosal barrier.
  • Anyone building a clean or natural personal care routine: If you're already switching to sulfate free shampoos and cleansers, your toothpaste is a logical next step.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label and Spot Hidden SLS

This is the practical skill most "SLS free" articles skip over. Here's exactly what to look for when you pick up a tube at the store.

The primary name: "Sodium lauryl sulfate" — spelled out in full. This is the most common listing and what most people know to look for.

Common aliases: Watch for "sodium laurilsulfate" (same compound, alternate INCI spelling) and "SLS" as an abbreviated label listing. Some brands also list it buried mid-formula.

Where it sits in the list: Ingredients are listed in descending order by concentration. If SLS appears near the top, it's a high-concentration formula. Even lower-listed SLS can irritate a reactive mouth.

SLS vs. SLES: Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES) is a related but milder compound — it's been ethoxylated, which reduces irritation somewhat. Still a synthetic sulfate, though, and many clean-beauty shoppers prefer to avoid both. Neither belongs in a genuinely sulfate free toothpaste.

Signs of a truly clean formula: Ingredients like coconut oil, baking soda, and xanthan gum (a natural thickener) signal a formula built around real, recognizable ingredients rather than synthetic surfactants. Fluoride or Fluoride Free? What SLS Free Shoppers Need to Know

Many people searching for SLS free toothpaste brands are also weighing the fluoride question. Here's the honest summary.

Fluoride is the primary cavity-prevention active ingredient recognized by dental health organizations worldwide. It works by helping remineralize weakened enamel and making teeth more resistant to acid erosion. If you have a history of cavities or live in an area without fluoridated water, your dentist may recommend keeping fluoride in your routine.

Fluoride-free options rely on alternatives — hydroxyapatite, xylitol, calcium phosphate compounds — that support oral health through different pathways. Early research on nano-hydroxyapatite is promising, though it is not yet considered equivalent to fluoride by major dental bodies.

Both can be SLS free. The choice depends on your oral health history and your personal approach to synthetic minerals. Real Skin Care carries options in both categories.
