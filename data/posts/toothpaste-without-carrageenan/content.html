What Is Carrageenan and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Carrageenan is pulled from red seaweed. In toothpaste, it works as a thickener and binder. That keeps the paste smooth so it doesn't separate in the tube. Food makers use it the same way in ice cream and plant milks.

Regulators allow it in food. The FDA lists it under 21 CFR 172.620. In 2018, EFSA (the European Food Safety Authority) re-evaluated carrageenan (E 407). It did not find a safety concern at current uses, though it noted some data gaps.

So why do some people avoid it? Animal and cell studies, reviewed by Tobacman in 2001, linked carrageenan in the diet to gut inflammation. But those studies are about eating it, often in large amounts. Toothpaste is spit out and rinsed away.

Evidence rating for harm from toothpaste: weak. Human data is limited and mixed, and the real-world risk from toothpaste isn't established. For most people, skipping carrageenan is a preference, not a medical need. If you have a digestive condition or a known sensitivity, ask your doctor or dentist.

1. Carrageenan and Other Thickeners

Carrageenan can hide under other names, so scan for these words. Every thickener has trade-offs, and "natural" doesn't automatically mean better. Pick based on your own preferences.

  • Carrageenan names: carrageenan, Irish moss, chondrus crispus, E407.
  • Xanthan gum: a thickener made by fermenting sugars. It's what we use.
  • Cellulose gum: a thickener made from plant fibers.
  • Silica and clays: mineral thickeners that can also add light scrubbing.

2. Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)

SLS is a foaming agent. It makes toothpaste lather, but lather doesn't make teeth any cleaner. Some small studies, such as Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) and Chahine et al. (1997), linked SLS to more canker sores in some people.

Evidence rating: mixed. Results aren't universal. If you get frequent canker sores or have a sensitive mouth, an SLS-free toothpaste is a fair thing to try. Ours has no SLS, so it foams only lightly.

3. Sweeteners: Sugar, Artificial Sweeteners and Xylitol

Nearly all toothpaste is sugar free. The sweet taste comes from sweeteners, so "toothpaste without sugar" is easy to find. Look for saccharin or sorbitol on the label if you want to avoid artificial or sugar-alcohol sweeteners.

Xylitol is the tricky one. A Cochrane review (Riley et al., 2015) found low-quality evidence for cavity benefits. Evidence rating: mixed to weak. Some people also avoid it because it can upset digestion. It is also harmful to dogs, so the FDA warns pet owners to keep xylitol products out of reach. Our toothpaste has no sugar, xylitol or artificial sweeteners. It's sweetened with stevia, a plant-based sweetener.

4. Gluten

Most toothpaste ingredients don't contain gluten. The worry for people with celiac disease is cross-contact during manufacturing and unclear flavorings. The NIDDK and the Celiac Disease Foundation offer good guidance.

5. Glycerin

Glycerin is a humectant, which means it holds moisture so paste doesn't dry out. You may have read that it coats teeth and blocks minerals from getting back in. That claim gets repeated a lot, but we couldn't find direct studies behind it. Evidence rating: weak.

Some people still skip glycerin by preference, and that's fine. Look for pastes that use coconut oil and a gum for texture. That's how ours works, and it has no glycerin. You can read more in our post on glycerin-free toothpaste.

6. Fluoride: Know Your Choice

The evidence here is strong. A Cochrane review (Walsh et al., 2019) found that fluoride toothpaste prevents cavities, and the ADA recommends it. Evidence rating: strong.

Some shoppers still choose fluoride-free toothpaste. If you're one of them, talk with your dentist first, especially if you have a high cavity risk. Our toothpaste has no fluoride. It cleans teeth and freshens breath, but it doesn't replace what your dentist advises.

7. Flavor and Mint Alternatives

If you dislike mint, cinnamon toothpaste is the usual swap. Essential-oil flavors, including cinnamon and mint, can bother some sensitive mouths. If a paste stings, switch flavors or ask your dentist.

Be aware that our formula blends peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon and clove oils. It isn't mint-free. If you like mint toothpaste with a warm cinnamon kick, it may suit you. If you want zero mint, look elsewhere.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label in 60 Seconds

You don't need a chemistry degree. Run through this quick scan, and you'll cover most of what matters. Note that "natural" isn't a tightly defined claim on toothpaste, so read the ingredients instead of the front of the box.

  1. Active ingredient: check whether it has fluoride.
  2. Thickener: look for carrageenan, xanthan gum or cellulose gum.
  3. Foaming agent: look for sodium lauryl sulfate.
  4. Sweetener: look for stevia, xylitol, sorbitol or saccharin.
  5. Preservatives and flavor: note the oils or flavors used.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is carrageenan in toothpaste harmful?
Harm from toothpaste hasn't been shown. The concerns come from animal and cell studies on dietary carrageenan (Tobacman, 2001). Regulators like EFSA allow it in food. Avoiding it is a preference.

Is carrageenan the same as degraded carrageenan (poligeenan)?
No. Poligeenan is a different, broken-down form made with acid and heat, and it isn't used in food. Food-grade carrageenan is the type regulators review. The Tobacman review discusses both, so read labels and sources carefully.

Can I make my own natural toothpaste?
You can find recipes online, but homemade pastes usually lack fluoride and can be too gritty for enamel. We don't give a recipe here. Talk with your dentist first, and see our post on coconut oil as toothpaste.

Is natural toothpaste safe for kids?
"Natural" doesn't automatically mean safe for kids. Little ones swallow paste, so use a small smear and supervise. Ask your pediatric dentist about fluoride. Our kids guide walks through the questions to bring.

Does toothpaste without carrageenan work as well?
Carrageenan only affects texture. Cleaning comes from gentle scrubbing and good brushing. The bigger difference is fluoride, which has strong evidence for preventing cavities.
