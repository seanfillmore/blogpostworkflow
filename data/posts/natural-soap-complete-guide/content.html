Natural soap is a cleansing bar made through saponification — a chemical reaction between plant oils or fats and an alkali — that leaves behind a gentle cleanser with no synthetic detergents and no petroleum-derived additives. It is fundamentally different from most bars you find in drugstores, which are technically detergent products that can strip your skin's protective moisture barrier. This guide covers everything you need to know about all natural soap and handmade soap: what's really in it, which ingredients do the work, which varieties suit different skin types, and how to read a label without getting fooled by greenwashing.

If you've been searching for a cleaner option, you're in the right place. By the end, you'll know exactly what to look for — and what to leave on the shelf.

What Is Natural Soap — And What Makes It Different?

Natural soap is produced through saponification: oils or fats are combined with an alkali (sodium hydroxide for a solid bar, potassium hydroxide for liquid soap). The alkali drives the reaction, but it is fully consumed in the process. The finished bar contains no lye — only the soap molecules and naturally retained glycerin that result from the reaction. That's soap making in its most fundamental form.

Most bars in the mass market are a different thing entirely. They're called syndets — synthetic detergent bars — and they're built around ingredients like sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), synthetic preservatives, and petroleum-derived compounds. Research published in the International Journal of Cosmetic Science has shown that SLS disrupts the skin's lipid barrier and can trigger irritation, particularly with repeated use. These bars may look and feel like soap, but they are not made through saponification.

One specific type worth knowing is castile soap. If you've ever wondered what is castile soap, here's the short answer: it's a natural soap made exclusively from plant oils — traditionally olive oil, originating in the Castile region of Spain. Organic castile soap has one of the simplest ingredient lists in the cleansing category, making it a benchmark for what "pure" really means. It can also be diluted for use as body wash, hand soap, or even a household cleaner.

The takeaway: natural soap cleans skin without dismantling the protective barrier that keeps moisture in and irritants out. Syndets do the same cleaning job, but often at a cost to long-term skin health.

Key Ingredients in Natural Soap — What to Look For

Not all natural soaps are equal. The oils used in the formula determine how the finished bar performs on your skin. Here's what the most common ingredients actually do.

Coconut oil is the backbone of many natural bar soaps. It produces a rich, bubbly lather and its high lauric acid content — approximately 50% of coconut oil's total fatty acid profile — gives it documented antimicrobial properties. A widely cited review by Dayrit (2015) in the Journal of Health and Nutrition Sciences confirmed lauric acid's activity against a range of bacteria and fungi. Real Skin Care's coconut oil soap bar is built around this ingredient.

Olive oil is high in oleic acid and has been used in soap making for centuries. It creates a mild, conditioning bar that rinses clean without stripping. Olive oil forms the base of castile soap and is particularly good for dry or sensitive skin.

Shea butter contributes stearic and oleic acids to a soap formula. Both support the skin's lipid barrier, and shea-rich bars tend to leave skin feeling softer after washing. This is especially useful in cold-process formulas where the butter's properties are preserved at lower temperatures.

Castor oil shows up in small amounts in many natural bar soaps because it boosts lather and contributes to a dense, creamy bubble structure. It's rarely a primary oil but makes a real difference in how the bar performs.

Essential oils vs. synthetic fragrance is one of the most important distinctions in this category. Natural soaps use essential oils or botanical extracts for scent. Synthetic fragrance — listed simply as "fragrance" on a label — can legally contain hundreds of undisclosed chemicals. The FDA notes that fragrance formulas are considered trade secrets and do not require full ingredient disclosure. For anyone with sensitive skin or fragrance sensitivity, this matters.

One more term you may encounter: tallow soap. This is a traditional natural soap made from rendered animal fat. It has a long history in soap making and produces a hard, long-lasting bar. It's not plant-based, so it's not suitable for vegan shoppers, but it is a legitimate form of natural soap by the saponification definition.

Popular Types of Natural Soap — A Complete Overview

The natural soap category covers a wide range of formulations. Here's a practical breakdown of the types you're most likely to encounter.

Castile Soap

Castile soap is a plant-based soap made entirely from vegetable oils — traditionally olive oil — and named for the Castile region of Spain where it originated. Organic castile soap is highly versatile: dilute it for a body wash, use it full-strength as a hand soap, or add it to a cleaning spray. Its short, simple ingredient list makes it an excellent choice for sensitive or allergy-prone skin.

Turmeric Soap

Turmeric soap incorporates turmeric root powder or extract, which is rich in curcumin. A review published in Phytotherapy Research (2016) documented curcumin's anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects on skin. A turmeric soap bar is popular for brightening and evening skin tone, and it suits acne-prone or dull skin well.

Goat Milk Soap

Goat milk soap is a moisturizing option that uses goat milk for its natural lactic acid content. Lactic acid is an alpha-hydroxy acid (AHA) — it gently loosens dead skin cells and supports healthy skin pH. A study in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology found that lactic acid at low concentrations effectively improves skin hydration. Goat milk soap is especially well-suited to dry or sensitive skin.

Handmade Cold-Process Soap

Cold-process soap is made by combining oils and lye at low temperatures, which preserves the integrity of heat-sensitive plant ingredients. The most important benefit: the process retains natural glycerin in the finished bar. Commercial soap manufacturers typically extract glycerin during production and sell it separately. That retained glycerin acts as a humectant — it draws moisture from the air into the skin — which is why handmade natural soap bars tend to feel noticeably more moisturizing than mass-market bars.

Unscented Natural Soap

True unscented soap contains no fragrance compounds whatsoever — not even masking agents. This is different from bars labeled "fragrance-free" that may still include scent-neutralizing chemicals. Unscented bars are the safest choice for people with fragrance sensitivity, eczema, contact dermatitis, post-procedure skin, or babies. If your skin reacts to almost everything, start here.

Worth a brief mention: soap nuts (also called soapberries) are an emerging natural cleansing ingredient derived from the Sapindus mukorossi tree. They contain saponins — plant-based surfactants — and are used in some natural cleansing products as a biodegradable alternative to synthetic lather agents.

How to Choose the Right Natural Soap for Your Skin Type

This is where most product pages fall short. Knowing that a soap is "natural" is a start — but the right formula depends on your skin.

  • Dry skin: Look for bars with olive oil, shea butter, or goat milk. These ingredients are high in oleic and stearic acids, which support the skin barrier without over-cleansing. Go easy on high-coconut-oil bars — coconut oil is a strong cleanser and can be drying in high concentrations if your skin is already moisture-depleted.
  • Oily or acne-prone skin: Look for bars with clay, activated charcoal, or tea tree oil. Castile soap or a turmeric soap bar can work well here. Avoid heavy butter-rich formulas that may leave a residue and clog pores.
  • Sensitive skin: Prioritize fragrance-free or unscented bars with short ingredient lists. Goat milk soap and organic castile soap are both strong picks. The fewer ingredients, the fewer potential irritants.
  • Normal or combination skin: Most natural bar soaps work well. A coconut oil-based bar is a solid default — you get good lather, a clean rinse, and a bar that lasts.

One note on face vs. body: facial skin is more delicate than skin on your arms or legs. If you want to use a bar on your face, look for one specifically formulated for that purpose — gentler oils like olive or jojoba rather than high-coconut formulas that cleanse aggressively.

How to Read a Natural Soap Label — Spotting Greenwashing

Here's something most brands won't tell you: the word "natural" on a soap label means nothing legally. The FDA does not regulate or define the term "natural" for cosmetic products. Any brand can print it on any bar, regardless of what's inside. So you need to read the actual ingredient list.

Here's what to look for — and what to avoid:

  • Real natural soap ingredients: Look for saponified oils listed by their botanical names — "saponified cocos nucifera oil" (coconut), "saponified olea europaea fruit oil" (olive). These tell you the soap was made through actual saponification, not detergent manufacturing.
  • Red flags: Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), sodium laureth sulfate (SLES), PEGs, parabens, and synthetic dyes listed as FD&C color codes. These are markers of a syndet bar, not a true natural soap.
  • "Fragrance" as a single ingredient: This is a catch-all term that can represent a blend of hundreds of undisclosed chemicals. If the label says "fragrance" rather than listing specific essential oils, that's a greenwashing signal.
  • Certifications worth trusting: USDA Certified Organic verifies that ingredients meet agricultural standards. EWG Verified means the formula has been screened for safety by the Environmental Working Group. Leaping Bunny confirms the product is cruelty-free.

Natural Soap vs. Body Wash — Which Is Better?

This comes down to priorities. Natural bar soap typically has a shorter ingredient list, requires no preservatives (because there's no water in a bar to grow bacteria), and retains glycerin that liquid formulas often leave out. From an environmental standpoint, a lifecycle analysis published in the International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment found that liquid soap requires roughly five times more energy to produce and generates significantly more plastic packaging waste than bar soap.

Body wash has real advantages too — it's convenient, easy to apply over large areas, and some people simply prefer the texture. If that's you, Real Skin Care's organic foaming hand soap is a liquid option made with organic ingredients and none of the synthetic additives found in conventional body washes.

Bottom line: if you want simplicity, fewer ingredients, and less packaging, a natural bar is hard to beat. If you prefer liquid, make sure the formula is as clean as the bar you'd otherwise choose.

Frequently Asked Questions About Natural Soap

What is the difference between natural soap and regular soap?
Natural soap is made through saponification — plant oils or fats react with an alkali to create a true soap molecule. Most "regular" bars are synthetic detergent products (syndets) built around SLS and petroleum-derived ingredients. Natural soap retains glycerin and avoids synthetic surfactants that can disrupt the skin's lipid barrier.

Is natural soap better for sensitive skin?
Yes, in most cases. Natural soap contains fewer potential irritants — no synthetic fragrance, no parabens, no SLS. The retained glycerin in cold-process bars also acts as a humectant, helping skin hold moisture. For very reactive skin, choose an unscented or fragrance-free organic bar with a short ingredient list.

Does natural soap expire?
Yes. Most natural bar soaps have a shelf life of one to two years. The plant oils in the formula can go rancid over time, which you'll notice as an off smell or orange discoloration (called "dreaded orange spots" in soap making). Store bars in a cool, dry place away from direct light to extend their life.

Can I use natural soap on my face?
Yes, but choose carefully. Facial skin is more delicate than body skin, so look for bars formulated with gentler oils — olive oil, jojoba, or goat milk — rather than high-coconut bars that cleanse more aggressively. A castile soap or a bar specifically labeled for facial use is a safe starting point for most skin types.
