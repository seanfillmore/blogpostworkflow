What Is Herbal Toothpaste?

Herbal toothpaste replaces synthetic active agents with plant-derived ones. Instead of triclosan or artificial foaming agents, you get things like clove oil, myrrh powder, and coconut oil. The category includes formulas that are fluoride-free, SLS-free, vegan, and organic — though not every herbal toothpaste checks all those boxes.

One important thing to know: "herbal" is not a regulated term in the US. Any brand can put it on a label. That makes reading the actual ingredient list more important than trusting the front of the package. A formula with "natural extracts" buried at the bottom of a long synthetic ingredient list is not the same thing as one built around botanicals from the start.

So does herbal toothpaste actually work? A 2014 clinical study by Tatikonda et al., published in the Journal of International Society of Preventive and Community Dentistry (PMC4278105), found herbal dentifrices to be as effective as conventional dentifrices for plaque control and gingivitis reduction. That's a meaningful finding — and a good starting point for the research we'll dig into below.

Key Herbs Found in Herbal Toothpaste — and What the Research Says

Not all botanical ingredients are created equal. Here's a look at the herbs with the most evidence behind them in oral care, plus what they actually do.

Neem

Neem (Azadirachta indica) has been used in Ayurvedic oral care for centuries — traditionally as a chewing stick called a "datun." The antimicrobial activity behind that tradition has since been studied. Pai MR et al., in a 2004 study published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology, evaluated neem leaf extract gel and found it showed measurable antiplaque activity. Neem's compounds appear to act against Streptococcus mutans, a key bacteria involved in plaque formation. It's one of the most commonly found active ingredients in fluoride-free and Ayurvedic toothpaste formulas.

Clove

Clove is one of the few herbal ingredients with deep roots in both traditional use and modern dentistry. Its active compound, eugenol, has well-documented analgesic and antimicrobial properties. Dental professionals have used eugenol in temporary fillings and dental cements for decades. Research published in dental pharmacology literature supports its activity against oral pathogens and its ability to reduce pain associated with tooth and gum sensitivity. It also contributes to natural breath freshening — which is why you'll find clove essential oil in many botanical toothpaste formulas, including Real Skin Care's.

Cinnamon

Cinnamon bark has documented antimicrobial and antifungal properties that make it a useful ingredient in oral care. Research on cinnamon bark extract shows activity against bacteria commonly associated with oral infections and bad breath. It also adds a naturally warming flavor that many people prefer over artificial mint. If you're interested in cinnamon-forward formulas, Real Skin Care's cinnamon toothpaste collection is worth exploring.

Myrrh

Myrrh resin has been used in oral care since ancient Egypt and appears in Ayurvedic practice as well. It is known for its anti-inflammatory and astringent properties, and is often used to support gum health. Peer-reviewed clinical research specifically on myrrh in toothpaste is limited — most evidence comes from traditional use and small studies. Where we cite it here, we're attributing its role to traditional practice and early-stage research rather than large clinical trials. Real Skin Care's formula uses wildcrafted myrrh powder — meaning it's harvested from wild plants rather than farmed, which preserves the resin's natural compound profile.

Coconut Oil

Coconut oil's antimicrobial properties come largely from its lauric acid content, which makes up roughly 50% of its fatty acid profile. Lauric acid has shown antimicrobial activity against oral bacteria in laboratory research. Oil pulling — the practice of swishing oil in the mouth — has also been studied for oral health benefits. A 2017 review by Shanbhag VK, published in the Journal of Traditional and Complementary Medicine, found oil pulling with coconut oil was associated with reduced plaque and improved gum health markers. Real Skin Care uses organic virgin coconut oil as a base in its coconut oil toothpaste — not as a trace additive, but as a core ingredient.

What to Look for in a Herbal Toothpaste Formula

Once you decide you want a natural toothpaste, the next question is what a good one actually looks like. Here's what to check before you buy.

  • A short, readable ingredient list: If you can't identify most of what's in it, that's worth noting. Botanical names (like Azadirachta indica for neem or Syzygium aromaticum for clove) are fine — they should be followed by a common name so you know what you're getting.
  • A natural abrasive: Baking soda and calcium carbonate are clean options that remove surface stains and plaque without microplastics. Avoid formulas that use synthetic microbeads.
  • A natural binding agent: Xanthan gum is a common plant-derived binder that holds a paste's texture without synthetic thickeners.
  • Plant-based flavoring: Real essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, clove, cinnamon) rather than "natural flavor" — a catch-all term that can include synthetic processing aids.
  • Certifications that mean something: Certified organic, certified vegan, and cruelty-free certifications require third-party verification. "Natural" on a label has no regulatory definition in the US and requires zero verification. Certifications provide accountability; labels don't.

Ingredients to Avoid in Conventional Toothpaste

Understanding what herbal toothpaste leaves out is just as useful as knowing what it includes. Here are the conventional ingredients most worth knowing about.

  • Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS): SLS is the foaming agent in most conventional toothpastes. It makes toothpaste lather — but it can also irritate oral tissue. Some research has linked SLS to increased frequency of canker sores in susceptible individuals. If you get recurring mouth sores, switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is often the first recommendation.
  • Artificial sweeteners and dyes: Saccharin and synthetic colorants are added for taste and appearance, not function. Consumer concern around long-term exposure is reasonable, even if evidence of harm at typical toothpaste doses is limited.
  • Triclosan: Triclosan is an antibacterial agent the FDA banned from hand soaps in 2016 due to safety concerns. It has since been removed from most toothpastes — but it's worth checking older or budget formulas.
  • Carrageenan: A seaweed-derived thickener that is controversial in some health circles. Degraded carrageenan (not the same form used in food) has been associated with gut inflammation in animal studies. The relevance to oral use is debated, but ingredient-conscious consumers often prefer to avoid it.
  • Parabens: Synthetic preservatives used across personal care products. Concern centers on their potential hormonal activity. Herbal toothpastes typically use natural preservation strategies instead.

Is Herbal Toothpaste Effective? What the Research Shows

This is the fair question to ask. The direct answer: yes, with consistency and good brushing technique, herbal toothpaste can be as effective as conventional toothpaste for everyday oral health.

The Tatikonda et al. 2014 study — "Effects of Herbal and Non-Herbal Toothpastes on Plaque and Gingivitis: A Clinical and Microbial Study," published in the Journal of International Society of Preventive and Community Dentistry — found no clinically significant difference between herbal and conventional dentifrices for plaque reduction and gingivitis control over the study period. Technique and brushing frequency mattered more than formula type.

What herbal toothpaste does not replace: professional cleanings, consistent twice-daily brushing, and flossing. No toothpaste does.

On fluoride: fluoride has a strong, well-established evidence base for cavity prevention. If you choose a non-fluoride toothpaste, that's a personal decision worth making with your dentist's input — especially for children. This is a genuine recommendation, not a disclaimer. A dentist can help you weigh your individual cavity risk against your preference for a fluoride-free formula.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is herbal toothpaste as effective as regular toothpaste?
Yes, when used consistently with proper technique. The 2014 clinical study by Tatikonda et al., published in the Journal of International Society of Preventive and Community Dentistry, found herbal dentifrices performed comparably to conventional ones for plaque and gingivitis control. How you brush — frequency, duration, and technique — matters more than formula type.

Is herbal toothpaste safe for kids?
Most natural herbal formulas are gentle enough for children, but fluoride status is the key question to resolve first. If your child is under the age where they can reliably spit, a fluoride-free formula reduces the risk of swallowing too much fluoride. That said, check with a pediatric dentist before switching — they can advise based on your child's individual cavity risk and age.

What herbs are best for oral health?
The herbs with the most research support in oral care are neem (antiplaque, antimicrobial), clove/eugenol (analgesic and antimicrobial), cinnamon (antimicrobial and antifungal), and coconut oil (lauric acid, antimicrobial). Each is covered in detail in the herb section above.

Is herbal toothpaste fluoride-free?
Many are, but not all. Some herbal formulas do include fluoride. Always check the active ingredients section of the label.
