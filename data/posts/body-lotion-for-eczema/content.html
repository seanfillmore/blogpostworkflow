What Eczema Actually Does to Your Skin (And Why Moisturizing Matters)

Eczema — also called atopic dermatitis — isn't just dry skin. It's a condition where the skin's protective barrier doesn't work the way it should. Research has shown that many people with eczema have a mutation or dysfunction in the filaggrin protein, which plays a key role in keeping the outer layer of skin intact. Without it functioning properly, the skin loses moisture faster and lets irritants, allergens, and bacteria in more easily.

Think of your skin barrier like a brick wall. In healthy skin, the "bricks" (skin cells) sit tightly packed together with "mortar" made of lipids — ceramides, fatty acids, and cholesterol. In eczema-prone skin, that mortar is thin or missing in spots. Moisture escapes. Things that wouldn't bother most people get through and cause that familiar itch-scratch cycle.

That's why daily moisturizing isn't just comfort — it's considered a cornerstone of eczema management. According to the National Eczema Association, consistent use of a body moisturizer helps restore and maintain the skin barrier over time. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends a technique called "soak and seal" — bathing in lukewarm water for 10–15 minutes, then applying moisturizer within three minutes while skin is still slightly damp to trap that moisture in.

A good eczema body cream isn't a cure. But used consistently, the right one can make dry, itchy skin dramatically more manageable between flares.

Key Ingredients to Look for in a Body Lotion for Eczema

When you're shopping for a body lotion for eczema, the ingredient list matters more than the marketing claim on the front. Here's what actually has science behind it — and why it helps specifically for eczema-prone skin.

  • Colloidal oatmeal: This is one of the most well-studied ingredients for eczema relief. The FDA recognizes colloidal oatmeal as an over-the-counter skin protectant. It contains compounds called avenanthramides, which have documented anti-inflammatory and anti-itch properties. It also forms a protective film on the skin that helps reduce moisture loss — exactly what compromised eczema skin needs.
  • Coconut oil: Virgin, unrefined coconut oil contains a high concentration of lauric acid, a fatty acid that may help reinforce the lipid barrier and has documented antimicrobial properties. A 2014 randomized controlled trial by Evangelista et al., published in the International Journal of Dermatology, found that virgin coconut oil was comparable to mineral oil in reducing eczema severity and significantly better for skin hydration. For a deeper look at coconut oil-based moisturizers, see our guide to coconut oil body lotions for extremely dry skin.
  • Jojoba: Jojoba oil is technically a liquid wax, and its structure closely resembles the skin's own sebum. It absorbs readily and helps seal moisture into the skin without feeling heavy. For eczema-prone skin, that means effective hydration without the greasy residue some natural oils can leave.
  • Shea butter: Rich in oleic and stearic fatty acids, shea butter acts as an emollient — it softens and smooths the skin's surface. It also contains triterpene compounds, which research suggests may have anti-inflammatory properties. In a body butter or rich body lotion for dry skin, shea butter is one of the better choices for barrier support. source
  • Aloe vera: Aloe is primarily water-based and has well-known soothing and anti-inflammatory properties. It works best when paired with an occlusive (like coconut oil) that prevents the moisture from evaporating off the skin surface.
  • Glycerin: A humectant — meaning it draws moisture from the air into the skin. Glycerin is one of the most studied and gentlest moisturizing ingredients available, well-tolerated by sensitive skin and widely used in clinical eczema formulas.

Ingredients to Avoid in a Body Lotion If You Have Eczema

This is where most eczema lotion guides fall short — they tell you what to look for, but not what to avoid. For eczema-prone skin, what's NOT in a lotion can matter just as much as what is.

  • Synthetic fragrances: Fragrance is one of the most common triggers for eczema flares and contact dermatitis. The National Eczema Association lists fragrance as a leading sensitizer and recommends fragrance-free formulas for people with eczema. One important note: "fragrance-free" and "unscented" are not the same thing. Unscented products may still contain masking fragrances added to neutralize a formula's natural smell — and those can still trigger a reaction. Always look for "fragrance-free" specifically.
  • Denatured or SD alcohol: Alcohol-based ingredients dry out the skin's surface and can further compromise a barrier that's already struggling. They're especially harsh on eczema-prone skin that's already moisture-depleted.
  • Parabens and harsh synthetic preservatives: Not all eczema sufferers react to parabens, but some individuals with sensitive skin report flares associated with them. The good news: clean, effective preservatives exist. Our post on paraben-free body lotion covers what to look for instead.
  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS): SLS is a surfactant used in washes and some cream formulas. It's well-documented as a skin irritant that can strip the lipid barrier — exactly the barrier eczema sufferers are trying to protect. Avoid it in both your cleanser and your lotion.
  • Artificial dyes and colorants: These add nothing functional to a body lotion and are unnecessary potential irritants for sensitive skin.

Natural vs. Conventional Eczema Lotions — Is There a Difference?

Here's the honest answer: it depends on the severity of your eczema and what's in the formula.

For moderate to severe flares, a dermatologist may prescribe topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors, or newer biologics. No moisturizer — natural or otherwise — replaces medical treatment during an active flare. If you're dealing with significant flares, please work with a dermatologist.

That said, for daily maintenance and barrier repair between flares, well-formulated natural body lotions can be genuinely effective. The Evangelista et al. study mentioned earlier found that virgin coconut oil performed comparably to mineral oil (a standard in conventional eczema care) for reducing skin dryness and improving barrier function. That's meaningful evidence that natural ingredients can meet clinical benchmarks — they just need to be the right ingredients at effective concentrations. source

Natural lotions also have a potential advantage for people who react to the synthetic fragrances and preservatives found in many conventional drugstore eczema creams. The takeaway: natural doesn't automatically mean better, but a clean, well-formulated moisturizing body lotion with the right ingredients is a legitimate, evidence-informed option for daily eczema management.

How to Apply Body Lotion for Eczema (The Right Way)

How you apply your body moisturizer matters almost as much as what's in it. Here's the method dermatologists recommend.

  • Soak and seal: Bathe or shower in lukewarm — not hot — water for about 10 minutes. Hot water feels good but strips moisture and can worsen eczema. After bathing, gently pat your skin with a soft towel, leaving it slightly damp. Then apply your body lotion within three minutes. This "soak and seal" technique, recommended by the National Eczema Association and the American Academy of Dermatology, traps moisture in the skin instead of letting it evaporate.
  • Use enough product: A thin smear isn't enough. You want a generous, even layer over affected areas. Don't be stingy — this is the step that actually moves the needle on dryness.
  • Apply at least twice daily: Once after bathing and once before bed is the minimum. More frequent application is better on very dry or cracked areas.
  • Pat, don't rub: Use a gentle pressing or patting motion rather than rubbing. Rubbing can irritate already-inflamed skin and friction is a known eczema trigger.
  • Don't forget your hands: Hands are especially vulnerable because washing and drying strips the barrier repeatedly throughout the day. Apply a hand cream or body lotion after every hand wash, not just morning and night.

Can You Use Body Scrub or Body Wash With Eczema?

Yes — with some important caveats. During an active eczema flare, skip physical body scrubs entirely. The compromised barrier can't handle that kind of friction, and a scrub can worsen inflammation and break open already-irritated skin. During remission, gentle, infrequent exfoliation with soft, natural materials is generally fine for most people.

For body wash, the best body wash for eczema-prone skin is fragrance-free, sulfate-free, and moisturizing. Avoid anything that lathers aggressively — that's usually a sign of a high surfactant load. A gentle, moisturizing body wash preserves more of your skin barrier going into your moisturizing step, which means your body lotion works better. The cleansing step literally sets up how well everything that comes after it absorbs.

Frequently Asked Questions About Body Lotion for Eczema

Is coconut oil good for eczema?
Evidence suggests it can be helpful, particularly virgin, unrefined coconut oil. The 2014 Evangelista et al. study found it comparable to mineral oil for reducing eczema dryness and improving barrier function, with the added benefit of antimicrobial lauric acid. That said, raw coconut oil on its own may not provide enough sustained moisture — a body lotion formulated with coconut oil alongside humectants and emollients tends to work better than applying the oil alone. source

What type of lotion is best for eczema?
Look for a fragrance-free formula with natural humectants like glycerin, natural emollients like coconut oil or shea butter, and no SLS, parabens, or synthetic dyes. A clean ingredient body lotion with a short, readable ingredient list is often your best bet for eczema-prone skin.

How often should you moisturize eczema?
At least twice daily — once after bathing using the soak-and-seal method, and once before bed. During flares or in dry winter conditions (when low humidity and indoor heating worsen symptoms), more frequent application on affected areas is recommended.

Can natural body lotion replace eczema medication?
No. A moisturizing body lotion supports the skin barrier and helps manage day-to-day dryness and itch, but it is not a treatment for active eczema flares. If you're experiencing significant flares, always follow your dermatologist's guidance. Natural lotion is a maintenance and prevention tool, not a medical treatment.

Is body butter or body lotion better for eczema?
It depends on your skin's needs. Body butter is richer and more occlusive — better for very dry, cracked skin or for overnight use. Body lotion is lighter and absorbs more quickly, making it easier to apply twice daily as part of a regular routine. For most people with eczema, a good lotion for daily use and a richer body butter for severely dry spots is a practical combination.
