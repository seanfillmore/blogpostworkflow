What Makes a Toothpaste "All Natural"?

There is no legal definition of "natural" on a toothpaste label. The FDA doesn't regulate the word in personal care products. A brand can call a toothpaste natural even if it contains synthetic detergents or artificial flavors.

So read the ingredient list. A genuinely natural toothpaste uses plant-based or mineral ingredients, has no synthetic foaming agents, and has no artificial colors or flavors. Third-party seals help too. Look for EWG Verified, USDA Organic, or NATRUE.

  • Natural: the broadest, loosest claim.
  • Organic: ingredients were grown without synthetic pesticides.
  • Clean: usually means free from a specific list of flagged chemicals.
  • Vegan: no animal-derived ingredients. Most natural formulas already meet this.

Natural Toothpaste Ingredients to Look For

Use this as your label checklist when comparing natural toothpaste ingredients:

  • Baking soda: a mild abrasive that lifts surface stains and helps neutralize mouth acids. It's gentle enough for daily use.
  • Xylitol: a plant-derived sweetener. Research suggests it limits Streptococcus mutans, the main bacteria linked to tooth decay. source
  • Coconut oil: its lauric acid has shown antimicrobial effects in lab studies. It's the base of most coconut oil toothpaste.
  • Nano-hydroxyapatite: a mineral that makes up most of your enamel. Early research suggests it may help support enamel, though more studies are needed. source
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, clove, cinnamon): they freshen breath and have antimicrobial properties. Clove contains eugenol, which has shown antibacterial activity.
  • Myrrh: a resin used in oral care for centuries, with antimicrobial and soothing properties shown in several small studies.

Pet note: xylitol is toxic to dogs. If your toothpaste contains it, keep it out of reach.

Toothpaste Without Chemicals: What to Avoid

Even some "natural" brands include these. Check before you buy:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS): a synthetic detergent that makes foam. Many people find it isn't necessary for a good clean, and some find it bothersome if their mouths are sensitive.
  • Artificial sweeteners (saccharin, aspartame): they don't belong in a toothpaste with natural ingredients.
  • Triclosan: removed from most toothpastes, but worth confirming it's absent.
  • Carrageenan: a seaweed thickener with mixed research on gut irritation. Many clean brands skip it.
  • Titanium dioxide: a whitening pigment. European regulators raised concerns about it in food, so careful formulators leave it out.

Fluoride-Free Toothpaste: What You Should Know

The American Dental Association endorses fluoride for cavity prevention, backed by decades of research. Many people still prefer to skip it, and that's a personal choice.

If you go fluoride-free, look for xylitol and a remineralizing mineral like hydroxyapatite. Fluoride-free formulas have less long-term evidence than fluoride for cavity prevention, so don't treat them as equal. If you've had cavities or have a high decay risk, talk to your dentist first. Brush twice a day, floss, and keep up your dental checkups either way.

A Fresh-Tasting Toothpaste With a Smooth Feel

Some natural pastes feel grainy because they use coarse abrasives. Here's how to find a smoother one:

  • Check the abrasive. Baking soda is finer and softer than many silica or clay abrasives. Skip charcoal and coarse mineral powders if texture bothers you.
  • Look for a thickener. Ingredients like xanthan gum help keep a paste creamy and even.
  • Pick a mint-forward flavor. Peppermint and spearmint feel the freshest.

Best Organic Toothpaste

"Natural" and "certified organic" are different. USDA Organic generally means a product is made mostly with organic ingredients, and ingredients like water and minerals typically aren't counted as organic. That makes truly USDA-certified toothpaste rare. NATRUE is another credible benchmark. When shopping for the best organic toothpaste, check whether the certification covers the full formula or just a few ingredients.
