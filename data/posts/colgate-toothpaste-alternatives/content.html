What to Look for in a Toothpaste Alternative

Here's the short checklist we used to sort these options. Our natural toothpaste collection follows the same logic.

  • Fluoride status: Fluoride has the strongest evidence for preventing cavities (Cochrane, 2019; ADA). Decide on this first.
  • Foaming agent: Some pastes use SLS to foam. Others skip it.
  • Sweetener and flavor: Look for plant-based flavor oils and a sweetener you're comfortable with.
  • Abrasiveness: A paste should polish gently. Harsh grit can wear on enamel and sensitive spots.
  • ADA Seal: The Seal is voluntary. Many natural pastes don't carry it, so a missing Seal isn't a safety verdict. It does tell you a paste's claims were reviewed.

1. Coconut Oil Toothpaste

Best for: people who want a simple, plant-based daily paste. Coconut oil gives a creamy base and mild cleansing. The evidence is honest but modest.

A 2020 systematic review of coconut oil pulling (a study that pools many other studies) found mixed-to-weak results for plaque and gum health. Evidence: mixed/weak. So think of it as a gentle way to clean teeth, not a gum treatment.

2. Fluoride-Free Natural Toothpaste

Best for: adults who prefer to avoid fluoride. Be clear-eyed here. Fluoride toothpaste has strong evidence for preventing cavities, and fluoride-free pastes don't have that evidence base (Cochrane; CDC). Evidence for fluoride: strong.

These pastes fit lower-risk adults who floss, watch sugar, and keep up with checkups. Common non-fluoride ingredients include baking soda, xylitol, and hydrated silica (a gentle, sand-like mineral that polishes teeth).

3. SLS-Free Toothpaste

Best for: people prone to mouth irritation or canker sores. SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate) is a foaming agent. Some small trials link it to more recurring mouth ulcers, such as Herlofson and Barkvoll's 1994 study, but results aren't consistent. Evidence: mixed.

If your mouth often feels sore, an SLS-free paste is a low-risk experiment. Expect less foam, since cleaning comes from brushing, not bubbles. See our SLS-free toothpaste collection.

4. Hydroxyapatite Toothpaste

Best for: enamel-conscious readers who want a fluoride-free option. Hydroxyapatite is a mineral that makes up much of your tooth surface. Early reviews, like Limeback, Enax and Meyer (2023), show promise. But trials are fewer and smaller than those for fluoride. Evidence: mixed/emerging. Don't assume it equals fluoride for cavity prevention. Our formula doesn't contain it.

5. Cinnamon-Flavored Natural Toothpaste

Best for: people who miss a bold, spicy flavor without artificial flavors or dyes. The kick comes from natural oils. Our cinnamon toothpaste collection pairs cinnamon with mint for a fresh, warm finish.

One caution: cinnamon oil can bother some sensitive mouths. If your gums or tongue sting, stop using it. A flavor you enjoy also helps you brush longer, which is a real plus.

6. Xylitol-Sweetened Toothpaste

Best for: anyone avoiding saccharin and other artificial sweeteners. Xylitol is a natural sugar alcohol. The Cochrane review (Riley et al., 2015), a study that pools many other studies, found low-quality evidence for cavity reduction, so keep expectations modest. Evidence: low/mixed.

One safety note: xylitol is dangerous for dogs. Keep tubes out of pet reach. Ours uses stevia instead.

7. Simpler-Ingredient Fluoride Toothpaste

Best for: cavity-prone readers, anyone with a dentist's recommendation, or anyone who simply wants to keep fluoride. If your dentist suggested fluoride, including a prescription-strength paste, follow that advice instead of going fluoride-free. Otherwise, pick the shortest ingredient list that still has an effective fluoride level and the ADA Seal. Evidence: strong (Cochrane, 2019).

How to Switch Toothpastes Without Regret

Change one thing at a time. If you swap paste, don't also change your brush and mouthwash that week. Give the new paste two to four weeks before you judge it, and expect less foam if you go SLS-free.

Watch for stinging, new sensitivity, or sore spots. And see your dentist if you notice bleeding gums, bad breath that won't go away, lingering sensitivity, or possible cavities.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fluoride-free toothpaste safe?
Yes, it's safe to use. It just doesn't carry fluoride's proven cavity protection (Cochrane). It suits lower-risk adults. If you're cavity-prone, ask your dentist first.

Does SLS-free toothpaste work as well?
It cleans teeth the same way, through brushing. It just foams less. Whether SLS affects canker sores is still mixed, so try it if you're prone to them.

Can natural toothpaste whiten teeth?
Only modestly. Gentle polishing agents like baking soda can lift surface stains, but they don't bleach. For whitening claims, look for products reviewed under the ADA Seal program.

Is coconut oil toothpaste good for gums?
The evidence is mixed to weak (Woolley et al., 2026). It cleans gently, but it won't treat gum problems. See a dentist if your gums bleed.
