What Actually Makes a Bar Soap Moisturizing?

Three things determine whether a bar soap hydrates your skin or strips it bare. Understanding them makes every soap label easier to read.

1. Glycerin Retention

When oils are saponified — meaning they react with an alkali like sodium hydroxide to become soap — glycerin is produced naturally as a byproduct. Glycerin is a humectant, which means it draws moisture from the air to the surface of your skin. Research published in the British Journal of Dermatology has confirmed glycerin's role in supporting the skin's moisture barrier. Most commercial soap manufacturers remove this glycerin and sell it separately for use in lotions and cosmetics. Natural cold-process soap retains it.

2. Superfatting

Superfatting means a percentage of the oils in the bar are left unsaponified — they don't become soap, they stay as free oils that condition your skin while you wash. A superfat of 5–8% is typical in quality natural bars. More free oil means more moisturizing effect with every use. source

3. Oil Profile

The fatty acids in your base oils matter a lot. Coconut oil brings a rich, dense lather and lauric acid, which has natural cleansing and antimicrobial properties. Olive oil is high in oleic acid, a skin-softening fatty acid that closely resembles the skin's own sebum. Shea butter contributes stearic acid, which helps reinforce the skin's protective barrier. Different oils create different feels — and the best natural bars are formulated to balance lather, conditioning, and longevity.

Bar Soap vs. Body Wash: Which Is Better for Moisture?

Here's the straight answer: a well-formulated natural bar soap will often outperform a conventional body wash for dry skin. Most body washes rely on sulfate-based surfactants that create foam but strip the skin's lipid layer in the process. Some dermatologists recommend avoiding SLS for people with sensitive or dry skin, noting it can compromise the skin barrier over time.

Body wash does have one edge — it can hold humectants like hyaluronic acid in suspension, which a solid bar cannot easily do. But the trade-off is that most mainstream body washes include synthetic fragrance, preservatives, and surfactants that offset those benefits for sensitive skin.

Key Ingredients to Look for in a Moisturizing Bar Soap

This is your practical label guide. If you see these on the ingredient list, you're holding a bar that was built to hydrate, not just clean.

  • Saponified coconut oil: The base of most great natural bars. Produces a rich, cleansing lather and contributes lauric acid, which has natural antimicrobial properties.
  • Saponified olive oil: A classic soap-making oil high in oleic acid. Creates a softer, creamier lather and is particularly good for dry or mature skin.
  • Shea butter: Occlusive and deeply conditioning. Shea-based bars tend to feel creamy on skin and may help calm irritation.
  • Castor oil: A humectant oil that draws moisture toward the skin. Adds a slick, moisturizing quality to lather.
  • Glycerin (retained): If a bar is made via true saponification and the glycerin isn't removed, it stays in the bar and works on your skin. The label may not always call it out, but cold-process natural soap bars retain it by default.
  • Oat extract or colloidal oatmeal: Particularly soothing for sensitive or reactive skin. Often found in bars formulated for people who deal with irritation or eczema-prone skin.

On the flip side, here's what to avoid: sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), synthetic fragrance (listed as "fragrance" or "parfum"), parabens, and artificial dyes. These are common in conventional bars and are the main reason bar soap has a reputation for drying skin out.

How to Use a Moisturizing Bar Soap to Get the Best Results

Even the best natural bar soap works harder when you use it right. A few small habits make a real difference.

  • Use lukewarm water: Hot water feels good but strips natural oils from skin. Lukewarm preserves more of the moisture your skin produces on its own.
  • Lather in your hands first: Rather than dragging the bar directly across skin, build a lather in your palms or on a washcloth. It's gentler and distributes the soap more evenly.
  • Pat dry, don't rub: Rubbing with a towel creates friction and removes more moisture. Pat skin gently and leave it slightly damp.
  • Apply lotion immediately after: The best window to lock in moisture is within two to three minutes of stepping out of the shower while skin is still slightly damp. A natural body lotion or coconut oil applied now seals in what the soap left behind.
  • Use a draining soap dish: Storing your bar in standing water dissolves it fast. A dish with drainage keeps it dry between uses, which extends the bar's life significantly.

Natural Bar Soap vs. Conventional Detergent Bars — What's the Difference?

This is the distinction most people don't know, and it explains almost everything. Many products on drugstore shelves labeled as "bar soap" are technically synthetic detergent bars, or syndets. They're not made from saponified oils — they're built with surfactant systems like SLS and other synthetic compounds. The FDA draws a clear regulatory line between true soap (made by combining fats or oils with an alkali) and synthetic detergent bars, which are regulated as cosmetics or even over-the-counter drugs.

In syndet bars, glycerin is typically removed and sold separately. The result is a bar that cleans aggressively, strips natural oils, and leaves skin dry — exactly the experience that gave bar soap its bad reputation. Some studies suggest SLS can disrupt the skin barrier with repeated use, which is why dermatologists often recommend avoiding it for sensitive or dry skin.

True natural bar soap — made through real saponification of oils like organic coconut oil — retains its glycerin, contains no synthetic surfactants, and works with your skin instead of against it. Frequently Asked Questions

Is bar soap good for dry skin?
Yes — but only if it's a natural, superfatted bar made with real saponified oils and retained glycerin. Conventional detergent bars strip skin and are often the cause of dryness people blame on "bar soap" in general. Many people with dry skin find that a natural cold-process bar with a good superfat level is a comfortable, moisturizing option for daily use.

What ingredients make a bar soap moisturizing?
The main ones are retained glycerin, saponified coconut oil, saponified olive oil, shea butter, and castor oil. Glycerin draws moisture to the skin surface. The oils condition skin during washing. Together, they keep the bar from stripping more than it gives back. See the ingredient section above for the full guide.

Is bar soap better than body wash for sensitive skin?
It depends entirely on the formulation. A natural bar soap with no SLS, no synthetic fragrance, and retained glycerin is often gentler for sensitive skin than a conventional body wash built on sulfate surfactants. The format matters less than the ingredient list.

Can I use moisturizing bar soap on my face?
Some natural bars are suitable for facial use, but it depends on the formula. Bars with a very high coconut oil percentage can be comedogenic — meaning they may clog pores — for some people, particularly on the face. Bars with no synthetic fragrance and a more balanced oil profile (coconut plus olive, for example) are a safer starting point for facial use. When in doubt, test on a small area first.
