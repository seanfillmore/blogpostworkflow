Non Toxic Toothpaste: Ingredients to Avoid (and Check) Before You Buy

Non toxic toothpaste has no official definition, so no rule tells you what the label means. The fix is simple. Check seven ingredients shoppers often ask about, weigh the evidence behind each one, and choose a formula that fits your mouth and your dentist's advice. Below, each ingredient gets a plain-language evidence note.

We cover fluoride-free toothpaste options too. And we cover fluoride itself candidly, because it deserves an honest look.

What "Non-Toxic Toothpaste" Actually Means

In the US, fluoride toothpaste is regulated as an over-the-counter drug. Toothpaste without fluoride is generally treated as a cosmetic. You can read how the FDA draws that line on its cosmetic vs. drug page. "Non-toxic" is just a marketing word.

Toxicity also depends on dose. Toothpaste is spit out, not swallowed, so the amount that reaches your body is small. That's why "synthetic" doesn't automatically mean "dangerous."

Here is how we describe the evidence:

  • Strong evidence: Multiple good studies or a regulatory decision back the claim.
  • Mixed evidence: Small or conflicting studies. Reasonable people can differ.
  • Weak evidence: Little direct proof of harm or benefit.

Not every item below is equally risky. Some are worth avoiding. Others are simply optional.

1. Triclosan and Other Antibacterial Additives

In 2016, the FDA finalized a rule removing triclosan from consumer antiseptic washes. Makers hadn't shown the ingredients were safe for long-term daily use or better than plain soap. That rule covered washes, not toothpaste.

A Cochrane review did find modest plaque benefits from triclosan toothpastes. Many brands have dropped triclosan, but scan older or imported tubes anyway. Essential oils are a popular plant-based alternative, though evidence for them is limited.

Evidence: Strong for the regulatory decision. Weak for natural alternatives.

2. Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)

SLS is a foaming agent. It isn't considered cancer-causing, and it isn't "toxic" at toothpaste doses. The honest concern is irritation. A few small studies, such as Herlofson & Barkvoll (1994) and Chahine et al. (1997), suggest it may aggravate recurring canker sores in some people.

The samples were small, so the findings aren't conclusive. If you get frequent canker sores or have a sensitive mouth, trying a toothpaste without SLS (also called sodium lauryl sulfate free toothpaste) is a reasonable experiment.

Evidence: Mixed.

3. Artificial Sweeteners, Dyes and Flavors

Saccharin and synthetic dyes (FD&C colors) are generally permitted by the FDA at the levels people are exposed to. You can browse the FDA's color additive information. We can't point to strong evidence that they harm you in toothpaste.

The better reason to skip them is simple. They add nothing your teeth need. A dye-free toothpaste keeps the label shorter.

Natural flavors like mint or cinnamon are an easy swap. Just know that strong spice oils can bother a few sensitive mouths.

Evidence of harm: Weak.

4. Overly Abrasive Ingredients (Including Charcoal)

Abrasives scrub off stains and plaque. Too much scrubbing can wear down enamel over time. Abrasivity is measured with an RDA value. That stands for Relative Dentin Abrasivity, or how rough a paste is on your teeth. Lower numbers are gentler, so a lower RDA means less wear on your enamel.

Charcoal toothpaste is the one people ask about most. A literature review in the Journal of the American Dental Association (Brooks et al., 2017) found not enough evidence that charcoal dentifrices (a fancy word for toothpastes and tooth powders) are safe or effective, and it raised abrasiveness concerns.

Gentler abrasives include hydrated silica and calcium carbonate. If a label doesn't list an RDA, ask the maker.

Evidence: Mixed, and limited.

5. Heavy Metal Contamination

Lead and arsenic aren't ingredients. They're contaminants, which makes this a quality-control issue. They can show up in some clays and minerals, and in poorly regulated imports, including some tooth powders. The CDC explains why lead exposure matters, especially for children.

Don't assume "natural" means contaminated. And don't assume it means tested, either. If you want a lead free toothpaste, look for brands that publish third-party heavy-metal results. If none are posted, you can email the company and ask.

Evidence: Mixed. The risk is real in principle, but it varies by product.

6. Titanium Dioxide and Unnecessary Whiteners

Titanium dioxide makes products look bright white. In 2021, EFSA concluded it could no longer be considered safe as a food additive, citing uncertainty about genotoxicity (possible DNA damage). The EU banned it in food in 2022.

That ruling was about food, not toothpaste. It doesn't directly apply here. Skipping titanium dioxide is a precautionary, optional choice, not a must.

Evidence for toothpaste: Mixed to weak.

7. Fluoride: What the Evidence Says (and Who Should Be Careful)

Fluoride toothpaste has strong evidence for preventing cavities. A Cochrane review (Walsh et al., 2019) found it works, and dental groups like the ADA recommend it. Sodium fluoride and stannous fluoride (a tin-based fluoride that also helps with gum health) toothpaste are the common types.

The real risk is dental fluorosis, faint white marks on teeth caused by swallowing too much fluoride while teeth form. The CDC and ADA advise a rice-grain smear for kids under 3 and a pea-size amount for ages 3 to 6, with supervision.

The 2024 NTP monograph on fluoride and neurodevelopment looked at higher drinking-water exposure. It isn't a finding about toothpaste.

Some people choose a non-fluoride toothpaste for personal preference, young children, or fluoridated water. That's a valid choice. If you get cavities easily, talk with your dentist first.

Evidence: Strong for cavity benefit. Mixed for non-dental concerns.

Label-Reading Checklist: How to Choose a Non-Toxic Toothpaste

Use this quick list at the shelf or online.

  • Fluoride status: Decide if you want it, based on your cavity risk and your dentist's advice.
  • The seven ingredients above: Scan for the ones you'd rather skip.
  • Full ingredient list: If the brand doesn't publish one, move on.
  • Third-party testing: Look for published heavy-metal results.
  • Abrasivity info: Look for an RDA value, or ask the maker.
  • Flavor: Pick one you'll actually use twice a day.
  • Age guidance: Check the recommended age, especially for kids.
  • Your dentist: Share your choice at your next visit.

One more note. The ADA Seal of Acceptance is a useful signal, but toothpaste with the Seal has typically contained fluoride. Most fluoride-free products won't carry it, and that doesn't make them unsafe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is non-toxic toothpaste as effective as regular toothpaste?

It depends on fluoride and your cavity risk. Fluoride toothpaste has strong evidence for preventing cavities. Fluoride-free pastes still clean teeth and freshen breath, but they haven't shown the same cavity benefit. If you get cavities easily, talk with your dentist before switching.

Is fluoride-free toothpaste safe for kids?

Swallowing a small amount is generally fine. But cavity prevention matters for kids, so discuss it with a pediatric dentist. Dentists often recommend infant toothpaste with fluoride, used in a rice-grain smear for young children, as the default.

Is hydroxyapatite toothpaste better than fluoride?

Not proven. Early research on hydroxyapatite looks promising, especially for sensitivity. But the evidence is much less extensive than for fluoride, and no study has shown it to be definitively better. Many dentists still start with fluoride.

Is SLS dangerous?

Not at toothpaste doses. SLS is a common foaming agent and isn't considered toxic here. Some people find it can irritate their mouths or bother canker sores. If that sounds like you, an SLS-free paste is worth a try.

Can I make my own toothpaste?

You can, but we don't recommend relying on it. Homemade baking soda or clay mixes are unregulated and can be too abrasive. They also don't replace fluoride. If you want a simple label, a well-made natural toothpaste is a safer route.

The Bottom Line

"Non-toxic" means reading labels and weighing evidence. It doesn't mean avoiding everything unfamiliar. Match the formula to your needs, and talk to your dentist about fluoride.
