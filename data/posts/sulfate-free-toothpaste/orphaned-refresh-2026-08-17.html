What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — also called lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or SDS — is a synthetic surfactant. It creates the foam in most conventional toothpastes, shampoos, and body washes. In toothpaste, its only real job is to make the paste spread across your teeth and produce lather.

Here's the thing: that foam doesn't clean better. Plaque removal comes from brushing mechanics — your technique and how long you spend. The lather is a sensory signal, not a cleaning mechanism. Manufacturers kept using SLS because people associate foam with clean. It's cheap, reliable, and consumer-tested. That's it.

The compound is often labeled as "coconut-derived" because it starts from lauryl alcohol. But the final product is highly processed. It behaves nothing like coconut oil in your mouth.

What SLS Actually Does to Your Mouth

SLS is a known irritant, and the mouth is one of the most sensitive places it ends up. Three effects are well-documented.

Canker sores. A 1994 study in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (Herlofson and Barkvoll) found that people using SLS-free toothpaste had significantly fewer canker sore episodes than those using SLS toothpaste. Follow-up research has supported this consistently. If you get recurring mouth sores, switching to a lauryl sulfate free toothpaste is one of the most evidence-backed steps you can take.

Raw or peeling gums. Your mouth is lined with a thin protective mucin layer. SLS strips it. When that barrier is gone, the tissue underneath gets irritated and raw. This isn't an allergy — it's a direct physical effect of a surfactant doing something it wasn't meant to do.

Bitter taste after brushing. SLS suppresses sweet taste receptors and amplifies bitter ones. That's why orange juice tastes awful right after brushing. It's not the mint flavor — it's SLS temporarily changing how your taste buds work.

Are Sulfate-Free Pastes Better for Sensitive Gums?

Yes — and the reason is straightforward. SLS strips the mucin layer that protects your gum tissue. When gums are already sensitive or inflamed, that stripping effect makes things worse. Switching to a sulphate free toothpaste lets that protective layer stay intact, which means less irritation after every brush.

People most likely to notice an immediate difference:

  • Anyone with recurrent canker sores
  • People with sensitive or inflamed gums
  • Orthodontic patients (braces and aligners already create friction — SLS adds to it)
  • Children with developing enamel and sensitive oral tissue
  • Anyone who notices peeling or rawness on the inside of their cheeks

If none of those apply to you, you can still benefit — you may just notice it more gradually.

Does SLS-Free Toothpaste Clean as Well?

Yes. Plaque removal depends on brushing technique and active cleaning agents — not foam volume. Studies consistently show that time spent brushing and bristle contact are the dominant variables. Lather contributes nothing to either.

Gentle surfactant alternatives — like sodium cocoyl glutamate, coco-glucoside, lauryl glucoside, and decyl glucoside — are derived from coconut or corn. They help the paste spread and loosen debris without stripping your oral mucin layer. Cavity-protective ingredients like fluoride, nano hydroxyapatite, and baking soda work the same way regardless of which surfactant is present. Removing SLS doesn't reduce cleaning power. It removes the irritant.

How to Read the Label on a No SLS Toothpaste

"Natural" and even "sulfate-free" claims on the front of the pack aren't regulated in most markets. Read the full ingredient list.

SLS has multiple names. Look for: sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, and SDS. Check for all three.

Good surfactant alternatives to look for. Sodium cocoyl glutamate, coco-glucoside, lauryl glucoside, decyl glucoside. These are coconut- or corn-derived and clean without the harsh stripping effect.

Other ingredients worth avoiding in toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate: saccharin (an artificial sweetener still common in "natural" brands), synthetic dyes, and carrageenan. None of these are SLS, but they're worth flagging if a clean ingredient list is your goal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sulfate-free toothpaste better for sensitive gums?

Yes. SLS strips the protective mucin layer lining your mouth, which makes sensitive gum tissue more vulnerable after every brush. Switching to a lauryl sulphate free toothpaste lets that barrier stay intact. Most people with sensitive gums notice less irritation within a few weeks.

Can I get sulfate-free toothpaste with fluoride?

Yes. Many no SLS toothpaste formulas include sodium fluoride or stannous fluoride. Others use nano hydroxyapatite as a fluoride-free alternative. Real Skin Care's formula skips fluoride and relies on baking soda and coconut oil. If your dentist recommends fluoride, look for an SLS-free formula that lists it in the active ingredients.

Does sulfate-free toothpaste foam less?

Yes, usually. SLS is specifically chosen for its foaming ability. Gentler alternatives like coco-glucoside produce less lather. Your teeth will still get clean — the reduced foam just takes some getting used to if you're switching from a conventional paste.

Is SLS-free the same as sulfate-free?

Effectively, yes. In toothpaste, the sulfate most people want to avoid is sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). "Sulfate-free," "SLS-free," "lauryl sulfate free," and "no sodium lauryl sulfate" all refer to the same thing. Check the ingredient list regardless of which label claim appears on the front.

Is affordable sulfate-free toothpaste actually available?

Yes. SLS-free formulas used to be a specialty item, but in 2026 they're widely available at most price points. Real Skin Care's toothpaste is competitive with mainstream drugstore options. You don't need to spend more to go SLS-free — you just need to know what to look for on the label.
