Why People Switch From Native Deodorant

Native isn't a bad product — it works well for plenty of people. But individual body chemistry means one all natural deodorant formula rarely works for everyone. Here are the most common reasons people go looking for a healthy deodorant alternative.

  • Baking soda sensitivity: Native's original formula uses sodium bicarbonate as its main odor-fighting agent. Baking soda is effective, but it's also alkaline — and for some people, that pH disrupts the skin barrier under the arm, causing redness, rash, or itching. This is one of the most-reported complaints across natural deodorant brands generally.
  • Formula changes: Some longtime Native users report that the product stopped working the way it used to. Formula tweaks happen in any brand, and they can affect both efficacy and skin feel.
  • Price: At around $14 per stick for a product you use daily, cost adds up. Some shoppers want comparable performance at a lower price point.
  • Packaging and sustainability: Native's plastic packaging has drawn criticism from shoppers building a lower-waste personal care routine.
  • Ingredient simplicity: Some users want a shorter, more recognizable ingredient list — fewer additives, more plant-based ingredients, and cleaner sourcing. If that resonates, it's worth knowing what the Native brand name actually means across their product line.

2. Magnesium-Based Deodorant — Best for Sensitive Skin

If baking soda is the ingredient that drove you away from Native, magnesium hydroxide is worth understanding. It works by raising the skin's surface pH to a level where odor-causing bacteria struggle to thrive — similar in concept to baking soda, but gentler because it's less alkaline.

This makes it a natural aluminum-free deodorant option well-suited for people with reactive underarm skin. A good magnesium formula will pair the active ingredient with a soothing carrier (aloe, coconut oil) and keep the overall ingredient list clean.

One honest caveat: magnesium-based deodorants may require reapplication on high-sweat days. They don't absorb moisture the way arrowroot or kaolin clay do, and heavier sweaters sometimes find them less reliable after intense activity. But for everyday use on sensitive skin, they're a solid category to explore. See how they compare in our guide to the best natural deodorant for sensitive skin.

3. Activated Charcoal Deodorant — Best for Heavy Sweaters

Activated charcoal is a porous carbon material with a high surface area — and that structure allows it to adsorb (bind to) odor compounds and excess moisture rather than simply masking them. It's a good category for people who found Native insufficient on active days or in warmer months.

4. Probiotic Deodorant — Best Long-Term Odor Solution

Probiotic deodorants take a different approach than most. Instead of fighting odor after it develops, they aim to shift the underarm microbiome — the community of bacteria living on your skin — toward strains that produce less odor over time.

The science here is genuinely interesting but still emerging. Early research on skin microbiome modulation suggests that introducing beneficial bacterial strains can reduce populations of odor-producing species — but most probiotic deodorant studies are small or preliminary. So keep expectations calibrated: this is a promising approach, not a guaranteed fix.

If you try a probiotic deodorant, plan for a 2–4 week adjustment period before evaluating efficacy. The microbiome doesn't shift overnight. Look for formulas with shelf-stable probiotic strains and essential-oil-only scenting to avoid fragrance interference.

5. Mineral/Crystal Deodorant — Best Budget-Friendly Alternative

Mineral salt deodorants — usually made from potassium alum crystals — are one of the most misunderstood products in the natural deodorant space. Here's the key distinction: potassium alum is structurally different from the aluminum chlorohydrate or aluminum zirconium compounds used in antiperspirants. It doesn't block sweat ducts. Instead, it creates a mineral surface environment that's inhospitable to odor-producing bacteria.

The practical appeal is real. One crystal can last months, which makes this the lowest-cost-per-use option in any natural deodorant comparison. The ingredient list is essentially one item.

The limitations are also real: crystals must be applied to wet skin to work, they offer zero moisture absorption, and lighter sweaters will get more mileage than heavier ones. But for minimalists or anyone building a lower-waste, vegan personal care routine, mineral deodorant is worth serious consideration. 7. Chlorophyll Deodorant — Best for Whole-Body Odor Support

Chlorophyll deodorants are a niche but growing category. The idea is that chlorophyll — typically sourced from alfalfa or wheatgrass — may neutralize odor compounds rather than simply covering them up. Some older clinical studies looked at internal chlorophyll use as a systemic deodorizer, with modest results. Topical evidence is much thinner.

To be honest about the evidence: chlorophyll deodorant research is limited. This is a promising ingredient category, not a proven powerhouse. If you're curious, it's a low-risk experiment — chlorophyll is safe and gentle. But go in with realistic expectations, and don't ditch a formula that's working just to chase a trend.

Some people interested in whole-body odor support combine topical chlorophyll deodorants with dietary sources — leafy greens and chlorophyll supplements. That combination is worth exploring if odor feels systemic rather than purely surface-level.

8. Fragrance-Free Natural Deodorant — Best for Sensitive Skin and Allergies

Even "natural" fragrances can irritate freshly shaved or sensitive underarm skin. Citrus essential oils, in particular, can be photosensitizing. If you've reacted to Native's scented formulas — or to essential oil blends in other natural deodorants — a truly fragrance-free formula may be what you need.

One label distinction worth knowing: fragrance-free means no added fragrance compounds at all, including masking agents. Unscented sometimes means masking agents are used to cover the product's natural smell — which means fragrance ingredients are still present. If you're avoiding all fragrance, check the full INCI ingredient list, not just the front label claim.

How to Transition From Native to a New Natural Deodorant

Switching deodorants — especially if you've been using an antiperspirant — comes with a short adjustment period. Antiperspirants plug sweat ducts with aluminum compounds. When you stop, those ducts reopen and sweat flow normalizes. That process takes roughly one to three weeks, and during that window, odor can temporarily seem worse. This is normal, not a sign the new product isn't working.

A few tips that make the transition easier:

  • Apply to clean, dry skin. Natural deodorants work best when there's no residual bacteria or old product on the surface.
  • Less is more. With a roll-on, one or two passes is enough. Overapplying doesn't improve performance and can lead to buildup.
  • Patch test first if you've had baking soda reactions before — apply to the inner arm for 24 hours before using underarm.
  • Watch your diet. Research shows that foods like red meat, alcohol, and cruciferous vegetables can influence body odor — not just your deodorant choice.

Give any new formula at least three weeks before deciding it doesn't work. Body chemistry and the underarm microbiome both need time to adjust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is naturally-marketed deodorant actually natural?
Many popular deodorant brands use some naturally derived ingredients, but their formulas often include synthetic compounds too. If ingredient transparency matters to you, read the full ingredient list rather than just trusting the "natural" label, since that term isn't regulated in the US. Real Skin Care and other truly natural deodorant brands offer shorter, more fully plant-based ingredient lists you can actually understand.

What is the healthiest natural deodorant alternative to conventional deodorants?
For most people, the healthiest options are magnesium hydroxide-based or coconut oil-based formulas. Both offer effective odor control through gentler, well-understood mechanisms without synthetic additives. Real Skin Care's coconut oil deodorant is one of the cleanest formulas in this category, with a fully organic ingredient list and no synthetic preservatives or stabilizers.

Do natural deodorant alternatives work as well as conventional deodorants?
Efficacy varies by body chemistry. Natural deodorant works by fighting odor bacteria — it doesn't stop sweat the way an antiperspirant does. The key is matching the formula's active mechanism to your specific needs: baking soda or magnesium for strong odor control, coconut oil for antimicrobial action, charcoal for moisture management. Most people find a formula that works well within a few tries.

Are natural deodorants vegan?
Many are — but not all. Some stick and cream deodorants use beeswax as a base or texture agent, which makes them non-vegan. To be sure, look for an explicit vegan certification on the label, and check the full ingredient list for beeswax (cera alba).
