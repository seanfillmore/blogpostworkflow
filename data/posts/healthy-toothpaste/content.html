What Makes a Toothpaste Actually Healthy?

Toothpaste does four main jobs: it removes plaque mechanically (with mild abrasives), fights bacteria (with antimicrobial agents), protects enamel, and freshens breath. A healthy toothpaste accomplishes all four without relying on synthetic chemicals that don't add value — or that may cause problems for some people.

Broadly, toothpastes fall into two camps. Conventional formulas lean on synthetic active agents — things like triclosan, sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), artificial flavors, and saccharin. All natural toothpaste and organic toothpaste take a different approach: plant-derived ingredients, mineral abrasives, and herbal antimicrobials do the same work without the synthetic additives.

The PMC review cited above looked specifically at herbal toothpaste ingredients — plants like neem, miswak, myrrh, and clove — and found strong evidence for their antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. That's meaningful. It means a clean, natural organic toothpaste isn't just a marketing concept. When the ingredient list is thoughtfully built, it genuinely works.

Key Ingredients to Look for in a Healthy Toothpaste

Not all "natural" labels mean the same thing. Here's what research-backed clean ingredients actually look like — and what each one does for your mouth.

  • Coconut oil: Organic virgin coconut oil is approximately 50% lauric acid, a fatty acid with well-documented antimicrobial activity. A study published in the Journal of Contemporary Dental Practice found that lauric acid is effective against Streptococcus mutans, the primary bacteria behind tooth decay. Oil pulling — swishing coconut oil — has been used for centuries in Ayurvedic oral care, and modern research supports the antimicrobial rationale behind it. It's a cornerstone of any toothpaste with natural ingredients built for a healthy mouth.
  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate): Baking soda is one of the most thoroughly studied ingredients in dental care. It's a mild abrasive that lifts surface stains without scratching enamel, and it also neutralizes the acids that erode enamel over time. Research published in the Journal of the American Dental Association consistently supports baking soda toothpastes for plaque removal and whitening — making it a staple of natural whitening toothpaste formulas.
  • Hydroxyapatite: This mineral is the same material enamel is made from. Nano-hydroxyapatite has emerged as a promising fluoride alternative, with studies showing it can help repair early enamel damage and reduce sensitivity. Research published in the Journal of Dentistry found nano-hydroxyapatite performed comparably to fluoride in enamel remineralization — which is why it's gaining traction as a mineral toothpaste ingredient.
  • Myrrh: Wildcrafted myrrh has been used in oral care for thousands of years — and for good reason. Modern research supports its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, making it a genuine healthy gums toothpaste ingredient. It's included in Real Skin Care's formula alongside other botanicals for exactly this reason.
  • Clove and cinnamon essential oils: Both have well-documented antimicrobial activity in peer-reviewed literature. Clove's active compound, eugenol, is widely used in dentistry for its antiseptic and analgesic properties. Cinnamon essential oil has shown activity against oral pathogens in multiple studies. These make cinnamon toothpaste formulas more than just good-tasting — they're functionally useful for a healthy mouth.
  • Xylitol and stevia: Xylitol is a plant-derived sweetener with a documented anti-caries effect — bacteria can't ferment it into the acids that cause cavities. The American Dental Association has acknowledged its role in cavity prevention. Stevia, another plant-based sweetener, provides sweetness without feeding oral bacteria. Both are cleaner alternatives to saccharin or aspartame.

Ingredients to Avoid in Conventional Toothpaste

Knowing what to skip is just as useful as knowing what to look for. Here are five ingredients that health-conscious consumers commonly avoid — explained calmly, with context.

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS): SLS is a synthetic detergent added to toothpaste to create foam. Foam doesn't improve cleaning — it just feels like it does. More importantly, some research suggests SLS can irritate the soft tissue inside your mouth, and studies have linked it to increased canker sore frequency in sensitive individuals. If you deal with recurring mouth sores, switching to an SLS-free toothpaste is often the first thing dental professionals recommend. See our deeper look at why SLS-free toothpaste is worth the switch.
  • Triclosan: This antibiotic agent was once common in toothpaste and hand soap. The FDA scrutinized it heavily and banned it from hand soaps in 2016 due to concerns about antibiotic resistance and potential hormonal disruption. Most major toothpaste brands have since removed it from their formulas, but it's worth checking labels on older stock.
  • Artificial sweeteners (saccharin, aspartame): Some research suggests these sweeteners may affect the gut microbiome, though the evidence in oral care specifically is still emerging. A hedged "some studies indicate potential disruption" is the honest framing here — but if you're choosing a natural fluoride-free toothpaste, there's no reason to include them when plant-derived sweeteners work just as well.
  • Parabens: Synthetic preservatives like methylparaben and propylparaben have raised endocrine-disruption concerns in high-dose studies. The concentrations in toothpaste are low, but many consumers reasonably prefer to avoid them altogether when clean alternatives exist.
  • Artificial dyes and flavors: These add color and taste but provide no oral health benefit. Some individuals report sensitivity reactions, and they're unnecessary in any well-formulated all natural fluoride free toothpaste that uses essential oils for flavor instead.

The Fluoride Question — What You Need to Know

Fluoride has one of the strongest evidence bases in dentistry. Decades of research show it helps prevent cavities by strengthening enamel, and mainstream dental organizations — including the American Dental Association — recommend fluoridated toothpaste for most adults and children over two.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label — A Practical Checklist

Here's something worth knowing: "natural" is not a regulated term on personal care labels in the United States. A product can call itself natural while containing synthetic detergents, artificial flavors, and preservatives. That's why label literacy matters more than marketing language. When you're evaluating any toothpaste — including options from our natural toothpaste — here's what to check:

  • Recognizable ingredients: Can you identify every item on the list? If you can't pronounce it and can't find a plain-language explanation for what it does, that's worth questioning.
  • No SLS, parabens, or artificial dyes: These three are the most common unnecessary additives in conventional formulas. Their absence is a good baseline signal.
  • Claim vs. ingredient match: If it says "natural peppermint flavor," check whether it lists an actual essential oil or just "flavor" — "flavor" is a catch-all that can include synthetic compounds.
  • Sweetener source: Look for xylitol or stevia rather than saccharin or aspartame.
  • Cruelty-free and vegan status: If this matters to you, look for a certification or confirm directly with the brand — labels don't always make it obvious.
  • Third-party certifications: USDA Organic, Non-GMO Project Verified, or EWG Verified ratings add a layer of accountability that self-reported "natural" claims don't.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the healthiest toothpaste you can use?

The healthiest toothpaste uses plant-derived or mineral-based ingredients, avoids SLS, artificial dyes, and synthetic preservatives, and is matched to your specific needs — whether that's whitening, sensitivity relief, or a fluoride-free formula. There's no single universal answer, but a short, recognizable ingredient list is a reliable starting point.

Is natural toothpaste as effective as conventional toothpaste?

Yes — when formulated with proven active ingredients. A 2022 review published in PMC (Kanouté et al., PMC9585602) found that natural and herbal toothpaste formulas can match conventional chemical toothpastes for antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties. Ingredients like coconut oil, baking soda, myrrh, and clove all have peer-reviewed support for their oral health benefits.

Can I use natural toothpaste for my infant or toddler?

Natural infant toothpaste is generally a good choice for very young children because it avoids harsh synthetic additives that kids may swallow. Fluoride needs vary by age and cavity risk, so talk to your pediatric dentist about the right formula for your child's specific situation before making the switch.

What makes a toothpaste vegan?

Vegan toothpaste avoids animal-derived ingredients like glycerin sourced from tallow (animal fat), carmine (a red dye made from crushed insects), and propolis (a resin collected by bees). If a formula uses plant-based glycerin or no glycerin at all, and skips animal-derived colorants, it qualifies as vegan. Always verify with the brand if you're unsure about sourcing.
