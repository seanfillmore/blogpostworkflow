What Does "Natural Soap" Actually Mean?

The word "natural" carries different meanings in personal care, and it's worth separating them. Sometimes it describes soap made with plant-based ingredients instead of synthetic ones. Other times it's used as a marketing term without strict rules behind it. That's why it helps to read ingredient labels and understand what "natural" really means for the products you use.

The first is the older, more literal meaning: soap made with indigenous or traditionally used ingredients. Think coconut oil pressed from tropical palms, olive oil from Mediterranean groves, or clay minerals pulled from the earth. These are ingredients humans have been using for cleaning themselves for thousands of years.

The second meaning is the modern marketing one. "Native" has become shorthand for clean, minimal, and unprocessed — a signal that a product isn't loaded with synthetic additives. You'll see this framing across personal care: native deodorant, native shampoo, native soap.

Here's the catch: neither "native" nor "natural" is a regulated term for soap in the United States. No brand is legally required to prove its product meets any standard to use these words. That means the label alone isn't enough. You need to understand what's actually inside — and that's exactly what the rest of this post covers.

The History of Natural Soap-Making

Soap has been around for at least 4,500 years. The earliest known soap-like substance was found in ancient Mesopotamia — a mixture of animal fat and wood ash that could remove grease and grime. Egyptians used similar fat-and-ash formulas for bathing and treating skin conditions.

By the Middle Ages, the Castile region of Spain had become famous for producing soap made entirely from olive oil. Castile soap — named for that region — became a benchmark for purity. The formula was simple: olive oil, water, and lye (sodium hydroxide). The saponification process, where lye reacts with fat to form soap and glycerin, hadn't changed much in principle since ancient times.

Across South Asia, people were using an entirely different approach: soap nuts. These are the dried shells of the Sapindus mukorossi fruit, and they contain saponin, a natural surfactant that creates a mild lather in water. They've been used as a plant-based cleanser for centuries and remain popular today.

One important thing traditional soap-making preserves: glycerin. When fats and lye react, glycerin is a natural byproduct. It's a humectant — it draws moisture to the skin. Commercial soap manufacturers often remove the glycerin to sell separately, which is one reason handmade cold-process soap tends to feel more moisturizing than a mass-market bar. According to the FDA's guide to soap regulation, most "soap" sold today is legally classified as a cosmetic or detergent — not true soap at all.

Coconut oil: This is the workhorse of natural soap-making. Coconut oil is high in lauric acid, a medium-chain fatty acid with well-documented antimicrobial properties. A study published in the Journal of Nutritional Biochemistry found that lauric acid shows meaningful activity against certain bacteria and viruses. When saponified — that is, reacted with lye to become soap — coconut oil produces a firm bar with rich lather. Real Skin Care's coconut soap uses saponified organic virgin coconut oil as its base.

Olive oil (castile soap): Olive oil is high in oleic acid, which makes it deeply moisturizing and gentle. True castile soap is made entirely or primarily from olive oil — no animal fats, no synthetic detergents. It's one of the most skin-compatible soap bases available, particularly for sensitive or dry skin.

Goat milk: Goat milk soap contains lactic acid, an alpha hydroxy acid (AHA) that gently exfoliates dead skin cells and supports a healthy skin barrier. Research published in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology confirms that AHAs improve skin texture and hydration at appropriate concentrations. Goat milk also has a pH close to human skin, making it a gentle choice for sensitive skin types.

Turmeric: Turmeric soap bars have become popular in both DIY and artisan soap communities. The active compound is curcumin, which has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties backed by a substantial body of research. It's particularly appealing for skin that's prone to redness or dullness.

Tallow: Rendered animal fat — typically beef — has been used in bar soaps for millennia. Tallow's fatty acid profile closely mirrors human sebum, which makes it exceptionally skin-compatible. It's not a plant-based option, but it is a traditional, minimally processed one. If you're looking for plant-based alternatives, explore Real Skin Care's vegan soap options.

Shea butter and cocoa butter: Both are highly emollient and suitable for dry or sensitive skin. They add a creamy, nourishing quality to bar soaps and help offset any drying effect from high-coconut-oil formulas.

Ingredients to Avoid in Commercial Soap

Knowing what's worth avoiding is just as useful as knowing what to look for. Here are the ingredients that separate a truly natural soap from a conventional one.

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) and sodium laureth sulfate (SLES): These are synthetic surfactants added to create lather. They're effective cleaners, but they can strip the skin's natural moisture barrier and trigger irritation — especially in people with eczema or sensitive skin. The EWG Skin Deep database rates SLS as a moderate hazard, noting documented skin irritation concerns.
  • Synthetic fragrance ("fragrance" or "parfum"): These two words can legally hide hundreds of unlisted chemical compounds, including potential allergens and endocrine-disrupting substances. If a soap lists "fragrance" without naming the actual source, that's a flag. Natural soaps use essential oils — like orange, lavender, or spearmint — which are disclosed on the label.
  • Parabens: Preservatives such as methylparaben and propylparaben have been studied for potential hormonal effects, and while the science is still evolving, many consumers and formulators prefer to skip them.
  • Artificial dyes: These add color and nothing else. They offer no skin benefit and can be irritating for reactive skin types.
  • Triclosan: This antibacterial agent was once common in hand soaps. In 2016, the FDA banned triclosan from over-the-counter consumer antiseptic washes, citing insufficient evidence of safety and effectiveness. It should not appear in any soap sold today — but it's worth knowing why it was removed.

None of this is meant to cause alarm. The goal is simply to help you read a label with confidence.

Bar Soap vs. Liquid Soap vs. Foaming Soap — Which Is Most Natural?

This is a common question, and the honest answer is: it depends on the formula, not the format.

Traditional cold-process bar soap is often the purest form of natural soap. The saponification process requires only fat, water, and lye — and when done well, the result retains natural glycerin and requires no additional preservatives. Bar soap also tends to have a smaller packaging footprint.

Liquid soaps require a slightly different chemistry. They're typically made with potassium hydroxide instead of sodium hydroxide, which keeps the soap in liquid form. Because liquid soaps are water-based, they usually need a preservative to prevent microbial growth. A well-formulated liquid soap will use gentle, plant-derived preservatives — but lower-quality options may rely on synthetic ones.

Foaming soaps are simply diluted liquid soaps dispensed through a mechanical pump that introduces air. The foaming action is entirely physical — no extra chemicals needed to create the bubbles. That means a carefully formulated foaming soap can be just as gentle and clean as a bar. For very dry skin, an oil-based body wash can offer an even more nourishing cleanse — the oils help maintain the skin barrier while still removing dirt and bacteria.

How to Choose the Right Natural Soap for Your Skin Type

The right soap depends on what your skin actually needs. Here's a simple guide.

  • Dry or sensitive skin: Look for soaps with goat milk, shea butter, cocoa butter, or coconut oil. Avoid SLS and synthetic fragrance. An unscented soap or one scented only with essential oils is your safest bet. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends gentle, fragrance-free cleansers for dry and sensitive skin types.
  • Oily or acne-prone skin: Castile soap, turmeric soap, and clay-based bars can help balance oil production without over-stripping the skin. Curcumin's anti-inflammatory properties may help calm the redness that comes with breakouts.
  • Normal skin: Most natural bar soaps will work well. Prioritize a short, recognizable ingredient list — if you can't identify most of what's listed, that's a sign to look elsewhere.
  • Men's skin: Natural bar soaps with sea salt, cedar, or activated charcoal are popular for good reason — they cleanse effectively without the synthetic fragrance overload common in conventional men's products. Frequently Asked Questions About Native and Natural Soap

What is the difference between castile soap and regular soap?
Castile soap is made entirely or primarily from plant oils — traditionally olive oil — with no animal fats or synthetic detergents. Conventional commercial soap often uses synthetic surfactants like SLS, artificial fragrance, and stabilizers that have no equivalent in a traditional castile formula.

Is handmade soap better for your skin than commercial soap?
Often, yes — specifically because handmade cold-process soap retains the glycerin produced during saponification. Commercial manufacturers typically remove that glycerin to sell separately, which strips one of the most naturally moisturizing elements from the finished bar. The result is that handmade soap tends to feel softer on skin for most people.

What does sulfate-free soap mean?
It means the formula skips sodium lauryl sulfate and similar synthetic surfactants, relying instead on gentler plant-derived cleansers. This matters most if you have sensitive, dry, or eczema-prone skin — SLS can disrupt the skin barrier and cause irritation with repeated use.

Can I use natural soap on my face?
Yes, but choose carefully. Look for a formula designed for facial skin, or one based on gentle oils like castile or coconut oil. If you have acne-prone skin, be cautious with soaps that are very high in coconut oil — its high lauric acid content is antimicrobial but can be comedogenic (pore-clogging) for some people at high concentrations.

What are soap nuts?
Soap nuts are the dried shells of the Sapindus mukorossi fruit. They contain saponin, a natural surfactant that creates a gentle lather in water. They've been used as a plant-based cleanser in South Asia for centuries and are still used today in laundry and body care applications.
