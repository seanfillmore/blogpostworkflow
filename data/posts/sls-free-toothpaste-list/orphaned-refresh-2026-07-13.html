What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is a surfactant — it makes toothpaste foam when you brush. That foam is cosmetic. It doesn't clean your teeth. Manufacturers use it because it's cheap and gives paste a familiar lathery feel. You'll find it in shampoos, body wash, and dish soap too.

In rinse-off products at low concentrations, regulators generally consider it safe. But some people do react to it. A well-cited 1994 study by Herlofson and Barkvoll (Acta Odontologica Scandinavica) found SLS exposure was linked to more frequent canker sores, and that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste reduced their occurrence in some patients.1 One working theory: SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer inside your mouth, leaving tissue more exposed to irritation.

Two relatives also appear in formulas marketed as "natural":

  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. Still a sulfate surfactant.
  • Sodium coco sulfate (SCS): Often marketed as coconut-derived. Technically true — but it's still a sulfate and can still irritate sensitive mouths. Plant-derived doesn't automatically mean gentle.

Who Should Switch to a Sodium Lauryl Sulfate-Free Toothpaste?

Not everyone needs to. But three groups tend to see the most benefit from switching to a lauryl sulfate-free toothpaste:

  • People with recurring canker sores. If you get mouth ulcers more than once or twice a year, removing SLS is a low-effort, low-risk thing to try.
  • People with sensitive gums. If brushing leaves your mouth feeling raw, a sulfate-free formula may help. Many people don't connect the discomfort to their toothpaste until they switch.
  • People cleaning up their personal care routine. If you're already reading labels on your shampoo and lotion, toothpaste is the logical next step.

One honest note: canker sores have multiple causes. If you have ongoing mouth problems, talk to your dentist. But switching toothpaste is low-risk and worth trying.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label

"SLS-free" on the front of a box means sodium lauryl sulfate doesn't appear on the ingredient list. But a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate can still contain other things worth avoiding. Here's a quick checklist:

  • Check for SLS and its relatives: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium laureth sulfate," and "sodium coco sulfate."
  • Read the full ingredient list — not just the front label: "Natural" on the front means nothing legally. The back panel is what counts.
  • Watch for artificial sweeteners, dyes, and parabens: A formula can skip SLS and still include saccharin, synthetic dyes, or parabens. Skipping one bad ingredient doesn't clean up the rest of the list.
  • Shorter is usually better: A short, recognizable ingredient list is a good sign.

What Does a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Toothpaste Look Like?

Avoiding SLS is the starting point, not the finish line. Here's what a clean natural toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate actually includes:

  • No sulfate surfactants at all — no SLS, SLES, or SCS. Some formulas use cocamidopropyl betaine (a milder, coconut-derived foaming agent) instead. Others skip surfactants entirely.
  • No artificial dyes or anonymous "flavor" — a toothpaste flavored with real peppermint oil is very different from one that lists "flavor" with no further detail.
  • No parabens or triclosan.
  • Functional natural ingredients — baking soda for gentle abrasion, coconut oil for antimicrobial support (Peedikayil et al., Nigerian Medical Journal, 2015),2 and wildcrafted myrrh for its traditional role in gum health.
  • A transparent ingredient list — you should be able to look up every ingredient on it.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms mean different things. SLS-free means no sulfate-based foaming agent. Fluoride-free means no fluoride — the mineral conventional dentistry recommends for enamel strengthening and cavity prevention. A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both, like our formula. Whether you want fluoride is a personal decision. If you're unsure, your dentist is the right person to ask. Frequently Asked Questions

What is an SLS-free toothpaste?

An SLS-free toothpaste is one that doesn't contain sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic foaming agent in most conventional toothpastes. It still cleans your teeth; it just doesn't produce as much lather. Some formulas use milder alternative surfactants; others skip surfactants entirely.

What toothpaste doesn't have SLS?

Any toothpaste that lists no "sodium lauryl sulfate" in its ingredients is SLS-free. Natural and health-focused brands are most likely to make toothpastes without sodium lauryl sulfate. Always check the ingredient list — not just the front label — since "natural" claims aren't regulated. Our best toothpaste without SLS guide covers more options.

Is sodium coco sulfate the same as SLS?

No, but it's related. Sodium coco sulfate (SCS) is a sulfate surfactant derived from coconut oil. It's often marketed as a natural alternative to SLS, but it can still irritate sensitive mouths. If you're switching specifically because of sensitivity, check for SCS on the label too.

Does Arm & Hammer have SLS?

Some Arm & Hammer formulas are SLS-free — specifically their baking soda-based lines — but not all. Formulas change, so always check the current ingredient list on the packaging before buying.
