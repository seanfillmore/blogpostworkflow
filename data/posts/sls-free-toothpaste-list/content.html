What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — SLS — is a surfactant. It makes your toothpaste foam when you brush. That foam is cosmetic; it doesn't clean your teeth. Manufacturers use it because it's cheap, stable, and gives paste a familiar lathery feel.

You'll find SLS in shampoos, body washes, and dish soap too. In rinse-off products at low concentrations, regulatory bodies generally consider it safe. This isn't a post about a dangerous ingredient hiding in your bathroom.

That said, some people do react to it. A well-cited 1994 study by Herlofson and Barkvoll (Acta Odontologica Scandinavica) found that SLS exposure was linked to more frequent canker sores, and that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste reduced their occurrence in some patients.1 One working theory: SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer inside your mouth, leaving tissue more exposed to irritation.

Two close relatives also appear in some formulas marketed as "natural":

  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. Still a sulfate surfactant. Some sensitive people tolerate it; others don't.
  • Sodium coco sulfate (SCS): Often marketed as coconut-derived and natural. Technically true — but it's still a sulfate, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Plant-derived doesn't automatically mean gentle.

Who Should Switch to a Lauryl Sulfate-Free Toothpaste?

Not everyone needs to. But three groups tend to see the most benefit from switching to a sodium lauryl sulfate-free toothpaste:

  • People with recurring canker sores. If you get mouth ulcers more than once or twice a year, removing SLS is a low-effort, low-risk thing to try. The Herlofson and Barkvoll research is one of the more consistent data points in this area.
  • People with sensitive gums. If brushing leaves your mouth feeling raw or irritated, a sulfate-free formula may help. Many people don't connect the discomfort to their toothpaste until they switch.
  • People cleaning up their personal care routine. If you're already reading labels on your shampoo and lotion, your toothpaste is a logical next step. The same label discipline applies.

One honest note: canker sores have multiple causes. If you have ongoing mouth problems, talk to your dentist. But switching toothpaste is low-risk and worth trying.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label

"SLS-free" on the front of a box means sodium lauryl sulfate doesn't appear on the ingredient list. But a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate can still contain other things worth avoiding. Here's a quick checklist:

  • Check for SLS and its relatives: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium laureth sulfate," and "sodium coco sulfate."
  • Read the full ingredient list — not just the front label: "Natural" on the front means nothing legally. The ingredient list on the back is what counts.
  • Watch for artificial sweeteners, dyes, parabens, and triclosan: A formula can skip SLS and still include saccharin, synthetic dyes, or parabens. Skipping one ingredient doesn't clean up the rest of the list.
  • Shorter is usually better: A recognizable, shorter ingredient list is a signal of a simpler formula. If you can't find information on an ingredient, that's worth noting.

Are Natural SLS-Free Toothpastes Easy to Find?

More so than they used to be — but the quality gap is real. You'll find affordable SLS-free toothpaste online from brands ranging from large natural retailers to small direct-to-consumer makers. Most health food stores carry at least a few options.

The harder part isn't finding a product labeled SLS-free — it's finding one that's genuinely clean across the whole ingredient list. Many "natural" toothpastes skip SLS but still use sodium coco sulfate, artificial sweeteners, or anonymous "flavor" ingredients. Reading the full label, not just the front, is how you tell the difference.

What a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Formula Looks Like

Avoiding SLS is the starting point, not the finish line. Here's what a clean formula actually has:

  • No sulfate surfactants at all — no SLS, SLES, or SCS. Some formulas use cocamidopropyl betaine (a milder, coconut-derived foaming agent) instead. Others skip surfactants entirely and rely on mild abrasives.
  • No artificial dyes or anonymous "flavor" — a toothpaste flavored with real peppermint oil is different from one that lists "flavor" with no further detail.
  • No parabens or triclosan — if you see either, it's worth questioning.
  • Functional natural ingredients — things that are actually doing something. Baking soda provides gentle abrasion for surface stains. Organic coconut oil has been studied for antimicrobial properties in oral care (Peedikayil et al., Nigerian Medical Journal, 2015).2 Wildcrafted myrrh has a long history in traditional oral care for its astringent, soothing properties.
  • A transparent ingredient list — you should be able to look up every ingredient on it.

Also worth asking about: glycerin. Some natural toothpaste advocates prefer glycerin-free formulas too. Our post on why glycerin-free toothpaste matters explains the reasoning.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms mean different things. SLS-free means no sulfate-based foaming agent. Fluoride-free means no fluoride — the mineral conventional dentistry recommends for enamel strengthening and cavity prevention.

A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both, like our formula. Whether you want fluoride is a personal decision based on your oral health history and values. Frequently Asked Questions

What is an SLS-free toothpaste?

An SLS-free toothpaste is one that doesn't contain sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic foaming agent in most conventional toothpastes. It still cleans your teeth; it just doesn't produce as much lather. Some SLS-free formulas use milder alternative surfactants; others skip surfactants entirely.

Is sodium coco sulfate the same as SLS?

No, but it's related. Sodium coco sulfate (SCS) is a sulfate surfactant derived from coconut oil. It's often marketed as a natural alternative to SLS, but it can still irritate sensitive mouths. If you're switching specifically because of sensitivity, it's worth checking for SCS on the label too.

Does Arm & Hammer make an SLS-free toothpaste?

Some Arm & Hammer formulas are SLS-free — specifically their baking soda-based lines — but formulas change, so always check the current ingredient list on the packaging before buying.
