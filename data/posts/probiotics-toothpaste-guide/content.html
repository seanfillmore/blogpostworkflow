What Is the Oral Microbiome (and Why Does It Matter)?

The oral microbiome is the community of bacteria, fungi, and other tiny organisms that lives in your mouth. It includes hundreds of species. The Human Oral Microbiome Database catalogs roughly 700 of them.

Most of these microbes aren't a problem. Many help keep things in balance by taking up space and food that harmful bacteria would otherwise use.

Trouble tends to start when the balance tips. Sugar-loving bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans make acid as they feed. According to the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, that acid is part of how tooth decay starts. Other bacteria that grow in low-oxygen spots, like the back of your tongue and under the gumline, can add to gum irritation and bad breath.

So the goal isn't a sterile mouth. It's a balanced one. That's the thinking behind probiotic toothpaste, and it's also why harsh, heavy-handed oral care can backfire for some people.

What Is Probiotic Toothpaste?

Probiotic toothpaste contains beneficial bacteria, most often Lactobacillus strains such as Lactobacillus reuteri or Lactobacillus paracasei. The goal is to add friendly microbes to your mouth rather than just scrub or kill what's there.

Conventional antibacterial toothpastes work differently. They aim to reduce bacteria broadly, and that can include the helpful kinds.

Many probiotic formulas are also made without SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate), the foaming agent in a lot of regular toothpaste. Some are fluoride-free too. If you're curious about SLS, our guide to the best toothpaste without SLS breaks it down.

Probiotic vs. Prebiotic Toothpaste: What's the Difference?

Probiotic toothpaste adds live beneficial bacteria. Prebiotic toothpaste aims to feed the good bacteria already in your mouth, using ingredients that help those microbes thrive. Some products combine both.

Here's a simple way to think about it:

  • Probiotic: Brings in new friendly bacteria. It's often discussed for people whose mouth balance may have been disrupted, such as after a course of antibiotics.
  • Prebiotic: Supports the bacteria you already have. It's more of a maintenance approach.
  • Neither: A gentle toothpaste that simply cleans teeth without harsh additives. This is where our formula sits.

Researchers are still working out which approach helps whom. Anyone who tells you one is clearly better is getting ahead of the evidence.

If you're deciding, think about your goal. Do you want to add something new, or do you want to take irritating things away? Often the second step is the easier first one.

What Does the Research Say About Probiotic Toothpaste?

The research is promising but still early. Most studies are small, use different strains, and test different products like lozenges, tablets, and gums. Few test toothpaste directly. Here's a general picture of where things stand.

Gum health

Some small trials suggest certain Lactobacillus strains may help reduce gum inflammation and bleeding. One example is a randomized trial by Iniesta and colleagues (2012) on L. reuteri tablets in people with gingivitis (see Sources). Probiotic toothpaste isn't a treatment for gum disease. It may play a supporting role. source

Bad breath

Bad breath often comes from bacteria that break down proteins and release smelly gases. The idea is that friendly bacteria may compete with them. Streptococcus salivarius K12 is one probiotic strain that people often talk about when it comes to fresher breath. Results like this are encouraging, but they don't prove a toothpaste will work for you.

Cavities

Balance may matter here too, since acid-making bacteria drive decay. But the evidence for probiotic toothpaste and cavities is the thinnest of the three. Brushing, flossing, limiting sugar, and regular dental visits are still the foundation.

A gentler formula

Many probiotic toothpastes skip SLS. Small studies have linked SLS to more canker sores in some people, though the evidence is mixed (see Shim et al. in Sources). If your mouth gets sore easily, an SLS-free toothpaste is a low-risk thing to try. source

Who Might Like Probiotic Toothpaste?

Probiotic toothpaste may appeal to you if you fit one of these profiles. None of them are a guarantee, just good reasons to be curious.

  • You deal with recurring bad breath: A balanced mouth may help, though bad breath can also come from other causes worth checking.
  • Your gums feel sensitive: A softer formula and a soft-bristle brush are a gentle place to start.
  • You recently finished antibiotics: Some people wonder about rebuilding their oral balance afterward. Ask your dentist or doctor.
  • You want to move away from harsh surfactants: Surfactants are the foaming agents in many toothpastes. SLS-free formulas skip the most common one.
  • You read labels on your skincare: If you hold your lotion to a clean-ingredient standard, it makes sense to do the same for what goes in your mouth. Our guide to ingredients to avoid in natural toothpaste is a good starting point.

One note. If you have active gum disease or a complex dental condition, talk to your dentist before changing your routine. Toothpaste is not a substitute for professional care.

What to Look for in a Natural Toothpaste (Probiotic or Not)

Whether a toothpaste has added probiotics or not, the same label-reading habits help. Here's a checklist you can use in the store or online.

  • Honest probiotic claims: If a label says "probiotic," it should name the actual strain, not just say "probiotic complex." Vague wording is a red flag.
  • No SLS: Sodium lauryl sulfate makes toothpaste foam, but it can bother sensitive mouths. Look for it on the label. Browse our natural toothpaste for SLS-free options.

  • A fluoride decision that's yours: The American Dental Association recommends fluoride toothpaste. Some people choose fluoride-free anyway. If you do, talk with your dentist about what's right for you, especially for kids.

How to Fit It Into Your Routine

Use any natural toothpaste the way you'd use a regular one. The American Dental Association recommends brushing twice a day for two minutes.

  • Use a soft-bristle brush: It's kinder to your gums and just as effective.
  • Brush gently: Light pressure cleans well without wearing on your gums.
  • Try oil pulling if you like it: Swishing coconut oil is a popular habit. It's a nice complement, not a replacement for brushing. Our coconut oil toothpaste builds on the same ingredient.
  • Go easy on strong mouthwash right after: If you're using a probiotic product, a harsh antibacterial rinse may work against it.
  • Keep your dental visits: Cleanings and checkups matter no matter what's in your toothpaste.

And if you're switching from a heavily foaming toothpaste, give it a few days. Less foam doesn't mean less clean. It's mostly a feel you get used to.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does probiotic toothpaste actually work?

Early research is encouraging, especially for gum health and bad breath. But most studies are small, and few test toothpaste specifically. Clinical evidence is still growing, so it's best to see probiotic toothpaste as a promising option, not a proven fix. source

Is probiotic toothpaste safe to use every day?

For most people, yes. It's made for daily use like regular toothpaste. If you have a weakened immune system or a health condition, ask your doctor or dentist first.

Can kids use probiotic toothpaste?

Some formulas are made for older children. Check the label and ask a pediatric dentist, especially for young kids who may swallow toothpaste. Our guide to kids' toothpaste without fluoride has more on what parents should know.

Is probiotic toothpaste the same as fluoride-free toothpaste?

No. A probiotic toothpaste can contain fluoride or not. Many natural versions leave it out. For a full look at that choice, read our guide to fluoride-free toothpaste.

What's the difference between a dental probiotic and probiotic toothpaste?

Dental probiotics are usually lozenges or tablets that dissolve slowly in your mouth. Toothpaste delivers its ingredients during a two-minute brushing. They're different formats, and research on each is still developing.
