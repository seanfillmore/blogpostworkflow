What Does "Organic Toothpaste" Actually Mean?

The word "organic" on a toothpaste label can mean almost anything. There are three distinct tiers, and knowing the difference is the most useful thing you can do before you buy.

  • USDA Certified Organic: At least 95% of ingredients are certified organic. This is the gold standard — you'll see the official green-and-white USDA seal on the package. source
  • "Made with organic ingredients": At least 70% of the formula is organic. Brands can say this on the label but cannot display the full USDA seal. Sounds more impressive than it is. source
  • "Natural" or "with organic extracts": Completely unregulated. No certification required, no minimum percentage, no oversight. Any brand can use this language — and many do.

Why is reaching 100% so hard? Water, minerals like baking soda, and binders like xanthan gum aren't grown on a farm. They can't be certified organic. So even a truly all natural organic toothpaste is unlikely to hit 100%. What matters is that every ingredient that can be organic is organic. source

When you're evaluating certified organic toothpaste brands, check for a certified seal, a short recognizable ingredient list, and no synthetic surfactants or petroleum-derived thickeners. Those three checks tell you more than any front-label claim ever will.

Organic Toothpaste Ingredients to Look For

Here's what's inside a well-made formula — and why each ingredient earns its place.

Organic Virgin Coconut Oil

Coconut oil is naturally antibacterial, soothing for gum tissue, and an effective base for other actives. Baking Soda

Baking soda is the gentle polishing agent. It scores low on the Relative Dentin Abrasivity (RDA) scale. That's a number that shows how harsh a toothpaste is on teeth. The lower the number, the gentler the paste. Baking soda removes surface stains without scratching enamel. It also helps neutralize acidity in your mouth, which is a real plus for daily use.

Wildcrafted Myrrh Powder

Myrrh has been used for centuries to support gum health. In lab studies, it has antimicrobial properties, which means it helps fight germs. It's the ingredient that most sets a quality formula apart from generic baking soda paste — and it's why "organic herbal toothpaste" is more than a marketing phrase here.

Stevia, Organic Essential Oils, and Functional Binders

Stevia — a leaf extract — replaces saccharin or artificial sweeteners. Organic peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, and clove essential oils deliver flavor and additional germ-fighting support. Xanthan gum (a plant-derived thickener) and purified spring water round out the formula. Every ingredient is functional. Nothing is there for show.

Organic Toothpaste Ingredients to Avoid

These are the ingredients that disqualify a toothpaste from being genuinely organic — no matter what the front label says. Our full breakdown is in 7 Ingredients to Avoid in Natural Toothpaste.

  • Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS): A synthetic foaming agent with no cleaning function — it just makes paste feel sudsy. Linked to increased mouth sores in some people. See our guide on Best Toothpaste Without SLS for what to look for instead.
  • Artificial dyes and synthetic flavors: These serve marketing purposes, not oral health ones. A genuinely organic formula has no need for Red 40 or a vague "flavor" listing.
  • Saccharin and artificial sweeteners: Synthetic compounds added purely for taste. Stevia achieves the same goal without the controversy.
  • Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) and petroleum-derived compounds: Used as humectants (ingredients that hold moisture in the paste) in conventional formulas. Derived from petroleum — no place in an organic product.
  • Carrageenan: Derived from seaweed, so it sounds natural — but considered controversial by some researchers due to potential effects on soft tissue. Worth watching for.
  • Glycerin: Common in conventional pastes, but some oral health advocates argue it leaves a coating on teeth that may interfere with natural remineralization. That's how teeth rebuild minerals on their own. More in Why Glycerin-Free Toothpaste Matters.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free?

Fluoride is strongly supported by mainstream dentistry for cavity prevention. If your dentist has flagged you as high cavity risk, talk with them before switching to a fluoride-free formula.

That said, many people choosing organic toothpaste prefer to avoid fluoride for legitimate reasons. Fluorosis — white spots on teeth — can happen when young children get too much fluoride while their teeth are still developing. Others simply want a whole-ingredient formula. These are reasonable preferences, not fringe concerns.

Fluoride-free organic formulas offer real alternatives. Baking soda makes your mouth less acidic. Coconut oil targets certain oral bacteria. Myrrh supports gum tissue.

Hydroxyapatite also appears in some fluoride-free formulas. It's a mineral naturally found in teeth and bones, and it helps rebuild tooth minerals.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between "natural" and "organic" toothpaste?

"Natural" is unregulated marketing language — any brand can use it. "Organic" should come with a certified seal (ideally USDA) and a minimum percentage of certified organic ingredients. If there's no seal, treat the claim skeptically.

Is organic toothpaste as effective as conventional toothpaste?

For daily cleaning and freshening breath, yes. For cavity prevention, the answer depends on whether you choose a fluoride or fluoride-free formula. A clean organic formula with baking soda and botanical actives cleans teeth effectively — it just does it without synthetic additives.

What are the best natural toothpaste ingredients to look for in 2026?

Baking soda, organic coconut oil, myrrh, neem, or hydroxyapatite — depending on your goals. Natural sweeteners like stevia. Organic essential oils for flavor. And no SLS, no PEG compounds, no artificial dyes. Short list = cleaner formula.

Which natural toothpaste has the best antimicrobial properties?

Look for formulas that combine coconut oil, myrrh, and clove or cinnamon essential oils. Each of these has documented antimicrobial activity (germ-fighting power) in lab studies. Together, they make a strong case for a genuinely functional natural organic toothpaste — not just a clean one by omission.

Can I use organic toothpaste if I have sensitive teeth?

Yes — often organic formulas are better for sensitive teeth because they skip SLS and high-abrasion synthetic polishing agents. Look for baking soda as the abrasive and avoid anything with strong synthetic astringents or foaming agents.

Are mainstream "clean" toothpastes truly organic?

Usually not. Many mainstream lines still contain SLS, artificial flavors, or other synthetic additives. Always read the full ingredient list on the back, not just the front label.
