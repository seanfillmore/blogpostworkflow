Pine tar soap has been used medicinally for over a century, and it remains one of the most searched natural bar soap options today — with searches peaking every spring as seasonal skin changes send people looking for something that actually helps. It earned that reputation for good reason. But before you buy, it helps to understand exactly what it is, what the research says, and whether it's the right fit for your skin.

Pine tar soap is a bar soap made by blending pine tar — a dark, thick substance produced from the carbonization of pine wood — with a carrier oil base. It has documented anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties and has long been used to help manage dry, itchy skin conditions like psoriasis, eczema, and dandruff. It is a naturally derived ingredient, not a synthetic one.

What Is Pine Tar Soap?

Pine tar itself is made through a process called destructive distillation — basically, heating pine wood (most often Pinus sylvestris) in a low-oxygen environment until it breaks down into a dark, viscous tar. That process has been used for thousands of years, and pine tar was a recognized medical ingredient long before it became a soap component.

Pine tar soap is made by combining pine tar with a soap base — typically coconut oil, olive oil, tallow, or a blend — using cold-process or hot-process soap-making methods. The pine tar gives the bar its characteristic dark brown color and smoky, earthy scent. There's no artificial color here. That's just what pine tar looks and smells like.

One important clarification: pine tar and coal tar are not the same thing. Coal tar is a petroleum byproduct with a different chemical profile and heavier regulatory scrutiny. Pine tar comes from wood, not fossil fuel. The distinction matters — pine tar is generally considered the milder, more natural of the two. The Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology has noted coal tar's more complex regulatory history compared to pine tar's long-standing OTC acceptance.

Pine Tar Soap Benefits for Skin

This is where the science gets interesting. Pine tar's benefits aren't just folk medicine — there's real dermatological backing behind several of them.

  • Anti-inflammatory properties: Pine tar contains phenolic compounds that may reduce redness, flaking, and irritation associated with psoriasis and eczema. The FDA has formally recognized pine tar as an approved active ingredient for psoriasis relief under 21 CFR § 358.710, which lists it as an OTC monograph ingredient for skin-scale conditions at concentrations between 0.5% and 5%.
  • Antimicrobial and antifungal activity: The phenols in pine tar have shown activity against common skin pathogens in laboratory studies. A review published on PubMed notes pine tar's phenolic content as a key driver of its antimicrobial action. This makes it useful for acne-prone or fungal-prone skin.
  • Scalp and dandruff treatment: Pine tar slows the overproduction of skin cells — the same mechanism behind its psoriasis benefit. On the scalp, that translates to reduced flaking and irritation associated with seborrheic dermatitis.
  • Deep cleansing and deodorizing: Pine tar has a natural astringency that helps remove excess oil and odor-causing bacteria from the skin's surface. It's a legitimate reason people reach for it as a body bar or shaving soap.
  • Base oil nourishment: Well-formulated pine tar soaps include carrier oils like coconut or olive oil that help offset any drying effect from the tar itself. The base matters as much as the active.

Pine Tar Soap Ingredients — What to Look For

Reading a pine tar soap label is straightforward once you know what you're looking for. The pine tar itself should be listed as an active ingredient — often as "pine tar" or "Pinus sylvestris tar" — and should make up roughly 2–8% of the formula for any therapeutic effect.

Who Should (and Shouldn't) Use Pine Tar Soap

Pine tar soap is a good fit for people managing psoriasis, eczema, seborrheic dermatitis, dandruff, or acne-prone skin who want a traditional, OTC-recognized remedy. It's versatile too — it works as a body bar, shampoo bar, or shaving bar depending on the formula.

Pine tar soap is also strongly associated with men's grooming, particularly for its deodorizing and deep-cleansing properties. If that's your angle, our post on the best natural bar soap for men is worth a read alongside this one.

That said, pine tar isn't right for everyone. A few groups should proceed carefully:

  • Very sensitive skin: The active phenolic compounds can be irritating if your skin is already reactive. Patch test first.
  • Pregnant women: There's limited safety data on topical pine tar during pregnancy. Check with your doctor before using it.
  • Tree resin sensitivities: If you have known reactions to pine or tree resins, pine tar is likely not a safe match.

And then there's the scent. Pine tar has a strong, smoky, medicinal smell that's genuinely polarizing — some people love it, others can't get past it. If you want the benefits of a natural, handcrafted bar soap without that intensity, a coconut oil-based or olive oil-based option may be a better daily fit. Pine Tar Soap vs. Other Natural Bar Soaps

Pine tar soap has a specific job. It's a targeted remedy, not a general daily cleanser. Here's how it fits relative to other natural bar soap categories:

  • Pine tar vs. castile soap: Castile soap — made from olive or other vegetable oils — is milder, unscented, and better suited for everyday gentle cleansing. Pine tar is more targeted for active skin conditions.
  • Pine tar vs. goat milk soap: Goat milk soap is deeply moisturizing and very gentle. It's a better fit for dry or sensitive skin that doesn't need a medicinal-strength active.
  • Pine tar vs. turmeric soap: Both have anti-inflammatory properties, but turmeric soap has a lighter scent profile and some brightening benefits. Less medicinal intensity overall.
  • Pine tar vs. coconut oil soap: Coconut oil soap is naturally antibacterial, widely tolerated, and works well as an everyday bar for most skin types — without the strong scent or therapeutic intensity of pine tar.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pine Tar Soap

Is pine tar soap good for eczema?
Pine tar soap may help reduce eczema-related itching and flaking. Its phenolic compounds have documented anti-inflammatory activity, and the FDA recognizes pine tar as an OTC active ingredient for related skin-scale conditions. Results vary by person and severity. It's not a cure, but many people with mild-to-moderate eczema find it helpful.

Can you use pine tar soap on your face?
Yes, with caution. Start with once or twice a week to see how your skin responds. Avoid the eye area. Monitor for dryness or irritation — the active compounds that help with skin conditions can also be drying if used too frequently on facial skin, which tends to be more sensitive than body skin.

Does pine tar soap help with psoriasis?
Yes. Pine tar is an FDA-recognized active ingredient for psoriasis under 21 CFR § 358.710. It works by slowing the abnormally fast skin cell turnover associated with psoriasis plaques. At concentrations of 0.5–5%, it qualifies as an OTC psoriasis treatment under federal monograph guidelines.

How often should you use pine tar soap?
For managing a skin condition, two to three times per week is a reasonable starting point. Daily use may be drying for some skin types, particularly on the face. Use your skin's response as a guide — if you notice increased dryness or irritation, scale back frequency.

Is pine tar soap the same as coal tar soap?
No. Pine tar comes from the carbonization of pine wood. Coal tar is a byproduct of coal processing — a petroleum derivative with a more complex chemical profile and heavier regulatory scrutiny. Pine tar is generally considered the milder, more naturally derived option. They're both used in dermatology, but they're chemically distinct.
