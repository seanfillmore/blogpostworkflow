What Is Handcrafted Soap, Really?

Handcrafted soap (also called handmade soap, artisan soap, or homemade soap) is made by combining oils or fats with sodium hydroxide (lye) in a process called saponification. Saponification is simply the reaction that turns oil into soap. The lye reacts completely with the oils — and the result is soap plus glycerin. Crucially, quality handcrafted makers leave that glycerin in the bar. Glycerin is a natural humectant, meaning it draws moisture to your skin and keeps it there.

Commercial manufacturers often extract glycerin from their soap and sell it separately into the cosmetics industry. What's left behind is a harsher bar that can strip your skin. That's a big part of why mass-market soap can leave your face feeling tight or your hands feeling dry.

The terms homemade soap, handmade soap bars, and natural handmade bar soap are largely interchangeable with handcrafted soap. What unites them: small-batch production, whole-ingredient formulas, and a process done by hand rather than industrial machine. The difference in skin feel is real and immediate.

How Handcrafted Soap Is Made — Cold Process vs. Hot Process

Most artisan soap makers use one of two methods, and both produce genuinely excellent bars.

Cold process is the most common. Oils and lye are blended at or near room temperature, poured into molds, and left to cure for 4–6 weeks. That curing time is important — it hardens the bar, evaporates excess water, and ensures saponification is fully complete. The result is a smooth, glycerin-rich bar that retains the skin-nourishing properties of the base oils.

Hot process uses the same ingredients but applies heat — typically in a slow cooker or oven — to speed saponification. Cure time is shorter, and the final bar tends to have a more rustic, textured look. It's a little less smooth than cold process but just as effective.

A third method, milled soap (or rebatched soap), starts with an existing base that's melted down and reformulated. It's common in commercial settings and not really considered true handcrafted soap-making.

One question comes up constantly: Is there lye in handcrafted soap? No. Lye is a processing agent. It is fully consumed during saponification. No lye remains in a properly made, fully cured bar. This is basic chemistry — and any knowledgeable soap maker can confirm it.

Key Ingredients That Define a High-Quality Handcrafted Soap

The base oil determines most of what a bar does for your skin. Here's what each common option contributes:

  • Coconut oil: Creates a hard bar with a rich, fluffy lather and strong cleansing power. It's the workhorse of most quality formulas. Real Skin Care's coconut soap uses it as the primary base — delivering deep cleaning without leaving skin stripped.
  • Olive oil (castile): The base of castile soap — a type of handcrafted soap made predominantly with olive oil. It's mild, moisturizing, and ideal for sensitive skin. Olive-dominant bars lather less dramatically than coconut bars but are exceptionally gentle.
  • Shea butter: Rich and conditioning. Adds a creamy feel and helps lock moisture in after washing.
  • Tallow: Traditional animal fat, highly skin-compatible, rich in vitamins A, D, and K. Worth noting: it's not vegan, so it's not for everyone.

Beyond the base, popular additives change what a bar does:

  • Goat milk: Contains lactic acid, which gently exfoliates and adds creaminess. Popular for dry or irritated skin.
  • Turmeric: An antioxidant-rich spice with brightening properties. A turmeric soap bar is a common choice for anyone targeting uneven skin tone.
  • Essential oils: Provide real fragrance from plant sources — not synthetic chemical copies. Lavender, peppermint, citrus, cedarwood — all plant-derived and functional.
  • Synthetic fragrance: The thing to avoid. "Fragrance" on a label can legally represent dozens of unlisted chemicals, some of which are known skin irritants.

What a quality bar should never include: sulfates, parabens, artificial dyes, or synthetic preservatives. If you see those on a label, you're holding a commercial detergent bar — not a handcrafted natural soap.

Handcrafted Soap vs. Commercial Soap — What You're Actually Comparing

Here's something most people don't know: many popular drugstore "soap" bars are not legally classified as soap. The FDA has a specific definition — soap must be made primarily from saponified oils. Many mass-market bars don't qualify. They're formulated with synthetic surfactants and marketed as "beauty bars" or "cleansing bars" instead. That's not a technicality. Frequently Asked Questions About Handcrafted Soap

Is handcrafted soap better for your skin than store-bought?

For most people, yes — and there are specific reasons why. Handcrafted soap retains glycerin, a natural byproduct of saponification that moisturizes skin as you wash. Commercial bars are often stripped of glycerin and made with synthetic surfactants that can disrupt your skin's natural moisture barrier. If your skin feels tight or dry after washing, switching to a genuine handmade natural soap bar often makes a noticeable difference.

Does handcrafted soap last as long as commercial bars?

A properly cured handcrafted bar lasts just as long — sometimes longer — than a commercial bar when stored correctly. The key is keeping it dry between uses. A draining soap dish that lets water run away from the bar will extend its life significantly. Leaving a bar sitting in pooled water softens it quickly and shortens its lifespan, regardless of how it was made.

What is the difference between handcrafted soap and castile soap?

Castile soap is a specific type of handcrafted soap made predominantly with olive oil. Not all handcrafted soaps are castile. A coconut oil soap, a goat milk soap, and a tallow soap are all handcrafted — but none of them are castile. The term castile comes from the Castile region of Spain, where olive-oil soap-making has centuries of history. If you see "organic castile soap" on a label, it should mean the base is primarily olive oil.

Is handcrafted soap safe for sensitive skin?

Generally yes — especially unscented bars or goat milk formulas with short ingredient lists. The absence of synthetic fragrances and sulfates makes most well-made handcrafted bars significantly gentler than commercial alternatives. That said, always check the ingredient list. Even natural essential oils can be irritating for some people. If your skin is highly reactive, start with a fragrance-free option and patch test first.
