What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)?

SLS is a synthetic detergent. Its job is to create foam and lift particles off surfaces. Think of it like dish soap — effective at cutting through grease, but harsh on delicate surfaces. Your mouth's soft tissue lining is about as delicate as tissue gets.

SLS shows up in shampoos, body wash, face wash, and most conventional toothpastes. It's derived from petroleum or coconut/palm oil, but either way it goes through significant chemical processing before it ends up on a label. One important thing to know: foaming is not the same as cleaning. The bubbles feel like deep-cleaning action, but that's mostly a sensory illusion. Actual cleaning comes from mechanical brushing and ingredients like baking soda or calcium carbonate.

Why Is SLS Added to Toothpaste?

From a manufacturer's perspective, SLS is cheap and familiar. It makes toothpaste foam, and that foam creates a sensation people associate with a thorough clean. It also helps spread the paste around your mouth quickly.

But studies haven't shown that SLS meaningfully improves plaque removal compared to SLS-free formulas. The foam is doing a job — but that job is mostly about how the product feels, not how well it cleans. If you switch to SLS-free toothpaste and notice less foam, that's not a sign it's working less. It's just more honest about what's happening.

Is SLS Safe? What the Research Actually Says

Regulatory bodies including the FDA and the EU consider SLS safe at concentrations used in rinse-off products like toothpaste. This isn't a case of something secretly toxic in your bathroom cabinet. That said, research does link SLS to a few specific issues worth knowing:

Canker sores: This is the most-studied concern. Multiple clinical trials — including research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine — found that people who use SLS toothpaste get more frequent and more severe canker sores than those using SLS-free toothpaste. The pattern across studies is consistent enough that many dentists now recommend SLS-free toothpaste for canker sore-prone patients.

The orange juice effect: Ever brushed your teeth and had orange juice taste terrible afterward? SLS degrades phospholipids that normally suppress bitter taste receptors on your tongue. For an hour or two after brushing, sweet things taste bitter and sour. SLS-free toothpaste largely eliminates this.

Gum irritation and dry mouth: SLS can disrupt the oral mucosal barrier — the protective lining of your mouth. For sensitive people, this causes irritation, a raw feeling, or worsened dry mouth (xerostomia). If you already struggle with saliva production, SLS is an added irritant you don't need.

SLS vs. SLES vs. Cleaner Surfactant Alternatives

Most guides stop at "avoid SLS." But reading labels well means knowing the full picture:

  • SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate): The harsher of the two common sulfates. Its small molecule size means it penetrates soft tissue more easily — which is largely why it's linked to irritation and canker sores.
  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): Chemically modified to be slightly milder. But the ethoxylation process can leave trace amounts of 1,4-dioxane, a potential contaminant flagged by consumer safety groups. Milder than SLS, but not a clean ingredient.
  • Ammonium lauryl sulfate: Another sulfate in the same family — worth avoiding for the same reasons.
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate: Naturally derived from coconut oil and fermented sugars. Gentle, biodegradable, and well-tolerated. A genuinely cleaner alternative if a formula needs a surfactant.
  • Decyl glucoside: Derived from coconut oil and glucose. Very mild and non-irritating. Common in gentle formulas for kids and sensitive users.

Who Should Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste?

SLS-free toothpaste is a smart choice for most people. For some, it's less of a preference and more of a real need:

  • Canker sore sufferers: The clinical evidence here is the strongest. If you get recurring mouth ulcers, cutting SLS is one of the most direct steps you can take. Many people notice fewer sores after switching.
  • People with sensitive gums: If your gums bleed easily or feel raw after brushing, SLS-free toothpaste reduces daily chemical irritation to that tissue.
  • Those with dry mouth: SLS makes dry mouth worse by disrupting the mucosal barrier. If saliva production is already low, you don't need an added irritant.
  • Kids and toddlers: Children's oral tissues are still developing and tend to be more sensitive. Many parents look specifically for kids' toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate — especially for toddlers who are still likely to swallow some paste.
  • People with sulfate sensitivity: If you've already switched to sulfate-free shampoo or body wash because SLS irritates your skin or scalp, the same logic applies in your mouth.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free When Going SLS-Free?

Many people searching for toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate are also thinking about skipping fluoride. These are two separate decisions — but they often come up together.

Fluoride has strong, long-established evidence behind cavity prevention. Most dentists recommend it. At the same time, some people prefer to avoid it — due to concerns about dental fluorosis in children, or simply a preference for fully natural products. You have real options either way:

  • Fluoride toothpaste without SLS: For those who want cavity protection but want to cut the harsh surfactant.
  • Toothpaste without fluoride and sodium lauryl sulfate: For a fully clean formula. Real Skin Care's fluoride-free toothpaste covers this space.

If your dentist has specifically recommended fluoride for cavity prevention or enamel repair, don't abandon it without talking to them first. But if you're a generally healthy adult with good dental habits, a clean fluoride-free formula can absolutely be part of a solid oral care routine.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label

Label reading gets easier once you know what you're looking for. Here's a quick reference for the next time you're in the toothpaste aisle.

Red flags — ingredients to avoid:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
  • Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES) — milder but may contain trace contaminants
  • Ammonium lauryl sulfate — same family as SLS
  • Artificial dyes — FD&C Blue No. 1, FD&C Red No. 40 — no benefit to teeth
  • Saccharin — synthetic sweetener; linked to digestive sensitivity in some people
  • Propylene glycol — synthetic humectant some sensitive users prefer to avoid

Green flags — ingredients that signal a cleaner formula:

  • Coconut oil (or organic virgin coconut oil) — non-foaming base with documented antimicrobial properties in laboratory studies
  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — gentle abrasive that polishes teeth and neutralizes acids
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, clove) — natural flavoring with antimicrobial activity in lab settings
  • Myrrh powder — a traditional botanical used for centuries in oral care, with emerging research on antimicrobial properties
  • Stevia — plant-derived sweetener with no known negative effects on teeth
  • Xanthan gum — plant-derived thickener; no concern here
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate / decyl glucoside — if a formula uses a surfactant, these are far gentler options than SLS

Frequently Asked Questions

Does toothpaste without SLS actually clean your teeth?

Yes. Cleaning happens through mechanical brushing and abrasive ingredients like baking soda and calcium carbonate — not foam. SLS adds lather, not cleaning power. Studies haven't found that SLS-containing toothpastes remove more plaque than SLS-free alternatives.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for canker sores?

The research says yes. Multiple clinical trials have found that people who switch to SLS-free toothpaste get fewer and less severe canker sores. It's one of the most well-supported reasons to make the switch.

What's the difference between SLS-free and sulfate-free toothpaste?

"Sulfate-free" means the formula contains no sulfate surfactants at all — including SLS, SLES, and ammonium lauryl sulfate. SLS-free specifically means no sodium lauryl sulfate, but may still contain SLES. Look for "sulfate-free" on the label if you want to avoid the whole category, or check the full ingredient list.

Can kids use SLS-free toothpaste?

Yes — it's actually a good fit. Children's oral tissues are more sensitive than adults', and toddlers are still likely to swallow some paste. A gentle, SLS-free formula with no artificial dyes or saccharin is a smart choice for the whole family. See our guide to the best natural toothpaste for families for specific picks.

Is fluoride-free toothpaste without SLS effective?

For generally healthy adults with good brushing habits, yes. Baking soda and hydroxyapatite are the most commonly studied fluoride alternatives, and emerging research on hydroxyapatite in particular is promising. If you have active cavities or your dentist has flagged enamel concerns, talk to them before dropping fluoride entirely.
