What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)?

Sodium lauryl sulfate is a synthetic detergent — the same basic chemistry as dish soap. Its job is to create foam and help lift particles off surfaces. In toothpaste, it spreads the paste around your mouth and creates that bubbly sensation most people associate with a thorough clean.

Here's the thing: foaming is not the same as cleaning. The bubbles feel like deep action, but they're mostly a sensory effect. Real cleaning comes from mechanical brushing and ingredients like baking soda or calcium carbonate. The foam is doing a job — it's just not the cleaning job you think it is.

SLS is derived from petroleum or coconut/palm oil, but regardless of source it goes through heavy chemical processing before it reaches your toothpaste tube.

Why Is SLS Added to Toothpaste?

It's cheap, it foams reliably, and consumers expect that sensation. That's mostly it. Studies haven't shown that SLS meaningfully improves plaque removal over SLS-free formulas. If you switch and notice less foam, that's not a sign the toothpaste is working less — it's just more honest about what's happening.

What Does the Research Say About SLS?

Regulatory bodies including the FDA and EU Cosmetics Regulation consider SLS safe at concentrations used in rinse-off products. This isn't a case of something secretly toxic in your cabinet. But research does connect SLS to a few specific problems worth knowing about.

Canker sores: This is the most-studied concern. Multiple clinical trials — including research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine — found that people using SLS toothpaste get canker sores more often and more severely than those using SLS-free toothpaste. The link isn't 100% proven as direct cause-and-effect, but the pattern is consistent enough that many dentists recommend SLS-free toothpaste for patients with recurring mouth ulcers.

The orange juice effect: Ever brushed your teeth and then had orange juice taste terrible? SLS degrades the phospholipids that suppress bitter taste receptors on your tongue. For an hour or two after brushing, sweet things taste sour. SLS-free toothpaste largely eliminates this.

Gum and tissue sensitivity: SLS can disrupt the protective lining of your mouth. For sensitive people, this shows up as a raw feeling, bleeding gums, or temporary dryness. Most people don't notice it — but for those who do, switching makes a real difference.

SLS vs. SLES vs. Gentler Alternatives

If you want to read labels intelligently, you need to know what else is out there beyond SLS.

  • SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate): The harsher of the two common sulfates. Its smaller molecule size means it penetrates soft tissue more easily — which is why it's most linked to irritation.
  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): Chemically modified to be milder than SLS. Still synthetic, and the manufacturing process can leave trace amounts of 1,4-dioxane, a contaminant flagged by consumer safety groups.
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate / decyl glucoside: Naturally derived from coconut oil. Gentle, biodegradable, and well-tolerated. If a formula does use a surfactant, these are the clean options.
  • Coconut oil (no surfactant): Some natural toothpastes skip surfactants entirely and use coconut oil as the base instead. No foam, but it carries ingredients across teeth and gums effectively. This is the approach Real Skin Care uses.

Who Should Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste?

SLS-free is a good choice for most people. For some, it's a genuine need:

  • Canker sore sufferers: The evidence here is the strongest. Cutting SLS is one of the most straightforward steps you can take if you get recurring mouth ulcers.
  • Sensitive gums: If your gums bleed easily or feel raw after brushing, removing daily SLS exposure reduces chemical irritation.
  • Dry mouth (xerostomia): SLS can worsen dry mouth by further disrupting the mouth's protective lining. If you already struggle with saliva production, you don't need an added irritant.
  • Kids and toddlers: Children's oral tissues are more sensitive, and toddlers still swallow some paste. A gentler, SLS-free formula is the smart call. See our guide to best natural toothpaste for families for more.
  • Sulfate-sensitive skin: Already use sulfate-free shampoo or body wash? The same logic applies in your mouth.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free When Going SLS-Free?

These are two separate decisions that often come up together. Fluoride has strong, long-established evidence for cavity prevention — most dentists recommend it. Some people prefer to avoid it due to concerns about dental fluorosis in children or a preference for fully natural products.

You have real options either way:

How to Read a Toothpaste Label

Label reading gets easier fast once you know what you're looking for.

Ingredients to avoid:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
  • Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES)
  • Ammonium lauryl sulfate
  • Artificial dyes (FD&C Blue No. 1, FD&C Red No. 40)
  • Saccharin (synthetic sweetener)
  • Propylene glycol

Ingredients that signal a cleaner formula:

  • Coconut oil — natural base with antimicrobial properties in laboratory studies
  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — gentle abrasive that polishes and neutralizes acids
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, clove) — natural flavoring with antimicrobial activity in lab settings
  • Stevia — plant-derived sweetener with no known negative effects on teeth
  • Myrrh powder — traditional botanical used in oral care for centuries, with emerging antimicrobial research
  • Xanthan gum — plant-derived thickener, no concern

Frequently Asked Questions

Does toothpaste without SLS actually clean your teeth?

Yes. Cleaning happens through brushing mechanics and ingredients like baking soda — not foam. SLS creates the sensation of cleaning without meaningfully adding to it. SLS-free toothpaste cleans just as well; it just doesn't perform for you while doing it.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for sensitive teeth?

It can be. SLS irritates the soft tissue lining of your mouth, which can make existing sensitivity feel worse. Removing it reduces that daily chemical load. For more on this, see our Best Toothpaste for Sensitive Teeth – 2025 guide.

What's the difference between SLS-free and sulfate-free toothpaste?

Sulfate-free means the formula avoids the whole sulfate family — SLS, SLES, and ammonium lauryl sulfate. SLS-free only guarantees one specific ingredient is out. When in doubt, look for "sulfate-free" or check the full ingredient list yourself.

Can kids use SLS-free toothpaste?

Yes — and it's often the better choice. Children's oral tissues are more sensitive than adults', and toddlers tend to swallow some paste. A clean, SLS-free formula with no artificial sweeteners or dyes is the gentler option for young mouths.

Does SLS-free toothpaste help with canker sores?

Research suggests it can. Multiple clinical trials have found that people using SLS-containing toothpaste experience canker sores more often and more severely. Many dentists now recommend switching to SLS-free as a first step for patients with recurring ulcers.
