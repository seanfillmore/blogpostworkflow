What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)?

SLS is a synthetic surfactant — a detergent. It lowers water's surface tension, which creates foam and helps lift debris from surfaces. Think of it like dish soap: great at cutting through grease, but rough on delicate surfaces. Your mouth's soft tissue lining is about as delicate as it gets.

SLS shows up in shampoos, body wash, face wash, and most conventional toothpastes. It's derived from petroleum or coconut/palm oil, but regardless of origin, it goes through significant chemical processing before it reaches your ingredient label.

One thing worth knowing right away: foam is not the same as clean. The bubbles feel thorough, but they're mostly a sensory illusion. Real cleaning happens through mechanical brushing and abrasive ingredients like baking soda or calcium carbonate.

Why Do Companies Add SLS to Toothpaste?

SLS is cheap, effective at creating foam, and familiar. That foam gives people the feeling of a deep clean. It also helps spread paste around your mouth quickly.

But studies haven't shown that SLS meaningfully improves plaque removal compared to SLS-free formulas. The foam is doing a job — but that job is about how the product feels, not how well it actually works. If you switch to SLS-free toothpaste and notice less foam, that's not a sign it's working less. It's just being more honest.

What Does Research Actually Say About SLS?

Regulatory bodies including the FDA consider SLS safe at concentrations used in rinse-off products like toothpaste. This isn't a hidden toxin. But research does connect it to a few specific problems worth knowing about.

Canker sores: This is the most studied concern. Multiple clinical trials — including research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine — have found that people using SLS-containing toothpaste get more frequent, more severe canker sores than those using SLS-free toothpaste. The link isn't proven as direct cause-and-effect, but the pattern is consistent enough that many dentists recommend SLS-free toothpaste for canker sore-prone patients.

The orange juice effect: SLS degrades phospholipids that suppress bitter taste receptors on your tongue. For an hour or two after brushing, sweet things taste sour or metallic. SLS-free toothpaste largely eliminates this.

Gum and tissue irritation: SLS can disrupt the protective lining of your mouth. For sensitive individuals, this causes a raw feeling, irritation, or temporary dryness after brushing. For most people, SLS causes no noticeable issues. But for those it affects, switching is a genuine improvement — not just a wellness trend.

SLS vs. SLES vs. Gentler Alternatives

If you're reading labels carefully, you need to know the full picture — not just "avoid SLS."

  • SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate): The harsher of the two common sulfates. Its smaller molecule penetrates soft tissue more easily, which is why it's linked to irritation and canker sores.
  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): Chemically modified to be slightly milder. But the ethoxylation process can leave trace amounts of 1,4-dioxane — a potential contaminant flagged by consumer safety groups. Milder than SLS, but still not a clean ingredient.
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate: Naturally derived from coconut oil and fermented sugars. Gentle, biodegradable, and well-tolerated by sensitive oral tissues.
  • Decyl glucoside: Derived from coconut oil and glucose. Very mild and non-irritating. Common in formulas for kids and sensitive users.
  • Coconut oil (no surfactant): Some natural toothpastes skip surfactants entirely, using coconut oil as the base instead. No foam, but it carries ingredients effectively across teeth and gums. This is the approach Real Skin Care uses.

Who Should Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste?

SLS-free toothpaste is a good choice for most people. For some, it's a genuine need.

  • Canker sore sufferers: The clinical evidence is the strongest here. Many people notice fewer ulcers after switching.
  • Sensitive gums: If your gums bleed easily or feel raw after brushing, removing SLS reduces daily chemical irritation.
  • Dry mouth (xerostomia): SLS can worsen dry mouth by disrupting the mucosal barrier. If you already struggle with saliva production, this matters.
  • Kids and toddlers: Children's oral tissues are still developing. For kids likely to swallow some paste, gentler formulas are the smart call. See our guide to best natural toothpaste for families.
  • People with sulfate sensitivities: If you've already switched to sulfate-free shampoo because SLS irritates your scalp, the same logic applies in your mouth.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free? What to Know

Many people searching for toothpaste without SLS are also thinking about going fluoride-free. These are separate decisions — but they come up together often.

Fluoride has strong, long-established evidence for cavity prevention. Most dentists recommend it. Some consumers prefer to avoid it due to concerns about dental fluorosis in children, or simply a preference for fully natural products.

You have real options either way. Fluoride toothpaste without SLS exists for those who want cavity protection without the harsh surfactant. Fluoride-free, SLS-free formulas exist for those going fully clean. If your dentist has specifically recommended fluoride for cavity prevention or enamel repair, talk to them before changing. How to Read a Toothpaste Label

Label reading gets easier once you know what you're looking for.

Avoid these:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
  • Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES)
  • Ammonium lauryl sulfate
  • Artificial dyes (FD&C Blue No. 1, FD&C Red No. 40)
  • Saccharin
  • Propylene glycol

Look for these:

  • Coconut oil — natural base with antimicrobial properties
  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — gentle abrasive that polishes teeth and neutralizes acids
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, clove) — natural flavor with antimicrobial activity in lab studies
  • Stevia — plant-derived sweetener, no known harm to teeth
  • Myrrh — traditional botanical with emerging research on antimicrobial properties
  • Xanthan gum — plant-derived thickener, no concern
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate or decyl glucoside — if a formula uses a surfactant, these are far gentler than SLS

Frequently Asked Questions

Does toothpaste without SLS actually clean your teeth?

Yes. Cleaning happens through brushing and abrasive ingredients like baking soda — not through foam. SLS-free toothpaste removes plaque just as well. The absence of foam is a cosmetic difference, not a functional one.

What's the difference between SLS-free and sulfate-free toothpaste?

"SLS-free" means no sodium lauryl sulfate specifically. "Sulfate-free" typically means no sulfate surfactants at all — including SLES and ammonium lauryl sulfate. A truly clean formula avoids all of them. Check the best toothpaste without SLS – 2025 for vetted picks.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for sensitive teeth?

Often, yes. SLS can aggravate gum sensitivity and oral tissue irritation. Removing it frequently reduces that discomfort. For more on this, see our best toothpaste for sensitive teeth – 2025 guide.

Can kids use SLS-free toothpaste?

Yes — and it's a good idea, especially for toddlers who may swallow some paste. Gentler formulas without SLS or artificial sweeteners are the cleaner choice for developing mouths.

Does glycerin in toothpaste matter too?

Some people prefer to avoid glycerin for similar reasons they avoid SLS — it's a processing aid that doesn't contribute to cleaning. Learn more in our guide on why glycerin-free toothpaste matters.
