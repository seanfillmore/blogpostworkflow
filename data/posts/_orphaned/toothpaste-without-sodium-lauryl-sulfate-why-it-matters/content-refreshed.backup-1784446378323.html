What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)?

Sodium lauryl sulfate is a synthetic detergent. Its job is to create foam by lowering the surface tension of water. Think of it like dish soap — effective at cutting grease, but rough on delicate surfaces. Your mouth's soft lining (the mucosa) is about as delicate as it gets.

SLS shows up in shampoos, body wash, face wash, and most conventional toothpastes. It's derived from petroleum or coconut/palm oil, but regardless of source, it goes through heavy chemical processing before it reaches your toothpaste tube.

One thing to know right away: foam is not the same as clean. The bubbles SLS creates feel thorough, but they're mostly a sensory experience. Real cleaning comes from mechanical brushing and ingredients like baking soda or calcium carbonate.

Why Is SLS in Toothpaste?

SLS is cheap, effective at spreading paste around your mouth quickly, and it creates the foamy feeling most people associate with a good brush. That's the whole story. Studies haven't shown that SLS meaningfully improves plaque removal compared to SLS-free formulas.

If you switch to SLS-free toothpaste and notice less foam, that's not a sign it's working less. It just means the product is more honest about what's happening.

Is SLS Safe? What the Research Says

Regulatory bodies including the FDA and the EU Cosmetics Regulation consider SLS safe at the concentrations used in toothpaste. This isn't a case of a hidden toxin in your cabinet.

That said, research connects SLS to a few specific problems:

  • Canker sores: Multiple clinical trials — including studies published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine — found that people using SLS toothpaste get more frequent and more severe mouth ulcers than those using SLS-free formulas. The link is consistent enough that many dentists recommend SLS-free toothpaste for canker sore-prone patients.
  • The orange juice effect: SLS degrades phospholipids that suppress bitter taste receptors. That's why orange juice tastes terrible after brushing. SLS-free toothpaste largely eliminates this.
  • Gum and tissue irritation: SLS can disrupt the protective lining of your mouth. For sensitive people, this causes rawness, irritation, or temporary dryness. Switching to SLS-free toothpaste is often a genuine fix — not just a wellness trend.

SLS vs. SLES vs. Natural Surfactant Alternatives

When reading labels, you'll run into more than just SLS. Here's what to know:

  • SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate): The harshest. Small molecule, penetrates soft tissue easily — which is why it's most linked to irritation and canker sores.
  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate): Chemically modified to be slightly milder, but still synthetic. The processing can leave trace amounts of 1,4-dioxane, flagged by consumer safety groups. Not a clean ingredient.
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate: Naturally derived from coconut oil and fermented sugars. Gentle, biodegradable, well-tolerated. Found in truly clean formulas.
  • Decyl glucoside: From coconut oil and glucose. Very mild and non-irritating. Common in kids' and sensitive formulas.
  • Coconut oil base (no surfactant): Some natural toothpastes skip surfactants entirely. Coconut oil doesn't foam, but it carries ingredients effectively across teeth and gums. This is the approach Real Skin Care uses — no synthetic surfactant of any kind.

Who Should Switch to SLS-Free Toothpaste?

SLS-free is a good choice for most people. For some, it's a genuine need:

  • Canker sore sufferers: The clinical evidence is strongest here. Many people see fewer mouth ulcers after switching.
  • Sensitive gums: If your gums bleed easily, feel raw after brushing, or your dentist has flagged thin tissue, cutting SLS reduces daily irritation.
  • Dry mouth (xerostomia): SLS worsens dry mouth by disrupting the mucosal barrier. If saliva production is already low, you don't need an extra irritant.
  • Kids and toddlers: Children's oral tissues are more sensitive, and toddlers often swallow some paste. Many parents specifically look for kids' toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate for this reason.
  • Sulfate-sensitive people: If SLS bothers your scalp or skin in shampoo or body wash, it can cause the same problems in your mouth.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free? What to Know When Going SLS-Free

These are two separate decisions that often come up together. Fluoride has strong, long-standing evidence for cavity prevention. Most dentists recommend it.

Some people prefer to avoid fluoride due to concerns about dental fluorosis in children, or simply a preference for fully natural products. You have real options either way:

  • Fluoride toothpaste without SLS: Cavity protection without the harsh surfactant. Several brands now offer this combination.
  • Toothpaste without fluoride and sodium lauryl sulfate: For a fully clean formula. Real Skin Care's fluoride-free toothpaste fits here.

If your dentist has recommended fluoride for cavity prevention or enamel repair, talk to them before changing. But for generally healthy adults with good brushing habits, a clean fluoride-free formula can work well.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label

Label reading gets easier once you know what to look for.

Avoid these:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
  • Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES)
  • Ammonium lauryl sulfate
  • Artificial dyes (FD&C Blue No. 1, FD&C Red No. 40)
  • Saccharin (synthetic sweetener)
  • Propylene glycol

Look for these:

  • Coconut oil — natural base with antimicrobial properties in lab studies
  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — gentle polish, neutralizes acids
  • Essential oils (peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, clove) — natural flavor with antimicrobial activity in lab settings
  • Stevia — plant-derived sweetener, no known harm to teeth
  • Myrrh — traditional oral care botanical with emerging antimicrobial research
  • Xanthan gum — plant-derived thickener, no concern
  • Sodium cocoyl glutamate / decyl glucoside — gentle, naturally derived surfactants if a formula needs one
