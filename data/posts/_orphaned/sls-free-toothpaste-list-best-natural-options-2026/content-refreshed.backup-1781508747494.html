What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — SLS for short — is a foaming agent. It's what makes your toothpaste lather up when you start brushing. That foam doesn't clean your teeth. It's a cosmetic effect that makes brushing feel familiar.

Manufacturers use SLS because it's cheap, stable, and easy to source. It helps spread the paste across your teeth and gums during brushing. You'll also find it in shampoos, body washes, dish soap, and face cleansers — it's one of the most common surfactants in personal care products, full stop.

At the levels found in toothpaste, SLS has a long safety history. Regulatory bodies generally consider it safe for oral use. That's worth saying clearly — this isn't a post about something dangerous hiding in your bathroom cabinet.

That said, a real number of people experience sensitivity to it. Research published in Acta Odontologica Scandinavica by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) found that SLS exposure was linked to more frequent canker sores — and that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste reduced how often they occurred in some patients.1 That study has been widely cited in dentistry literature for over three decades.

The mechanism isn't fully settled, but one working theory is that SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer inside your mouth — a natural protective coating — leaving the tissue more exposed to irritation.

What Is an SLS-Free Toothpaste?

An SLS-free toothpaste is simply a toothpaste that doesn't contain sodium lauryl sulfate anywhere on the ingredient list. No sulfate foaming agent. That's the whole definition.

But "SLS-free" doesn't automatically mean natural, clean, or gentle. A formula can skip SLS and still be loaded with artificial sweeteners, synthetic dyes, or other surfactants. The absence of one ingredient doesn't tell you much about the rest of the list.

Genuinely clean SLS-free toothpastes go further. They replace the foam with functional ingredients — things like baking soda for gentle abrasion, coconut oil for antimicrobial action, and plant-based essential oils for flavor and freshness. Natural SLS-free toothpastes skip the surfactant entirely rather than swapping it for a milder cousin.

This distinction matters. It's the difference between a toothpaste that technically passes an SLS-free test and one that's actually built around a cleaner approach to oral care.

Not everyone needs to make the switch. But there are three groups of people who regularly find it worth trying.

One important note: this isn't a diagnosis. Canker sores have multiple causes. If you're dealing with ongoing mouth problems, talk to your dentist. How to Read a Toothpaste Label: What "SLS-Free" Actually Means

"SLS-free" means sodium lauryl sulfate does not appear on the ingredient list. Simple enough — but there are a few things worth knowing before you trust that label at face value.

Watch for Close Relatives of SLS

Two related compounds sometimes appear in formulas that claim to be SLS-free:

SLES (sodium laureth sulfate / sodium lauryl ether sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. It's generally considered less irritating because it's processed differently, but it's still a sulfate surfactant. Some people who react to SLS tolerate SLES fine. Others don't.

SCS (sodium coco sulfate): Often marketed as a "natural" or "coconut-derived" alternative. It is derived from coconut oil — but it's still a sulfate, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Plant-derived doesn't automatically mean gentle. If you see sodium coco sulfate toothpaste marketed as clean, look closely at the rest of the label before deciding.

SLS-Free Doesn't Mean Fully Clean

A formula can skip SLS and still contain artificial sweeteners (like saccharin), synthetic dyes, parabens, or triclosan. The absence of one ingredient doesn't tell you much about everything else.

Alternative Surfactants to Know

Some formulas use cocamidopropyl betaine — a coconut-derived, milder foaming agent used in gentle cleansing products. Others skip surfactants entirely and rely on baking soda and oils to clean without any foam at all. Both approaches can work well depending on what you're looking for.

Quick Label-Reading Checklist

  • Check for SLS and its relatives: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium laureth sulfate," and "sodium coco sulfate" on the ingredient list.
  • Scan the full list, not just the front label: "Natural" on the front means nothing legally. The ingredient list on the back is what counts.
  • Count the ingredients: A shorter, more recognizable list is usually a sign of a simpler, cleaner formula.
  • Look up anything you don't recognize: If you can't find clear information about an ingredient, that's worth noting.

What to Look For in a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Toothpaste

Avoiding SLS is the starting point — not the finish line. Here's what a genuinely clean formula looks like when you apply the same scrutiny to the whole ingredient list.

No sulfate-based surfactants: That means no SLS, no SLES, no SCS. If you want a truly surfactant-free toothpaste, look for formulas that clean through mild abrasives like baking soda rather than foaming agents.

No artificial dyes or synthetic fragrances: These add nothing to the cleaning function and are common sensitivity triggers. A toothpaste flavored with real peppermint essential oil is always better than "flavor" listed as a vague, anonymous ingredient.

No parabens or triclosan: Parabens are preservatives with a contested safety record. Triclosan is an antimicrobial agent that's been phased out of many products. If you see either one, it's worth questioning.

Functional natural ingredients: This is where clean formulas earn their keep. Organic coconut oil has been studied for its antimicrobial properties in oral care — a 2015 study by Peedikayil et al. published in the Nigerian Medical Journal found that coconut oil reduced plaque and gingivitis markers in a small study population.2 Baking soda provides gentle abrasion to remove surface stains. Wildcrafted myrrh has a long history of use in oral care for its astringent and soothing qualities.

A short, transparent ingredient list: If you can't find information about every ingredient on the label, that's a signal. Transparency is the baseline for any brand worth trusting.

Best SLS-Free Toothpaste for Sensitive Teeth

If your teeth or gums are sensitive, the formula you use matters more than average. SLS is a common trigger — but it's not the only one. Here's what to look for specifically when sensitivity is your main concern.

Skip all sulfate surfactants: SLS, SLES, and SCS can all irritate sensitive oral tissue. For truly sensitive mouths, it's worth avoiding the whole class — not just SLS.

Avoid strong abrasives: Some whitening toothpastes use high-RDA (relative dentin abrasivity) abrasives that can wear down enamel over time with sensitive teeth. Baking soda has a low RDA and is considered gentle even for sensitive use.

Look for soothing ingredients: Coconut oil, myrrh, and clove oil all have naturally soothing or antimicrobial properties that support gum health without harsh chemical action.

Avoid artificial sweeteners: Saccharin and artificial flavors can trigger sensitivity reactions in some people — especially those already prone to mouth irritation.

Consider fluoride needs: If enamel erosion is part of your sensitivity picture, discuss fluoride with your dentist. If you prefer fluoride-free, look for remineralizing ingredients like calcium or baking soda instead.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms come up together a lot in natural oral care conversations — but they refer to completely different things.

SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate — specifically, no sulfate-based foaming agent. That's about the cleaning action (or lathering).

Fluoride-free means the formula contains no fluoride — a mineral that conventional dentistry recommends for strengthening enamel and helping prevent cavities.

A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both SLS-free and fluoride-free, like our formula. These are separate decisions.

Whether you want fluoride in your toothpaste is a personal choice based on your oral health, diet, and water supply. There are legitimate perspectives on both sides. If you're unsure, talking to your dentist is the right move — especially if you have enamel concerns or a history of cavities.

The short version: you don't automatically need both. But if you're cleaning up your oral care routine, it makes sense to think about both decisions intentionally rather than by default.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does SLS-free toothpaste mean?

SLS-free toothpaste is toothpaste made without sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic foaming agent found in most conventional tubes. Without it, the toothpaste doesn't foam much or at all when you brush. That's normal. The foam was never doing the cleaning anyway. Cleaning comes from the abrasives, oils, and mechanical action of brushing.

Is sodium lauryl sulfate in toothpaste dangerous?

Not dangerous in the way a toxic ingredient would be. Regulatory bodies consider it safe for oral use at the concentrations found in toothpaste. The issue for some people is sensitivity — recurring canker sores, gum irritation, or a sore mouth after brushing. SLS has been associated with increased canker sore frequency in research going back to the 1990s. For most people it causes no problems. For some, removing it makes a noticeable difference.

What is sodium coco sulfate, and is it better than SLS?

Sodium coco sulfate (SCS) is a surfactant derived from coconut oil. It's often marketed as a natural, gentler alternative to SLS. It is plant-derived — but it's still a sulfate surfactant, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Some people tolerate it better than SLS; others don't notice a difference. If you're switching because of mouth sensitivity, it's worth avoiding the whole sulfate surfactant category rather than just SLS specifically.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for sensitive teeth?

For many people with sensitive gums or recurring canker sores, yes — switching to a sodium lauryl sulfate free toothpaste does help. The research on canker sores specifically is the most consistent. For general tooth sensitivity (temperature, pressure), SLS is less likely to be the culprit — that's more often related to enamel wear or exposed roots. But removing a known irritant from your oral care routine is a reasonable first step regardless.
