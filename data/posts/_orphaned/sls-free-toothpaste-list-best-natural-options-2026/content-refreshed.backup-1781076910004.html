What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — SLS for short — is a surfactant. That's just a fancy word for a foaming agent. It's what makes your toothpaste lather up when you start brushing.

That foam? It's mostly cosmetic. It doesn't clean your teeth. The lather just makes brushing feel familiar and satisfying — and that feeling is exactly why manufacturers keep using it.

SLS is cheap, stable, and widely available. It helps distribute the paste across your teeth and gums. You'll also find it in shampoos, body washes, dish soap, and face cleansers — it's one of the most common surfactants in personal care products, full stop.

In rinse-off products at low concentrations, SLS has a long safety history. Regulatory bodies generally consider it safe for oral use at the levels found in toothpaste. That's worth saying clearly — this isn't a post about something dangerous hiding in your bathroom cabinet.

That said, a meaningful number of people do experience real sensitivity. Research published in Acta Odontologica Scandinavica by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) found that SLS exposure was linked to more frequent canker sores — and that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste reduced how often they occurred in some patients.1 That study has been widely cited in dentistry for over three decades.

The mechanism isn't fully settled, but one working theory is that SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer — a natural protective coating — on the inside of your mouth, leaving your tissue more exposed to irritation. In plain terms: it may make the inside of your mouth more vulnerable with every brush.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label: What "SLS-Free" Actually Means

"SLS-free" means sodium lauryl sulfate does not appear on the ingredient list. Simple enough — but there are a few things worth knowing before you trust that label at face value.

Watch for close relatives. Two related compounds sometimes appear in SLS-free formulas:

  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate / sodium lauryl ether sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. It's generally considered less irritating because it's processed differently, but it's still a sulfate surfactant. Some people who are sensitive to SLS tolerate SLES better; others don't.
  • Sodium coco sulfate (SCS): Often marketed as a "natural" or "coconut-derived" surfactant. It is — technically — derived from coconut oil. But it's still a sulfate, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Just because something is plant-derived doesn't automatically make it gentle. If you see "sodium coco sulfate toothpaste" marketed as a clean alternative to SLS, read the full label carefully before buying.

Understand that "SLS-free" alone doesn't mean clean. A formula can skip SLS and still contain artificial sweeteners like saccharin, synthetic dyes, parabens, or triclosan. The absence of one ingredient doesn't tell you much about the rest of the list.

What to Look For in a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Toothpaste

Avoiding SLS is the starting point — not the finish line. Here's what a genuinely clean formula looks like when you apply the same scrutiny to the whole ingredient list.

No sulfate-based surfactants. That means no SLS, no SLES, no sodium coco sulfate. If you want a truly surfactant-free toothpaste, look for formulas that rely on mild abrasives like baking soda rather than foaming agents.

No artificial dyes or synthetic fragrances. These add nothing to the cleaning and are common sensitivity triggers. A toothpaste flavored with real peppermint essential oil is always preferable to "flavor" listed as a mystery ingredient.

No parabens or triclosan. Parabens are preservatives with a contested safety record. Triclosan is an antimicrobial agent that's been phased out of many products — if you still see it on a label in 2026, that's worth questioning.

Functional natural ingredients that actually do something. This is where clean formulas earn their keep.

  • Organic coconut oil has been studied for its antimicrobial properties in oral care contexts. A 2015 study by Peedikayil et al. in the Nigerian Medical Journal found coconut oil pulling reduced plaque and gingivitis markers in a small study population.2
  • Baking soda provides gentle abrasion to help remove surface stains without scratching enamel.
  • Wildcrafted myrrh has a long history in oral care for its astringent and soothing properties.
  • Essential oils like clove and cinnamon bring antimicrobial properties alongside flavor.

A short, transparent ingredient list. If you can't find information about every ingredient on the label, that's a signal. Transparency is the baseline.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms come up together a lot in natural oral care conversations — but they refer to completely different things. It's worth separating them clearly.

SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate — specifically, no sulfate-based foaming agent. That's about how the paste lathers (or doesn't).

Fluoride-free means the formula contains no fluoride — a mineral that conventional dentistry recommends for strengthening enamel and helping prevent cavities.

A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both SLS-free and fluoride-free, like our formula. These are independent choices.

Whether you want fluoride in your toothpaste is a personal decision based on your oral health history, your diet, your water supply, and your values. There are legitimate perspectives on both sides of that conversation. We won't make the choice for you — but we do think you should be making it consciously, not just defaulting to whatever came in a three-pack at the grocery store.
