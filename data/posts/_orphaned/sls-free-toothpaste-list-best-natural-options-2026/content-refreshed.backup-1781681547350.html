What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — SLS for short — is a surfactant. That's a fancy word for a foaming agent. It's what makes your toothpaste lather up when you start brushing.

And that foam? It's mostly cosmetic. It doesn't clean your teeth. The lather just makes brushing feel familiar and satisfying. Manufacturers love SLS because it's cheap, stable, and easy to source. You'll find it in shampoos, body washes, dish soap, and face cleansers — it's one of the most common synthetic surfactants in personal care products.

In low concentrations and rinse-off products, SLS has a long safety history. Regulatory bodies generally consider it safe for oral use at the levels found in toothpaste. That's worth saying clearly — this isn't a post about something dangerous hiding in your bathroom cabinet.

That said, a meaningful number of people do experience sensitivity. Research published in Acta Odontologica Scandinavica by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) found that SLS exposure was linked to more frequent canker sores — the painful little mouth ulcers that keep coming back — and that switching to a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate reduced their occurrence in some patients.1 That study has been widely cited in dentistry for over 30 years.

The exact reason isn't fully settled, but one working theory is that SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer — a natural protective coating — on the inside of your mouth, leaving the tissue more exposed to irritation. Think of it like stripping a protective film off a surface that needs it.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label: What "SLS-Free" Actually Means

"SLS-free" means sodium lauryl sulfate does not appear on the ingredient list. Simple enough — but there are a few things worth knowing before you trust that label at face value.

Watch for Close Relatives

Two related compounds sometimes appear in SLS-free formulas:

SLES (sodium laureth sulfate / sodium lauryl ether sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. It's generally considered less irritating because it's processed differently, but it's still a sulfate surfactant. Some people who react to SLS tolerate SLES better; others don't.

SCS (sodium coco sulfate): Often marketed as a "natural" or "coconut-derived" surfactant. Technically, it is derived from coconut oil. But it's still a sulfate, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Plant-derived doesn't automatically mean gentle. If you've been searching for info on "sodium coco sulfate toothpaste," this is the key thing to know: it's a step up from SLS, but it's not the same as truly surfactant-free.

"SLS-Free" Doesn't Automatically Mean Clean

A formula can skip SLS and still contain artificial sweeteners like saccharin, synthetic dyes, parabens, or triclosan. The absence of one ingredient doesn't tell you much about the rest of the list. You need to read the whole thing.

Some formulas use alternative surfactants like cocamidopropyl betaine — a coconut-derived, milder foaming agent used in many gentle cleansing products. Others skip surfactants entirely and rely on baking soda and oils to clean without foam. Both approaches can work well depending on what your mouth needs.

Quick Label-Reading Checklist

  • Check for SLS and its relatives: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium laureth sulfate," and "sodium coco sulfate" on the ingredient list — not just the front label.
  • Scan the full list, not just the marketing claims: "Natural" on the front label means nothing legally. The ingredient list on the back is what counts.
  • Count the ingredients: A shorter, more recognizable ingredient list is usually a signal of a simpler, cleaner formula.
  • Look at the sweetener: Saccharin is common in conventional toothpastes. Stevia or xylitol are cleaner options.
  • Check for "flavor" as a catch-all: A toothpaste flavored with real peppermint or spearmint essential oil is always preferable to "flavor" listed as an anonymous ingredient.

What to Look For in a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Toothpaste

Avoiding SLS is the starting point — not the finish line. Here's what a genuinely clean formula looks like when you apply the same scrutiny to the whole ingredient list.

No sulfate-based surfactants. That means no SLS, no SLES, no SCS. If you want a truly surfactant-free toothpaste, look for formulas that rely on mild abrasives like baking soda rather than foaming agents to do the cleaning.

No artificial dyes or synthetic fragrances. These add nothing to the cleaning function and are common sensitivity triggers. A toothpaste flavored with real peppermint essential oil is always a better sign than "flavor" listed with no further detail.

No parabens or triclosan. Parabens are preservatives with a contested safety record. Triclosan is an antimicrobial agent that's been phased out of many products — if you see it on a label in 2026, that's worth questioning.

Functional natural ingredients. This is where clean formulas earn their keep. Organic coconut oil has been studied for its antimicrobial properties in oral care — a 2015 study by Peedikayil et al. in the Nigerian Medical Journal found that coconut oil reduced plaque and gingivitis markers in a small study population.2 Baking soda provides gentle abrasion to help remove surface stains. Wildcrafted myrrh has been used in oral care for centuries for its astringent and soothing properties.

A short, transparent ingredient list. If you can't find information about every ingredient on the label, that's a signal. Transparency is the baseline for any brand worth trusting.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms come up together a lot in natural oral care conversations — but they refer to completely different things. It's worth separating them clearly.

SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate — no sulfate-based foaming agent. That's about how the toothpaste cleans (or in this case, what's doing the lathering).

Fluoride-free means the formula contains no fluoride — the mineral that conventional dentistry recommends for strengthening enamel and helping prevent cavities.

A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both SLS-free and fluoride-free, like our formula. These are two separate decisions.

Whether you want fluoride in your toothpaste is a personal decision based on your oral health needs and values. There are thoughtful perspectives on both sides. If you want a full breakdown, our post on fluoride-free toothpaste: benefits, how it works, and best picks covers the topic in detail.

The short version: if your primary concern is mouth irritation or canker sores, focus on the SLS question first. The fluoride question is worth asking separately, on its own terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an SLS-free toothpaste?

An SLS-free toothpaste is any toothpaste made without sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic surfactant that creates foam in most conventional tubes. Instead of SLS, these formulas use gentler cleaning agents like baking soda, coconut oil, or mild plant-derived surfactants. The result is a toothpaste that still cleans your teeth but doesn't produce as much foam — and doesn't carry the same irritation risk for sensitive mouths.

Is SLS-free toothpaste actually better for you?

For most people, the difference is small. SLS at the concentrations used in toothpaste has a solid safety record. But for people who get recurring canker sores, have sensitive gums, or experience mouth irritation after brushing, switching to a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate can make a real difference. The 1994 Herlofson and Barkvoll study remains one of the clearest data points here — it found reduced canker sore frequency in patients who switched away from SLS-containing toothpaste.1

Does SLS-free toothpaste still clean effectively?

Yes. The foam from SLS doesn't actually clean your teeth — it just feels like it does. The real cleaning work is done by abrasives (like baking soda) and the mechanical action of your toothbrush. An SLS-free formula with baking soda and a good brushing technique cleans your teeth just as well as a foamy conventional paste.

What's the difference between SLS and sodium coco sulfate in toothpaste?

Both are sulfate-based surfactants, but they're not identical. SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate) is a synthetic surfactant derived primarily from petroleum or palm oil. Sodium coco sulfate (SCS) is derived from coconut oil and is generally considered slightly milder — it's a mixture of fatty acid sulfates rather than a single compound. However, SCS is still a sulfate, and people with very sensitive mouths may still react to it. If you're switching specifically to reduce irritation, look for formulas that skip both.
