What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — SLS for short — is a surfactant. That's just a fancy word for a foaming agent. It's what makes your toothpaste lather up when you start brushing.

That foam? It's cosmetic. It doesn't clean your teeth. The lather just makes brushing feel familiar and satisfying.

Manufacturers love SLS because it's cheap, stable, and easy to source. It does a reasonable job of helping spread the paste across your teeth and gums. You'll also find it in shampoos, body washes, dish soap, and face cleansers — it's one of the most common surfactants in personal care products.

In rinse-off products used at low concentrations, SLS has a long safety history. Regulatory bodies generally consider it safe for oral use at the levels found in toothpaste. That's worth saying clearly — this isn't a post about something dangerous hiding in your bathroom cabinet.

That said, a meaningful number of people do experience sensitivity. Research published in Acta Odontologica Scandinavica by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) found that SLS exposure was linked to a higher frequency of canker sores — and that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste reduced how often they came back in some patients.1 That study has been widely cited in dentistry for over 30 years.

The mechanism isn't fully settled. One working theory is that SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer — a natural protective coating — on the inside of your mouth, leaving the tissue more exposed to irritation.

How to Read a Toothpaste Label: What "SLS-Free" Actually Means

"SLS-free" means sodium lauryl sulfate does not appear on the ingredient list. Simple enough — but there are a few things worth knowing before you trust that label at face value.

Watch for Close Relatives

Two related compounds sometimes appear in SLS-free formulas:

  • SLES (sodium laureth sulfate / sodium lauryl ether sulfate): A milder cousin of SLS. It's generally considered less irritating because it's processed differently, but it's still a sulfate surfactant. Some people who are sensitive to SLS tolerate SLES better; others don't.
  • SCS (sodium coco sulfate): Often marketed as a "natural" or "coconut-derived" surfactant. It is — technically — derived from coconut oil. But it's still a sulfate, and it can still irritate sensitive mouths. Just because something is plant-derived doesn't automatically make it gentle.

If you want a completely sulfate-free toothpaste, check for all three names: sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium laureth sulfate, and sodium coco sulfate. None of them should appear on the list.

"SLS-Free" Doesn't Mean "Clean"

A formula can skip SLS and still contain artificial sweeteners (like saccharin), synthetic dyes, parabens, or triclosan. The absence of one ingredient doesn't tell you much about the rest of the list. Always scan the full ingredient panel — not just the marketing claims on the front of the box.

Alternative Surfactants to Know

Some formulas use milder surfactants like cocamidopropyl betaine — a coconut-derived foaming agent used in gentle cleansing products. Others skip surfactants entirely and rely on baking soda and oils to clean without any foam at all. Both approaches can work well.

Quick Label-Reading Checklist

  • Check for SLS and its relatives: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium laureth sulfate," and "sodium coco sulfate."
  • Scan the full list, not just the marketing claims: "Natural" on the front label means nothing legally. The ingredient list on the back is what counts.
  • Count the ingredients: A shorter, more recognizable ingredient list is usually a sign of a simpler, cleaner formula.
  • Check the sweetener: Stevia and xylitol are plant-derived. Saccharin and aspartame are synthetic. The sweetener matters.

How to Compare Different SLS-Free Toothpaste Options

Once you know what to avoid, the next question is: how do you choose between the SLS-free options out there? Here's a simple framework.

1. Check the Full Ingredient List

Don't stop at the front label. Flip the tube or box and read every ingredient. Look for things you recognize. If you see a long string of chemical names you can't place, that's worth researching before you buy.

2. Decide on Fluoride

Some SLS-free toothpastes contain fluoride; others don't. This is a personal choice based on your oral health needs. If you have a history of cavities, talk to your dentist about whether fluoride matters for you. If you're looking for a fully natural formula, fluoride-free options exist — including ours. Our post on fluoride-free toothpaste: benefits, how it works, and best picks covers both sides of that decision.

3. Look at the Abrasive

Something has to do the scrubbing. In conventional toothpastes, that's often hydrated silica or calcium carbonate. In natural formulas, baking soda is a common and well-studied choice. It's gentle enough for daily use and effective at removing surface stains.

4. Consider the Flavor Source

Toothpastes that list "flavor" as a single anonymous ingredient leave you guessing. Formulas that list actual essential oils — peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon — are more transparent and often gentler on sensitive mouths.

5. Factor in Texture and Format

Natural toothpastes can feel different from conventional ones. Less foam, different consistency, no artificial sweetness. Some people adapt immediately; others take a week or two. That's normal. The formula is still working even without the lather.

Are natural SLS-free toothpastes easy to find? Increasingly, yes — especially online. The natural personal care market has expanded significantly in recent years. But reading the label yourself is still the best filter, because "natural" is not a regulated term. A product can call itself natural and still contain SLS.

What to Look For in a Genuinely Clean SLS-Free Toothpaste

Avoiding SLS is the starting point — not the finish line. Here's what a genuinely clean formula looks like when you apply the same scrutiny to the whole ingredient list.

No sulfate-based surfactants: That means no SLS, no SLES, no SCS. If you want a truly surfactant-free toothpaste, look for formulas that rely on mechanical cleaning from mild abrasives like baking soda rather than foaming agents.

No artificial dyes or synthetic fragrances: These add nothing to the cleaning function and are common sensitivity triggers. A toothpaste flavored with real peppermint essential oil is always preferable to "flavor" listed as a catch-all anonymous ingredient.

No parabens or triclosan: Parabens are preservatives with a contested safety record. Triclosan is an antimicrobial agent that's been phased out of many products — if you see it, it's worth questioning.

Functional natural ingredients: Organic coconut oil has been studied for its antimicrobial properties in oral care. A 2015 study by Peedikayil et al. in the Nigerian Medical Journal found coconut oil reduced plaque and gingivitis markers in a small study population.2 Baking soda provides gentle abrasion to help remove surface stains. Wildcrafted myrrh has a long history of use in oral care for its astringent and soothing properties.

A short, transparent ingredient list: If you can't find information about every ingredient on the label, that's a signal. Transparency is the baseline.

And it's worth asking about glycerin while you're at it — some clean-toothpaste advocates prefer glycerin-free formulas too. Our post on why glycerin-free toothpaste matters explains the reasoning.

SLS-Free vs. Fluoride-Free: Do You Need Both?

These two terms come up together a lot in natural oral care conversations — but they refer to completely different things. It's worth separating them.

SLS-free means no sodium lauryl sulfate — no sulfate-based foaming agent. That's about what's doing the lathering (or in clean formulas, what isn't).

Fluoride-free means no fluoride — a mineral that conventional dentistry recommends for strengthening enamel and helping prevent cavities.

A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride. Or it can be both SLS-free and fluoride-free, like our formula. Whether you want fluoride is a personal decision based on your oral health history and values. There are legitimate perspectives on both sides.

The short version: SLS-free and fluoride-free are separate choices. You don't have to pick one or the other — but you do need to check the label for both if both matter to you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "SLS-free toothpaste" actually mean?

It means the toothpaste does not contain sodium lauryl sulfate — the synthetic surfactant that creates foam in most conventional toothpastes. SLS-free formulas either use a milder surfactant or skip the foaming agent entirely and rely on other ingredients to clean your teeth.

Is SLS in toothpaste dangerous?

Not in the way that word usually implies. Regulatory bodies consider SLS safe at the levels found in toothpaste. But a meaningful number of people experience mouth irritation, canker sores, or sensitive gums that improve after switching to an SLS-free formula. It's not dangerous for most people — but it's not necessary either, and removing it is a low-risk change.

Are there completely sulfate-free toothpaste brands?

The key is reading the full ingredient list, because some products marketed as "sulfate-free" only skip SLS while still including sodium coco sulfate or sodium laureth sulfate.

What's the difference between SLS-free and sulfate-free toothpaste?

SLS-free specifically means no sodium lauryl sulfate. Sulfate-free is broader — it means no sulfate-based surfactants at all, including SLS, SLES (sodium laureth sulfate), and SCS (sodium coco sulfate). If you have sensitive teeth or gums, look for a formula that's completely sulfate-free rather than just SLS-free.

Can I find SLS-free toothpaste for the whole family?

Yes. SLS-free toothpastes work for adults and children alike. The main thing to check for kids is whether the formula contains fluoride (if that matters to you) and that the flavor is mild enough for younger brushers. Does SLS-free toothpaste actually clean your teeth?

Yes. The foam in conventional toothpaste is cosmetic — it doesn't do the cleaning. The actual cleaning comes from abrasives (like baking soda), antimicrobial ingredients (like coconut oil or essential oils), and the mechanical action of your toothbrush. SLS-free toothpastes clean your teeth just as effectively. They just don't lather as much, which can feel different at first but is completely normal.

Is SLS-free toothpaste good for canker sores?

It can help. Research by Herlofson and Barkvoll (1994) found that people who switched to SLS-free toothpaste had fewer canker sore recurrences. It's not a cure, and canker sores have multiple causes — but removing SLS from your daily routine is a low-effort change with a reasonable evidence base behind it.

How do I compare different SLS-free toothpaste options?

Start with the ingredient list, not the front label. Compare: (1) whether the formula is completely sulfate-free or just SLS-free; (2) what's doing the cleaning — baking soda, silica, or something else; (3) the sweetener — stevia and xylitol are natural, saccharin is synthetic; (4) the flavor source — named essential oils vs. anonymous "flavor"; and (5) total ingredient count. Fewer recognizable ingredients generally signals a cleaner formula.
