SLS Free Toothpaste: Why It Matters & Best Options

SLS-free toothpaste cleans your teeth just as effectively as conventional toothpaste — without the foaming agent that can cause canker sores, dry mouth, and gum irritation. Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is added purely for lather; it plays no role in actually cleaning teeth. If you get recurring mouth sores or your gums feel raw after brushing, switching to toothpaste without SLS is often the first thing dentists recommend.

We make our own SLS-free toothpaste at Real Skin Care, so we have a stake in this topic — we'll be upfront about that. But this guide is designed to be genuinely useful no matter what you decide to buy.

What Is SLS in Toothpaste?

SLS stands for sodium lauryl sulfate. It's a synthetic surfactant — a foaming agent — added to toothpaste to make it lather when you brush. You'll find it in shampoos, body washes, and dish soap too.

Here's the thing: SLS doesn't clean your teeth. The actual cleaning comes from brushing mechanics, mild abrasives like baking soda or hydrated silica, and active ingredients like fluoride. The foam is there because we've come to expect it — it signals "working" even though it isn't doing the work.

On a toothpaste label, it appears as Sodium Lauryl Sulfate in the inactive ingredients section. It may also appear as sodium dodecyl sulfate — worth knowing when you're reading labels. Research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine found SLS can irritate the soft tissue lining the mouth, especially with repeated daily exposure. That finding is a big reason why SLS-free and sulfate-free toothpaste options have grown well beyond the natural-beauty niche.

Why Switch to Toothpaste Without SLS?

Not everyone needs to make the switch — SLS isn't harmful for most people. But if any of the following sound familiar, it's worth trying a non-SLS toothpaste:

  • Canker sores: This is the biggest reason. Studies have found a clear link between SLS use and the frequency of aphthous ulcers (canker sores). Participants using SLS-free toothpaste had noticeably fewer outbreaks. source
  • Gum or mouth sensitivity: SLS disrupts the mucosal lining that protects soft tissue. If your inner cheeks feel raw or peel slightly after brushing, SLS is a likely cause.
  • The orange juice effect: SLS temporarily suppresses sweetness receptors while amplifying bitter ones — that's why juice tastes awful after brushing. Toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate doesn't do this.
  • Dry mouth: SLS strips natural oral moisture. If you already deal with dry mouth from medication or aging, SLS makes it worse — and a dry mouth raises cavity risk since saliva fights decay-causing bacteria.
  • Clean ingredient habits: If you already avoid SLS in your shampoo or face wash, it makes sense to keep it out of your toothpaste too.

To be clear: SLS is not toxic, and there's no credible link to cancer. But for sensitive people, it's a real irritant — and since it doesn't help clean your teeth, there's no downside to leaving it out. Does SLS-Free Toothpaste Actually Clean Your Teeth?

Yes. Foam does not equal clean. Cleaning comes from brushing, mild abrasives, and active ingredients like fluoride or nano-hydroxyapatite. SLS contributes none of that.

Many SLS-free toothpastes use gentler plant-derived surfactants like sodium cocoyl glutamate or coco-glucoside to help the paste spread without stripping your mouth's natural protective coating. Our own formula skips surfactants entirely and relies on baking soda and brushing to do the work.

You may notice less foam when you switch. Most people adjust within a week. Their mouths feel softer and more comfortable — and their teeth get just as clean.

Who Should Use SLS-Free Toothpaste?

SLS-free toothpaste is a good choice for almost anyone — but especially if you:

  • Get canker sores or recurring mouth ulcers
  • Have dry mouth, receding gums, or sensitive oral tissue
  • Are in orthodontic treatment or recovering from dental work
  • Take medications that affect soft tissue in your mouth
  • Notice food tastes off for hours after brushing
  • Prefer a shorter, cleaner ingredient list — for yourself or your kids

Children are especially good candidates. Their mouths are more sensitive to detergent-based irritants, and many pediatric dentists now recommend SLS-free toothpaste as a standard choice. See our best natural toothpaste for families guide for more.

Is Colgate SLS-Free?

Most standard Colgate products — Colgate Total, Optic White, Cavity Protection — contain SLS. However, some Colgate Sensitive formulas have been reformulated without it. The safest approach: read the inactive ingredients on the current packaging before you buy. Formulas change, and the label on the shelf is the only reliable source.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does SLS-free mean on a toothpaste label?

It means the formula contains no sodium lauryl sulfate — the foaming detergent added to most conventional toothpastes. The toothpaste may still foam lightly from other gentler surfactants, or not at all.

Is sulfate-free the same as SLS-free?

Not exactly. SLS is one type of sulfate. A toothpaste labeled "sulfate-free" should be SLS-free, but always check the ingredient list — the terms are sometimes used loosely.

Can SLS-free toothpaste still whiten teeth?

Yes. Whitening comes from mild abrasives or ingredients like baking soda — not from SLS. Our coconut whitening toothpaste guide covers how natural whitening works without harsh additives.

Does toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate and fluoride still prevent cavities?

Fluoride is the most clinically studied cavity-prevention ingredient. Without it, prevention relies on good brushing habits, limiting sugar, and supporting remineralization through diet and saliva.
