What Is SLS in Toothpaste?

SLS stands for sodium lauryl sulfate. It's a synthetic foaming agent added to most conventional toothpastes to make them lather. You'll find it in shampoos, body wash, and dish soap too.

Here's the key fact: SLS doesn't clean your teeth. The foam is cosmetic — it signals "working" without doing any actual cleaning. Real cleaning comes from brushing mechanics, mild abrasives like baking soda, and active ingredients like fluoride or hydroxyapatite.

On a label, look for: Sodium Lauryl Sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or monododecyl ester. All are the same ingredient. Toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate will not list any of these.

What SLS Does to Your Mouth

SLS isn't dangerous for most people, but it is a real irritant for many. Here's what the research and dentists point to:

  • Canker sores: This is the biggest one. A widely cited study by Herlofson and Barkvoll (Acta Odontologica Scandinavica, 1994) found that participants averaging roughly 18 canker sores per three months saw significant improvement when they switched to toothpaste without SLS. Many dentists suggest making the switch as a first step for recurring mouth ulcers. See our sensitive teeth guide for more on this.
  • Gum and mouth sensitivity: SLS disrupts the thin mucin layer inside your mouth — a natural coating that protects soft tissue. Strip it repeatedly and some people notice raw inner cheeks or peeling lips after brushing.
  • The orange juice effect: SLS temporarily suppresses sweetness receptors while enhancing bitter ones. That's why OJ tastes metallic after you brush. Non-SLS toothpaste tends not to cause this.
  • Dry mouth: SLS strips natural oral moisture. If you already deal with dry mouth — from medication, aging, or health conditions — SLS can make it worse. A dry mouth raises cavity risk because saliva fights decay-causing bacteria.

To be clear: the FDA, the American Cancer Society, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer have all confirmed there is no credible link between SLS and cancer. But for sensitive individuals, it's a legitimate irritant — and since it adds nothing to cleaning, there's no real reason to keep it in your toothpaste.

Does SLS-Free Toothpaste Still Clean Your Teeth?

Yes. Completely. Foam has nothing to do with how clean your teeth get.

Many sulfate-free toothpastes use milder plant-derived surfactants — like sodium cocoyl glutamate or coco-glucoside — to help the paste spread without stripping your mouth's natural lining. Our formula skips surfactants altogether and relies on baking soda and brushing to do the work.

When you first switch to toothpaste without SLS, less foam can feel strange. Most people adjust within a few days — and notice their mouths feel softer and more comfortable afterward.

Who Should Use SLS-Free Toothpaste?

Non-SLS toothpaste is a reasonable choice for almost anyone. It's especially worth making the switch if you:

  • Get recurring canker sores or mouth ulcers
  • Have sensitive gums, dry mouth, or receding gums
  • Are in orthodontic treatment or recovering from dental work
  • Notice food tastes off for hours after brushing
  • Take medication that affects your mouth's soft tissue
  • Want a shorter, cleaner ingredient list for yourself or your kids

Children are especially good candidates. Their mouths are more sensitive to detergent-based irritants, and many pediatric dentists now recommend SLS-free formulas as a standard choice — not just for kids with mouth sores. See our best natural toothpaste for families guide for more.

Fluoride-Free vs. Fluoride: Does It Matter When Going SLS-Free?

These are separate questions. You can get SLS-free toothpaste with fluoride or without — the two ingredients are unrelated. Most dentists still recommend fluoride for cavity protection, especially for children. Fluoride-free options using hydroxyapatite are growing in popularity as an alternative mineral-based approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for canker sores?

Yes, for many people. Multiple studies have found a link between SLS and the frequency and severity of canker sores. Switching to toothpaste without SLS is one of the first things dentists often suggest for recurring mouth ulcers — it's low-risk and worth trying.

Does SLS-free toothpaste foam less?

Usually, yes. Less foam can feel different at first, but it has no effect on how well the toothpaste cleans. Most people adjust within a few days.

How do I know if my toothpaste has SLS?

Check the inactive ingredients. Look for "Sodium Lauryl Sulfate," "sodium dodecyl sulfate," or "monododecyl ester." If none of those appear, the formula is SLS-free.

Is sulfate-free toothpaste the same as SLS-free?

Essentially, yes. "Sulfate-free toothpaste" typically means free of sodium lauryl sulfate and similar sulfate compounds (like SLES and SCS). The terms are used interchangeably.
