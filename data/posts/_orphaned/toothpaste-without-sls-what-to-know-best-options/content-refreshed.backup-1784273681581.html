What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

SLS stands for sodium lauryl sulfate. It's a synthetic surfactant — a foaming agent — added to toothpaste to make it lather when you brush. You'll also find it in shampoo, body wash, and dish soap.

Here's what matters: SLS doesn't clean your teeth. The foam is a sensory experience. Real cleaning comes from the mechanical action of brushing, mild abrasives like baking soda or hydrated silica, and active ingredients like fluoride. The lather just signals "this is working" — it isn't actually doing the work.

SLS is derived from lauric acid (found naturally in coconut oil), but the finished ingredient is highly processed. On an FDA-regulated toothpaste label, it appears as Sodium Lauryl Sulfate, usually in the inactive ingredients section.

Why Go SLS-Free? The Honest Reasons

SLS isn't dangerous for most people. But for some, it's a real irritant — and since it contributes nothing to cleaning, there's no downside to leaving it out. Here's who benefits most from switching:

  • Frequent canker sores: This is the strongest reason. Research published in the Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine found that people using SLS-free toothpaste had noticeably fewer canker sores than those using a standard SLS formula. If you get recurring mouth sores, this is often the first thing dentists suggest changing. See our full guide to ingredients to avoid in toothpaste.
  • Sensitive gums or mouth tissue: SLS can disrupt the soft lining inside your mouth. For people with already-irritated gums, that daily exposure adds up.
  • Taste disruption: SLS temporarily blocks sweetness receptors and amplifies bitter ones — the reason orange juice tastes awful after brushing. SLS-free formulas don't cause this.
  • Clean ingredient preferences: If you already avoid SLS in your shampoo or face wash, it makes sense to keep it out of your toothpaste too.

Does SLS-Free Toothpaste Actually Clean Your Teeth?

Yes — completely. Foam does not equal clean. Brushing, abrasives, and active ingredients do the actual work. SLS just makes the paste feel frothy.

When you switch to a sodium lauryl sulfate free toothpaste, you'll notice less foam. That feels strange for a week or so. But your teeth get just as clean, and most people find their mouth feels better afterward — less raw, less dry.

Many dentists specifically recommend SLS-free formulas for patients with recurring canker sores, gum sensitivity, or dry mouth. The foam is cosmetic. The cleaning is real either way.

Is SLS-Free Toothpaste Better for Sensitive Gums?

For people with sensitive gums, yes — removing SLS is often a meaningful improvement. SLS disrupts the mucosal lining inside your mouth. With daily brushing, that repeated irritation can make gum sensitivity worse over time, not better.

Switching to an SLS-free toothpaste for sensitive teeth removes one consistent irritant from your routine. Many people notice their gums feel less raw within a few weeks.

For conventional sensitive-tooth formulas, look for potassium nitrate or stannous fluoride — both help calm nerve sensitivity. Sensodyne ProNamel covers both and is SLS-free. For a natural approach, gentle abrasives like baking soda and soothing botanicals like clove avoid additional irritation without needing synthetic desensitizing agents.
