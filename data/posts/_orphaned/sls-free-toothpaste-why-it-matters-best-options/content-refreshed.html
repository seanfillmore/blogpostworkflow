What Is SLS and Why Is It in Toothpaste?

Sodium lauryl sulfate — also listed as sodium dodecyl sulfate or SDS — is a synthetic surfactant. It foams. That's its main job. It was originally developed for industrial cleaning, then adopted by personal care brands because it's cheap and consumers associate lather with clean.

But foam doesn't clean your teeth. It's a cosmetic effect.

SLS works by disrupting the outer layer of cells. It's in the same ingredient class as dish soap. When it contacts the soft tissue lining your mouth, it strips away a protective barrier that's supposed to stay intact. Most people don't notice. But for anyone with naturally reactive or thinner oral tissue, it causes real problems.

SLS is different from SLES (sodium laureth sulfate) — a milder relative, but still a synthetic detergent worth avoiding in a clean formula. For a deeper look at the chemistry, see our explainer on 7 ingredients to avoid in natural toothpaste.

What the Research Says

A double-blind crossover study published in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology (Herlofson & Barkvoll, 1994) found that people using SLS toothpaste developed significantly more canker sores than those using SLS-free toothpaste. The researchers concluded SLS-free formulas should be considered for anyone with recurrent oral ulcers.

Separate research shows SLS temporarily disrupts taste receptor proteins — specifically the ones that detect sweetness. That's why food tastes bitter or metallic right after you brush. It's not in your head. It's a documented effect, and it goes away once the SLS washes out.

The FDA classifies SLS as generally recognized as safe at low concentrations. Worth acknowledging honestly: for most healthy adults, it isn't a medical emergency. But "safe at low doses" and "ideal for twice-daily contact with your mouth lining" are two different standards.

What to Look for on the Label

Not every "natural" toothpaste is genuinely clean. Some swap SLS for other synthetic detergents. Here's what to check:

  • Confirm SLS is absent: Look for "sodium lauryl sulfate," "sodium dodecyl sulfate," and "SDS" — all three are the same thing. If any appear, it's not SLS-free.
  • Look for a gentle cleaning base: Coconut oil is a natural antimicrobial base. Calcium carbonate is a mild abrasive some formulas use. Baking soda is common, though it can irritate sensitive mouths — our toothpaste contains neither.
  • Decide on fluoride: Fluoride has strong clinical backing for cavity prevention. Fluoride-free options using nano hydroxyapatite are gaining research support as an alternative. More on this below.
  • Check for natural sweeteners: Xylitol has some evidence for reducing cavity-causing bacteria. Our formula uses stevia — plant-based and gentle.
  • Avoid synthetic additives: Skip anything with artificial colors, carrageenan, triclosan, or parabens.

Fluoride or Fluoride-Free?

These are two separate decisions, but people often make them at the same time.

Fluoride has decades of clinical evidence behind it. The American Dental Association recommends fluoride toothpaste for most adults, and that recommendation is backed by consistent research. If you have high cavity risk, talk to your dentist before dropping it.

That said, many people prefer fluoride-free — for personal reasons, for young children where fluorosis (white spotting from excess fluoride during tooth development) is a concern, or as part of a broader clean-ingredient approach.

The most-researched fluoride-free alternative is nano hydroxyapatite — a biomimetic mineral that mirrors the natural composition of tooth enamel. It's been used in Japanese dentistry for decades. Studies by Limeback and others have shown it can support enamel remineralization, with some results comparable to fluoride at appropriate concentrations.

How to Make the Switch

  • Expect less foam: Foam is cosmetic, not functional. Your teeth can be just as clean — or cleaner — without it. It takes a few days to stop reaching for lather that isn't there.
  • Give it 2–4 weeks: If you have existing canker sores or gum irritation, oral tissue needs time to recover. Don't judge the switch in the first week.
  • Keep your technique consistent: Two minutes, twice daily, soft-bristle brush. Technique matters more than foam level.
  • Talk to your dentist if you're leaving fluoride behind: Especially if you have active cavities or high decay risk.
  • Store at room temperature: Coconut oil-based toothpaste may thicken in cold environments. Completely normal — let it warm up or hold the tube in your hands for a moment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for canker sores?

Yes. A double-blind crossover study by Herlofson & Barkvoll (Journal of Clinical Periodontology, 1994) found that people using SLS-free toothpaste developed significantly fewer canker sores than those using SLS-containing formulas. Removing SLS is one of the most evidence-backed steps for anyone with recurrent oral ulcers.

What does SLS-free mean on toothpaste?

It means the formula contains no sodium lauryl sulfate — also listed as sodium dodecyl sulfate or SDS. The toothpaste cleans without synthetic foaming agents.

Does SLS-free toothpaste still clean your teeth?

Yes. Foam is cosmetic — it doesn't remove plaque. Cleaning comes from the mechanical action of brushing and the antimicrobial or mildly abrasive ingredients in the formula. An SLS-free toothpaste cleans just as well as a foaming one.

Is SLS-free toothpaste safe for kids?

Yes — and often a better fit than conventional toothpaste. Children have thinner, more permeable oral tissue, so a gentler formula without synthetic detergents is a sensible choice. Look for a fluoride-free option if your child is young enough to swallow toothpaste. See our best natural toothpaste for families guide for more.

What's the difference between SLS-free and natural toothpaste?

"Natural" isn't regulated, so it doesn't guarantee SLS-free. Always check the ingredient list for sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, or SDS — regardless of what the front of the label says. For a deeper look, see our best natural toothpaste guide.

Why does food taste bad after brushing with regular toothpaste?

SLS temporarily disrupts the taste receptor proteins responsible for detecting sweetness, which makes food taste bitter or metallic right after you brush. It's a documented biochemical effect — not your imagination. Switching to an SLS-free formula typically resolves it within a few weeks.
