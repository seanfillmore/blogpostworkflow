What Is Coconut Oil Toothpaste?

Coconut oil toothpaste is any toothpaste where coconut oil plays a starring role — either as the main base or as a key active ingredient. It's usually combined with other natural ingredients like baking soda, essential oils, or natural sweeteners to create a complete cleaning formula.

You'll find it in a few different forms:

  • Homemade DIY versions: Mixed at home from scratch using coconut oil, baking soda, and essential oils. Easy to make, but consistency and shelf life can vary.
  • Commercially formulated natural toothpastes: Professionally made products where coconut oil is a hero ingredient, blended with other actives for texture, stability, and effectiveness.
  • Oil pulling with straight coconut oil: This is not toothpaste. Oil pulling is a separate practice where you swish a tablespoon of coconut oil around your mouth. It has its own potential benefits, but it doesn't replace brushing.

If you're wondering whether plain coconut oil can sub in for toothpaste entirely, we cover that question in depth in our guide: Can You Use Coconut Oil as Toothpaste? Here's What Science Says.

The reason coconut oil shows up in so many oral care formulas comes down to one compound: lauric acid. Coconut oil is roughly 50% lauric acid, a medium-chain fatty acid with well-documented antimicrobial properties. That's the active mechanism behind the oral health benefits — and the type of coconut oil you use affects how much lauric acid you're actually getting.

Why Coconut Oil Works in Toothpaste — The Science

This isn't wellness trend talk. There's real research backing coconut oil's role in oral care.

A study published in the Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research found that oil pulling with coconut oil significantly reduced plaque scores and gum inflammation in participants after just 30 days. While oil pulling isn't the same as brushing with coconut oil toothpaste, the study points to the same active mechanism: coconut oil's antimicrobial properties disrupting the bacteria responsible for plaque formation.

That bacteria has a name: Streptococcus mutans. It's the main culprit behind tooth decay and cavities. Research has shown that lauric acid — the dominant fatty acid in coconut oil — has meaningful antimicrobial activity against S. mutans. Specifically, lauric acid converts to monolaurin in the body, and monolaurin has been shown to break down bacterial cell membranes. This makes coconut oil functionally different from a neutral carrier oil that offers no antimicrobial benefit.

When coconut oil is paired with baking soda — which gently raises the mouth's pH to create a less acidic, bacteria-unfriendly environment — and natural essential oils like peppermint, spearmint, clove, and cinnamon (all of which have their own antimicrobial properties), the formula becomes significantly more effective than any single ingredient alone. Clove oil in particular contains eugenol, a compound long used in dentistry for its antibacterial and mild pain-relieving properties.

It's worth being clear: research is promising and ongoing, but coconut oil toothpaste is not a cure for cavities. It works best as part of a consistent oral care routine — brushing twice daily, flossing, and seeing your dentist regularly. That's true of any toothpaste, natural or conventional.

Which Type of Coconut Oil Is Best for Toothpaste?

Not all coconut oil is the same — and that matters when you're choosing it for oral care. If you want the antimicrobial benefits discussed above, the type of oil in your toothpaste matters quite a bit. Here's how to read the labels.

Virgin (Unrefined) Coconut Oil — Best Choice

This is the gold standard for toothpaste. Virgin coconut oil is minimally processed, which means it retains its full lauric acid content and natural plant compounds. It has a mild coconut scent and flavor. For oral care, this is what you want.

Refined Coconut Oil — A Step Down

Refined coconut oil has been processed to remove the scent and flavor. It's more neutral, but some of its natural compounds are reduced in the process. It still contains lauric acid, but it lacks some of the intact beneficial compounds found in unrefined oil.

"Extra Virgin" — Mostly a Marketing Label

Here's something worth knowing: in the coconut oil world, "extra virgin" is largely a marketing label, not a regulated standard (unlike olive oil, where extra virgin has a strict legal definition). Virgin and extra virgin coconut oil are essentially the same thing. Don't pay a premium for the "extra."

Fractionated Coconut Oil (MCT Oil) — Not Ideal for Toothpaste

This is liquid at room temperature and has a very long shelf life, which is why you see it in lots of cosmetics. But here's the catch: fractionated coconut oil has had most of its long-chain fatty acids removed, including the majority of its lauric acid. That means it loses most of the antimicrobial benefit that makes coconut oil useful in toothpaste. If a toothpaste lists fractionated coconut oil, it's likely there for texture, not for oral health action.

What to look for on the label: organic, virgin or unrefined, cold-pressed. Those three words together tell you you're getting coconut oil in its most beneficial form.

Is Coconut Oil Toothpaste Safe?

Yes — for most healthy adults, coconut oil toothpaste is safe to use daily. The ingredients are food-grade and well-tolerated. Here's what you should know:

  • Coconut oil: Generally recognized as safe. Rare allergies exist but are uncommon. If you're allergic to coconut, skip it.
  • Baking soda: Safe in toothpaste concentrations. Some people with very sensitive enamel prefer to use it every other day rather than twice daily — though at the amounts used in toothpaste, it's considered mild.
  • Essential oils (peppermint, clove, cinnamon): Safe at the low concentrations used in toothpaste formulas. Clove oil contains eugenol, which has a long history of safe use in dentistry.
  • Fluoride-free: Coconut oil toothpaste does not contain fluoride. For most healthy adults with a balanced diet and good oral hygiene habits, this is fine. If you're high-risk for cavities — due to dry mouth, a high-sugar diet, or a history of frequent decay — talk to your dentist before switching.
  • Don't swallow: This applies to any toothpaste. Rinse thoroughly after brushing.

How to Make Homemade Coconut Oil Toothpaste

If you want to try the DIY route first, here's a simple, practical recipe to get you started.

What You Need

  • 3 tablespoons organic virgin coconut oil (softened to room temperature)
  • 2 tablespoons baking soda
  • 10–15 drops peppermint essential oil
  • Optional: 1 teaspoon xylitol for natural sweetness
  • Optional: 5 drops clove essential oil for added antimicrobial support

Instructions

  1. Soften the coconut oil until it's scoopable but not fully liquid.
  2. Mix in the baking soda until smooth and well combined.
  3. Stir in the essential oils. Taste and adjust if needed.
  4. Transfer to a small, clean glass jar with a lid.
  5. Use within 2–3 weeks. Store at room temperature.
  6. Use a clean spoon to scoop — fingers introduce bacteria into the jar.

It's a solid starting point. But here's an honest note: homemade toothpaste has real limitations. The baking soda concentration can vary batch to batch. Essential oil quality varies widely by brand. There's no quality control in a home kitchen, and no stability testing to confirm your formula stays effective over time.

The texture can also shift a lot depending on room temperature — coconut oil hardens in cool weather and liquefies in warm conditions. If you find yourself making this regularly and want a more consistent, mess-free experience, a professionally formulated coconut oil toothpaste is worth considering.

How to Use Coconut Oil Toothpaste

Using coconut oil toothpaste is nearly identical to using conventional toothpaste. A few practical tips:

  • Use a pea-sized amount. That's all you need — same as any toothpaste. More isn't better.
  • Brush for two full minutes. Cover all surfaces: front, back, and the chewing surfaces of every tooth. Don't rush.
  • Brush twice daily. Morning and before bed. Consistency is what delivers results over time.
  • Temperature check. If you're using a coconut oil-based formula that hardens in cold weather (common with DIY versions), let it warm slightly before applying. Real Skin Care's squeeze bottle formula is designed for consistent texture regardless of temperature.
  • Don't swallow. This applies to any toothpaste. Rinse thoroughly after brushing.
  • Know your risk level. Coconut oil toothpaste is fluoride-free. For most healthy adults, that's a fine choice. If you're high-risk for cavities, talk to your dentist before making the switch.

Does Coconut Oil Toothpaste Actually Whiten Teeth?

Yes — but in a different way than bleaching products do. Coconut oil toothpaste whitens by removing surface stains, not by chemically altering the color of your enamel.

Here's how it works: baking soda is a mild abrasive that gently polishes away the surface discoloration left behind by coffee, tea, and food. Coconut oil helps lift and loosen that buildup. Used consistently over a few weeks, most people notice their teeth look brighter and cleaner — not Hollywood-white, but noticeably fresher.

This kind of gradual, gentle whitening is actually better for your enamel long-term. Harsh bleaching agents can weaken the enamel over time and cause sensitivity. Coconut oil toothpaste cleans without stripping.

Can Kids Use Coconut Oil Toothpaste?

Many parents choose coconut oil toothpaste for their kids precisely because it skips the synthetic dyes, SLS, and artificial sweeteners found in most children's toothpastes. That's a valid reason.

A few things to keep in mind:

  • Fluoride: Pediatric dentists typically recommend fluoride toothpaste for children, especially those prone to cavities. If you're considering a fluoride-free option for your child, check in with your dentist first.
  • Essential oils: The essential oils in coconut oil toothpaste (peppermint, clove, cinnamon) are safe at toothpaste concentrations for most older children, but may be too strong for very young kids or toddlers.
  • Flavor: Cinnamon and clove can taste strong to some children. Let your child try a tiny amount first to see if they like the flavor before committing to the full tube.
