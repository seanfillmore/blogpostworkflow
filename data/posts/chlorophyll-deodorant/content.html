What Is Chlorophyll and Why Are People Using It as a Deodorant?

Chlorophyll is the green pigment found in plants. It's what allows plants to absorb sunlight and convert it into energy — the engine behind photosynthesis. You get small amounts of it naturally through leafy greens like spinach and kale.

The supplement form people are actually taking is usually chlorophyllin — a water-soluble, semi-synthetic derivative made from sodium copper chlorophyllin. It's not the same as the chlorophyll in your salad. That distinction matters when you're trying to evaluate what the research says.

The trend got popularized on TikTok and Reddit, where users claimed that drinking liquid chlorophyll reduced body odor and bad breath from the inside out. The "internal deodorant" angle made it feel like a clean, holistic alternative to conventional products.

What most people don't realize is that this idea isn't new. Researchers in the mid-20th century were already exploring chlorophyll derivatives for internal deodorizing purposes. A 1951 study published in JAMA reported on orally administered chlorophyll compounds and their potential effects on body odor. So while it feels like a social media invention, the concept has actual research history behind it.

What Does the Science Actually Say?

Here's where it gets nuanced — and where a lot of online coverage gets it wrong.

Early research showed promise, but was limited. That 1951 JAMA study reported that orally administered chlorophyll derivatives appeared to reduce body odor in some patients. But it was a small, early study conducted with very different research standards than we apply today. It's interesting evidence — not a green light to swap your deodorant for chlorophyll drops.

Current evidence is thin. The Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) addresses this directly in their explainer on chlorophyll supplements, noting that research is limited and evidence remains largely anecdotal. In other words: people report results, but well-controlled studies confirming those results are lacking.

Chlorophyllin does have real properties worth noting. According to WebMD's chlorophyllin overview, the compound has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. That's genuinely interesting. But antioxidant activity doesn't automatically translate into odor control, and the overview does not confirm chlorophyllin as a reliable solution for body odor.

The bottom line: the science is intriguing but not conclusive. There isn't enough peer-reviewed evidence to recommend chlorophyll supplements as a dependable deodorant replacement. Some people try it and feel it helps. Others notice nothing. Without larger, well-designed studies, it's impossible to know how much of that is the supplement and how much is something else entirely.

How Body Odor Actually Forms (and Why This Matters)

Sweat itself is largely odorless. Body odor happens when bacteria that live on the skin's surface break down sweat compounds — particularly in the underarm area, where apocrine glands produce a richer, protein-containing sweat that bacteria thrive on. That bacterial activity is what creates the smell.

This is a skin-surface process. It happens outside your body, not inside it. Which is why topical deodorants — products that directly address the skin environment where the bacteria are active — tend to have a more direct mechanism of action than an internal supplement.

But the primary driver of underarm odor is topical and bacterial. Addressing it at the skin level is simply more targeted.

Who Might Benefit from Chlorophyll Supplements (and Who Should Be Cautious)

Chlorophyll supplements are generally considered safe for most healthy adults when taken as directed. But a few things are worth knowing before you start:

  • Green discoloration of stool and urine: This is harmless but can be surprising if you don't expect it.
  • Digestive upset: Some people experience nausea or loose stools, especially at higher doses.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements recommends consulting a healthcare provider before taking any supplement if you're pregnant or breastfeeding. This applies to chlorophyllin too.
  • It's a derivative, not pure chlorophyll: If you're looking for a fully "natural" supplement, it's worth understanding that chlorophyllin involves a chemical modification process — copper replaces magnesium in the molecule.

Nothing in this section constitutes medical advice. Please talk to a healthcare professional before adding any supplement to your routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does chlorophyll actually work as a deodorant?
The research is limited. A 1951 JAMA study suggested chlorophyllin supplements may help reduce internal body odor in some people, and anecdotal reports exist — but current evidence isn't strong enough to recommend it as a deodorant replacement. As CHOP notes, the evidence remains largely anecdotal. Topical natural deodorants address odor at the source — the bacteria on your skin — more directly.

Can I use chlorophyll instead of deodorant?
Not reliably. Chlorophyll supplements target internal factors, but underarm odor is primarily caused by bacteria acting on the skin's surface. A topical natural deodorant addresses that mechanism directly and is a more dependable primary solution for most people.

Is chlorophyll safe to take every day?
For most healthy adults, chlorophyllin supplements are generally considered safe at recommended doses. Common side effects include green-tinted stool and mild digestive upset. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, or managing a health condition, talk to your healthcare provider first.

What is the healthiest natural deodorant?
The healthiest options tend to be aluminum-free, made with clean recognizable ingredients, and free of synthetic fragrance. Read more about what makes a deodorant healthy before you shop.
