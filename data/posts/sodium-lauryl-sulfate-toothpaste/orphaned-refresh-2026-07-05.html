What Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate in Toothpaste?

SLS is a synthetic surfactant — a foaming agent that helps toothpaste spread across your teeth and creates that bubbly sensation most of us grew up associating with "clean." Its main job is cosmetic. It doesn't clean your teeth on its own.

On ingredient labels, SLS appears under a few names:

  • Sodium lauryl sulfate — the most common listing
  • Sodium dodecyl sulfate — the technical chemical name
  • SDS — the abbreviation for sodium dodecyl sulfate
  • LSL — a shorthand sometimes used in ingredient tables

If you see any of those on the label, the formula contains SLS regardless of how natural the packaging looks. The EWG Skin Deep database rates SLS with moderate concern for irritation, noting that effects depend on concentration and how long it stays in contact with tissue.

Does Colgate Contain Sodium Lauryl Sulfate?

Yes. Most Colgate toothpastes — including Colgate Total, Colgate Cavity Protection, and Colgate Optic White — list sodium lauryl sulfate as an inactive ingredient. It's one of the most widely used toothpaste surfactants in the world.

If you're checking a specific Colgate product, look in the "inactive ingredients" section of the label. SLS will appear there if it's present.

Does Crest Contain Sodium Lauryl Sulfate?

Yes. Crest toothpastes, including Crest Pro-Health and Crest 3D White, typically contain SLS. As with Colgate, it appears in the inactive ingredients list. If you want to confirm a specific Crest product, check the label directly — formulations vary across product lines.

What Does the Research Say About SLS in Toothpaste?

A peer-reviewed review published in PMC (Sabri et al., PMC10506142) looked at the clinical evidence on SLS and oral health. Three findings stand out.

Canker sores. SLS-containing toothpaste is linked to higher frequency and severity of recurrent aphthous stomatitis — the clinical term for canker sores. The likely reason: SLS strips away the mucin layer, a thin protein coating that protects the inside of your mouth. Without it, tissue becomes more vulnerable to irritation and ulcers.

Bitter taste after brushing. SLS can break down phospholipids on the surface of your taste buds. This disrupts the receptor for sweetness, making things taste bitter or metallic for a while after you brush. That orange juice problem? SLS is why.

Dry mouth sensitivity. SLS can dehydrate the mucin layer itself. If you already have dry mouth, SLS makes it worse by removing one of the mouth's natural defenses.

The counterpoint: at low concentrations, SLS shows some antimicrobial activity and has been studied for plaque reduction. It isn't useless. But for people with canker sores, dry mouth, or taste disruption, SLS-free formulas produce measurably better outcomes. The question is whether the foam is worth the trade-off.

Is Sodium Lauryl Sulfate Bad in Toothpaste?

"Bad" depends on who you are. At the concentrations used in toothpaste, the FDA and EWG consider SLS safe for most people. But safe and well-tolerated aren't the same thing. If you get canker sores, have dry mouth, or notice a bitter taste after brushing, SLS is a reasonable first ingredient to cut. If none of those apply to you, the risk is low.

How to Identify SLS in Toothpaste Ingredients

Check the inactive ingredients list — SLS almost always appears there, not in the active ingredients. Look for: sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium dodecyl sulfate, SDS, or LSL. Some labels use "SFS" (sodium fatty sulfate) as a related compound — it's structurally similar and worth noting, though less common. If you see any of these, the formula contains a sulfate surfactant.

Gentler Alternatives to SLS in Toothpaste

Not all surfactants are created equal. A few worth knowing:

Sodium lauroyl sarcosinate is a milder surfactant derived from sarcosine (an amino acid). It still produces some foam but is considered gentler on oral tissue. Studies suggest it's less likely to disrupt the mucin layer than SLS. It appears in several "sensitive" and natural toothpaste formulas and is generally regarded as safe for daily oral use.

Lauryl glucoside is a plant-derived surfactant made from coconut or palm oil and glucose. It's non-ionic, meaning it's less likely to irritate tissue than anionic surfactants like SLS. Lauryl glucoside safety in oral care products is well-regarded — it's used in natural and organic toothpaste formulas as a gentler foaming base.

Coconut oil doesn't foam at all, but foam was never doing the cleaning. It works as a base and has documented antimicrobial properties. It's the foundation of Real Skin Care's toothpaste formula.

Who Should Avoid SLS in Toothpaste?

Based on the clinical evidence, these groups have the most to gain from switching:

  • People with recurrent canker sores — multiple studies show that cutting SLS reduces both frequency and severity of outbreaks
  • People with dry mouth — SLS strips the mucin layer that's already compromised
  • People who notice a bitter taste after brushing — that's the phospholipid disruption mechanism; SLS-free formulas don't cause it
  • People with general oral sensitivity — if your gums feel raw after brushing, SLS is a logical first thing to eliminate
  • People reading labels on everything else they use — if you're checking your shampoo and body wash, your toothpaste deserves the same look

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sodium lauryl sulfate in toothpaste safe?

At the concentrations used in toothpaste, the FDA and EWG consider SLS safe for most people. But safe and well-tolerated are different things. For people prone to canker sores, dry mouth, or taste disruption, the clinical evidence supports switching to an SLS-free formula.

Does SLS-free toothpaste clean as well?

Yes. Foam is cosmetic — it doesn't do the cleaning. The actual work is done by abrasives like baking soda, antimicrobial ingredients like clove and myrrh, and physical brushing. You don't need a single bubble to get your teeth clean.

What's the difference between SLS-free and fluoride-free toothpaste?

These are separate choices. A toothpaste can be SLS-free and still contain fluoride, or both SLS-free and fluoride-free — like Real Skin Care's coconut oil toothpaste, which contains neither. Check the label for both if you want to avoid both.

Can SLS toothpaste cause canker sores?

The research suggests a real link. The PMC review by Sabri et al. found that SLS-containing toothpaste is associated with higher frequency and severity of recurrent aphthous stomatitis. For people who get canker sores regularly, switching to SLS-free is one of the simplest things to try first.

Is sodium lauroyl sarcosinate safe in toothpaste?

Yes. Sodium lauroyl sarcosinate is considered a safer alternative to SLS for oral use. It's gentler on the mucin layer, produces mild foam, and is used in several natural and sensitive-formula toothpastes. It's a reasonable middle ground if you want some foam without the irritation risk of SLS.

Oral-B Densify toothpaste does contain sodium lauryl sulfate. It appears in the inactive ingredients list. If you're looking for a remineralizing or enamel-support toothpaste without SLS, look for formulas built around hydroxyapatite instead.

The Bottom Line

SLS is a foaming agent, not a cleaning agent. For most people it's low risk. But for anyone prone to canker sores, dry mouth, or taste disruption, the clinical evidence is clear: SLS-free toothpaste produces better outcomes. The foam isn't doing you any favors — and for a meaningful number of people, it's actively making things worse.
