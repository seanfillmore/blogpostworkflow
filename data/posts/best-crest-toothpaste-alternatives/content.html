What to Look for in a Toothpaste Alternative

Check five things on the label: fluoride status, abrasivity, foaming agents, sweeteners and flavors, and glycerin. Fluoride has the strongest evidence for cavity prevention. The others mostly come down to comfort and personal preference.

  • Fluoride status: Fluoride toothpaste has strong evidence for preventing cavities. A large Cochrane review (Walsh et al., 2019) found it works, with higher concentrations helping more. If you skip fluoride, talk with your dentist about your cavity risk.
  • Abrasivity: This is how scratchy a paste is on tooth surfaces. Lower is gentler. The American Dental Association (ADA) notes that its Seal of Acceptance is a useful benchmark for safety and effectiveness. Many natural pastes don't carry it, so check the label closely.
  • Foaming agents: Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) makes toothpaste foam. Some people who get canker sores prefer to avoid it, though experiences vary. Read more on why SLS-free toothpaste matters.
  • Peroxide: A bleaching ingredient in some whitening pastes. It can bother sensitive teeth, so many people choose peroxide-free formulas.
  • Sweeteners and flavors: Look for plain-language ingredients. Stevia and essential oils are common natural options.
  • Glycerin: A thick, sweet liquid that keeps paste smooth. Nothing shows it harms teeth. Some people skip it for personal reasons, and our glycerin-free guide explains why.

If cavity protection is your top priority, a fluoride paste is the best-supported choice. Evidence: strong. The Cochrane review and the CDC both back fluoride for lowering tooth decay. 3. SLS-Free Gentle Paste — Best for Sensitive Mouths

An SLS-free paste is a reasonable choice if your mouth gets sore easily or you get canker sores. Evidence: mixed. A few small studies, such as Healy et al., 1999, suggest SLS may play a role in recurrent canker sores. But results aren't consistent. Switching may help some people and do nothing for others. For sensitive teeth, pick a low abrasivity toothpaste and brush softly. Spicy flavors like cinnamon bother some mouths, so go slowly. See our guide on toothpaste for sensitive teeth. 4. Hydroxyapatite Toothpaste — Best Fluoride-Free Enamel Support

Hydroxyapatite is a mineral that makes up much of your tooth enamel. Fluoride-free pastes use a lab-made version of it, and it's getting more popular. Evidence: promising but still emerging. Some systematic reviews and clinical trials report encouraging results, but there's far less long-term research than for fluoride. It shouldn't be treated as a replacement for fluoride. It's best for people who avoid fluoride and want an option to discuss with their dentist.

5. Peroxide-Free Whitening Paste — Best for Gentle Stain Removal

A peroxide-free whitening paste lifts surface stains from coffee, tea and daily life, but it can't change your tooth's natural color. Gentle polishing ingredients do the work, so choose low-abrasion formulas. Skip charcoal. A 2017 literature review in JADA (Brooks et al.) found no proof that charcoal pastes whiten or are safe, and some can be abrasive. Evidence for charcoal: weak. For more ideas, browse these natural whitening toothpaste alternatives. Whatever you pick, expect cleaner-looking teeth, not a new shade.

6. Tooth Powder — Best Low-Waste, Minimalist Option

Tooth powder is a dry, low-packaging alternative that suits people who want less plastic and a simple routine. The main caution is abrasivity. Powders can be gritty, so brush gently and check the ingredients. Homemade mixes heavy in baking soda are a mixed idea for enamel because it's easy to scrub too hard and hard to know how abrasive your blend is. The ADA recommends choosing pastes that meet abrasivity standards. We don't share DIY recipes for that reason.

7. Toothpaste Tablets — Best for Travel and Plastic-Free Routines

Toothpaste tablets are chewable pellets you crush with your teeth, then brush as usual. They're easy to pack, leak-proof and often come in plastic-free packaging. The tradeoff is less published research than for traditional pastes. Check whether a tablet contains fluoride, and read the full ingredient list. Teens often like them because they're compact and fun to try. Pair them with a dentist's guidance.

How to Choose the Right Alternative

Start with your main reason for switching, then match it to a type.

  • Cavity protection: Choose a fluoride paste (type 2). Watch for dyes or sweeteners if those bother you.
  • Sore mouth or canker sores: Choose an SLS-free paste (types 1 or 3). Watch for strong flavors.
  • Fluoride-free: Choose hydroxyapatite (type 4) or a natural paste (type 1). Watch for less research and talk with your dentist.
  • Surface stains: Choose a peroxide-free, low-abrasion paste (type 5). Watch for charcoal and harsh scrubbing.
  • Low waste or travel: Choose powder or tablets (types 6 and 7). Watch for abrasivity and fluoride status.

What the Research Says (and Doesn't)

Here's a quick scorecard. We keep the tone neutral because the science varies a lot by topic.

  • Fluoride and cavities: Strong. See the Cochrane review (2019) and the CDC.
  • SLS and canker sores: Mixed. Small studies exist, such as this set on PubMed, but results conflict.
  • Hydroxyapatite: Emerging, moderate at best. See this PubMed search. It is weaker than fluoride evidence.
  • Charcoal: Weak. The JADA review (2017) found no proof of whitening or safety.
  • Coconut oil: Weak to mixed. A small 2015 study (Peedikayil et al.) looked at gum health, but oil is not a substitute for toothpaste. See can you use coconut oil as toothpaste.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is natural toothpaste as effective as conventional?
It depends on what you mean by effective. Any paste cleans teeth when you brush well. But only fluoride pastes have strong proof for preventing cavities. Natural pastes are a fair choice for clean ingredients and comfort, so ask your dentist if you're at high cavity risk.

Do I need fluoride?
Fluoride has the strongest evidence for cavity prevention, so many dentists recommend it. Some people choose to avoid it. If you do, keep up with brushing, flossing and dental checkups, and talk with your dentist about your personal risk.

Will a peroxide-free paste whiten my teeth?
It can lift surface stains, so teeth may look cleaner. It won't change your natural tooth color, though. Only bleaching ingredients or professional treatments do that, and results vary. Be wary of any paste promising dramatic shade changes.

Is SLS-free toothpaste better for canker sores?
It may help some people, but the evidence is mixed. Many people with frequent canker sores like to try an SLS-free paste to see if it feels gentler on their mouth. Trying an SLS-free paste is low-risk. If sores keep coming back, see a dentist or doctor.

What is a safe toothpaste for teenagers or kids?
Look for age-appropriate pastes your dentist approves, with attention to fluoride, since growing teeth benefit from it. Little ones also shouldn't swallow paste. For milder options, see our SLS-free toothpaste for kids guide.
