Speaking as the person who made it

A customer can only report what happened to them. You can say why the bottle is the way it is — and that is a claim nobody can copy, contradict or reproduce. Three moves carry every line below, all three taken from the brand's own voice document.

1 · Name the cheaper ingredient you rejected

The animating question is already written down: if a cheaper substitute would do the same job, would we use it? Saying the substitute out loud — mineral oil, PEG emulsifier, refined palm oil, parabens — is more persuasive than any adjective, because it is checkable and it costs you something to say.

"We could make this lotion for a fraction of what it costs us. There's one ingredient we'd have to swap to do it."

2 · Lead with mechanism, end with cost

Explain why it works, then what that choice cost. Never the other way round — cost-first sounds like a complaint, mechanism-first sounds like expertise.

"Jojoba's structure is close enough to what your own skin makes that skin takes it in rather than wearing it."

3 · Own the trade-off. Don't apologise for it, and don't moralise about the alternative

The scent fades. The texture shifts batch to batch. It's thirty dollars. Each one is a downstream cost of a decision you'd make again — say so. And cheap substitutes exist for real reasons; naming them without sneering is what makes you sound like a formulator instead of a competitor.

"They're right, and I'm not going to argue with them. I'd rather tell you what's actually in the bottle."

"We" for the company, "I" for the moment. The brand voice is deliberately first-person plural and family-driven, not solo-founder. On camera that resolves cleanly: we decided, we pay more, we won't swap it — and I turned the box over, I read the comment, I can put a shirt on.

The one rule that decides whether these work

A hook earns the scroll-stop. It must not become the video's subject. After it lands, the body pivots onto the reasons people actually buy this lotion, and the hook's premise returns once at the end, if at all, as a small bonus.

That is why the hooks are grouped by which script they hand off to, not by theme. Three of the most tempting angles in this category — the ingredient count, the price, and "coconut oil clogs pores" — swallow a video whole if you let them keep talking. Pick a hook, shoot the script it points at, don't improvise the middle.

Nothing here says eczema, dermatitis, heals, treats, soothes or prevents. This is a cosmetic, and the most emotionally loaded problem in the category is the one thing it may not name. Every hook is built to be urgent without it.

The strongest asset in the review corpus is a mother's month-apart photographs of her daughter's skin. Do not use it. It is a disease claim in picture form, and it is the single most likely thing to get an account actioned.

The twenty hooks

Say each one cold, in the first two seconds, before anything else happens on screen. None of them resolves itself — that is deliberate. If you finish the thought inside the hook, you have made an abstract rather than a hook.

Hooks 1–7 → Script A The oil we didn't use

Pivots to: the carrier decision, and why it absorbs. This is the attribute customers volunteer more than any other — "absorbs quickly" in 37 reviews, "not greasy" in 38 — and you can explain it from the inside.

  1. 01

    "We could make this lotion for a fraction of what it costs us. There's one ingredient we'd have to swap to do it."

    We-paid-more · The brand's own animating question, said out loud

  2. 02

    "Every lotion that ever felt greasy on you had the same ingredient doing it, and it wasn't the one you think."

    Failed explanation · Withholds the carrier — mineral oil and petrolatum form the film — for the script to deliver

  3. 03

    "Watch my hand. I want to show you what an emulsifier actually does, because nobody explains this part."

    Mid-action, mechanism · Pure demo. The hook contains no claim at all

  4. 04

    "People ask why our lotion doesn't feel like the drugstore one. That's not an accident. It's a decision, and it cost us margin."

    Yes-opener · The signature construction: we paid more for X because Y

  5. 05

    "There's an ingredient we get asked to add every year. It would make this cheaper, more consistent and more shelf-stable. We keep saying no."

    Trade-off owned · Every one of those three adjectives is true of the substitute — which is what makes the refusal land

  6. 06

    "Somebody commented that coconut oil is comedogenic. They're right, and I'm not going to argue with them. I'd rather tell you what's actually in the bottle."

    Concede, don't argue · Comedogenic fear — 6 mentions. Conceding it costs nothing and buys the rest of the video

  7. 07

    "If coconut oil reliably breaks you out, don't buy our lotion. I'd rather say that now than take the order."

    Disqualify · From "who we're not for" — 5 mentions. Only the owner can turn down a sale on camera

Hooks 8–14 → Script B Six things, and why each one is there

Pivots to: every ingredient earning its place, and the ones you refused. Reading the ingredient list is what closes this sale — 5 mentions say so outright.

  1. 08

    "The average person puts a hundred and twelve different ingredients on their skin before leaving the house. I'm not going to tell you that's dangerous. I'm going to tell you what it makes impossible."

    Fact hook + refusal · EWG / Morning Consult, 2,200 US adults, 2023. Keep the "as many as" hedge, never frame it as safety

  2. 09

    "There are six things in this bottle and I can tell you why every one of them is in there. It's the sixth that people ask about."

    Count + authority · "the least number of ingredients possible, every one earning its place"

  3. 10

    "This company exists because I turned over a box in my own bathroom and read the warning on the back."

    Origin moment · Sean's real story — the toothpaste "do not swallow" moment. True, specific, unrepeatable by anyone else

  4. 11

    "Our family wanted products where we could confirm every single ingredient. That turned out to be much harder to buy than to make."

    Origin, withholds · Straight from the founder narrative's opening

  5. 12

    "Unscented and fragrance-free are not the same word. One of them means something was added, and we had to pick one."

    Conflict · Scent appears in ~26 reviews; 4 mentions of buying for someone scent-intolerant

  6. 13

    "We don't put the word natural on this bottle. There are products labelled natural with SLS in them — the word isn't regulated and it doesn't do any work."

    The natural trap · Competitor deliberately not named. The point survives without picking the fight

  7. 14

    "The texture of our lotion shifts batch to batch and we're not going to fix it. It costs us reviews and we're keeping it."

    Flaw first, owned · The variation is a downstream cost of using oil that hasn't been stripped — weld the flaw to the reason

Hooks 15–20 → Script C Where thirty dollars goes

Pivots to: the four swaps you refused, then the repurchase number. The ceiling in shoppers' heads is $15 — 4 mentions. This script answers that and nothing else.

  1. 15

    "Thirty dollars for a bottle of body lotion. I know exactly how that sounds. Give me forty seconds and I'll tell you where it goes."

    Yes-opener on price · Names the objection before the viewer can

  2. 16

    "We could make more money on this bottle tomorrow. There are four ingredients we'd swap, and most people wouldn't notice for a month."

    We-paid-more · All four substitutes are real and named in the script: refined coconut oil, mineral oil, PEG emulsifier, parabens

  3. 17

    "Out of everything our family makes, one product brings back more repeat customers than all the others put together. It wasn't the one we expected."

    One-thing · Measured: 29.2% first-time-buyer repurchase, more returning revenue than every other SKU combined

  4. 18

    "Nurses, chefs, dishwashers, massage therapists — you all send us the same message, and it changed what we recommend."

    Call-out by name · Occupational trigger — 4 mentions, including a massage therapist's review

  5. 19

    "Every October our inbox changes. Same message, different people, and it starts the week it gets cold."

    Mid-action, seasonal · Cold weather is when the search starts — 5 mentions. Bank these in September

  6. 20

    "A customer wrote that ours is THE moisturizer for Wisconsin winters for her whole family. I didn't write that line, and I couldn't have."

    Borrowed verbatim · Real review, quoted exactly. Put it on screen while you say it

The three scripts

Each runs 40–50 seconds spoken. Beat numbers are cut points, not sentences. Say them plainly — the copy is already doing the work, and pushing the delivery is what makes it read as an ad rather than as the person who made it.

Script AThe oil we didn't use

Shoot this one first. It argues the attribute customers volunteer most, and it is the only script that answers the drugstore buyer and the burned natural-products buyer in the same breath.

1

"Most lotion is mostly water and a thickener. That's why you can still feel it on your skin an hour later — it never went anywhere."

2

"The cheap way to fix that is mineral oil, or petrolatum. A fraction of the cost, perfectly uniform every batch, and it would do very good things for our margins."

3

"It also sits on top of your skin. That film is the thing you're feeling. It's extremely good at making a lotion seem like it's working."

4

"We use jojoba instead. Jojoba is technically a wax ester, and its structure is close enough to what your own skin already makes that skin takes it in rather than wearing it."

5

"That's the whole difference. It was never the coconut oil. It's the carrier."

6

"Thirty seconds, and I can put a shirt on."

7

"If a cheaper ingredient did the same job, we'd use it. That one doesn't."

≈ 45 sec · Beat 6 is the demo — one unbroken take or it proves nothing · Beat 7 is the brand's own line, don't reword it

Script BSix things, and why each one is there

The credibility play. Beat 2 is the most important line in this document — refusing to make the claim the viewer is braced for is what makes everything after it believable.

1

"The average adult uses twelve personal care products a day, made with as many as a hundred and twelve different ingredients. That's EWG and Morning Consult, twenty-two hundred US adults, 2023."

2

"I'm not going to tell you that's dangerous. I don't know that, and neither does anybody selling you the alternative."

3

"What I'll tell you is that our family couldn't verify a hundred and twelve of anything. That's the actual reason this company exists."

4

"So there are six. Purified spring water. Organic virgin coconut oil. Organic jojoba. A plant-based emulsifying wax. Organic grapefruit seed extract. Organic red palm oil."

5

"The wax is the one worth explaining. The industry standard is a PEG emulsifier — cheaper, more shelf-stable, easier to formulate with. We use a plant-derived one that costs us more to do the identical job."

6

"And the red palm oil is unrefined, which is why it has a colour. Refined palm oil is bleached white, and the antioxidants come out with the colour. If we used that, there'd be no reason to have palm oil in here at all."

7

"Six things. You can read the whole label in about four seconds. That was the brief."

≈ 50 sec · Beat 4 read straight off the bottle, on camera, no cuts · Beat 6 needs the bottle in frame so the colour is visible

Script CWhere thirty dollars goes

Answers price by way of formulation rather than by way of discount. Pair only with hooks 15–20.

1

"Thirty dollars for body lotion. The number in most people's head is fifteen, so let me do this properly."

2

"Most lotion is mostly water. You are paying for dilution, and a thickener that makes it feel like more than it is."

3

"We could get this bottle cheaper tomorrow. Refined coconut oil instead of cold-pressed. Mineral oil instead of jojoba. A PEG emulsifier. Parabens instead of grapefruit seed extract."

4

"Every one of those is cheaper, more consistent and more shelf-stable than what we use. Most people wouldn't notice for a month."

5

"We didn't make those swaps. This is a premium product and we're not going to compromise that for margin."

6

"Here's the number I actually care about. Of everyone who bought this as their first order, twenty-nine percent came back and bought it again — the highest of anything we make."

7

"And you get thirty days. If you don't like it, email us and we refund you. You don't send it back."

≈ 48 sec · Beat 3 is a list — put the four swaps on screen as you say them · Beat 6 is the payoff, hold on your face not the bottle

The three closes

One ask, at the end, after the payoff — never before it. Say it as an instruction rather than an invitation: "go and get it" outperforms "you can learn more", because it is the only sentence in the video that tells the viewer what to do.

Close 1The plain ask

Default. Use whenever the body has already done the persuading — which, shot as written, it has.

"We make it, we ship it, it's on realskincare.com. Pure Unscented if you react to things, Coconut Breeze if you don't. Go and get it."

Close 2The risk reversal

Pair with the price hooks (15–17) and with any cold first-touch video. The only close that answers "what if it doesn't work on my skin" — the objection sitting under most of the others.

"Take thirty days with it. If you don't like it, email us and we'll refund you — and you don't have to send it back. We'd rather do that than keep an order you regret. Link's in my bio."

Close 3The first-order nudge

For videos reaching people who have never bought. Removes the last friction without discounting the product, and the lotion clears the threshold on its own so there's no upsell to explain.

"If it's your first order, use SHIPFREE at checkout — shipping's free over twenty-five dollars and this is thirty, so you're covered. realskincare.com, link's in the bio."

The vocabulary tension, and how these scripts resolve it

The voice document says teach the customer real words — cold-pressed virgin, wax ester, lauric acid. The claim audit says buyers never use them: cold-pressed appears 20 times in our copy and 0 times in 390 reviews. Both are right, and the resolution is an order, not a winner. Hook in their word, body in ours. Every hook above is in plain customer language; the vocabulary arrives in the script, as the proof, once they have a reason to want it.

One thing to fix outside this document. data/brand/founder-narrative.md — which pdp-builder reads as its exemplar tone for the per-product "From Sean" block — contains the sentence "the person whose sensitive skin or sensitive teeth started to heal a few weeks after switching."

"Heal" is blocking-tier therapeutic language. It has not shipped anywhere I can find, but it is seeding the tone for a generator that writes live product copy, and the health gate does not read that file. Worth a one-line edit.

Cadence

Roughly ten value posts to one ask post. Twenty hooks against three scripts is sixty shootable combinations — a month of daily posting without repeating a pairing. Repeat the pairings that land rather than reaching for a new hook; most of the audience missed the first one.
