## Why 90 days

Your skin's outer layer renews on roughly a 28-day cycle. Most dermatologists suggest giving a new routine 6–12 weeks before judging it. The most common reason a routine fails isn't the product — it's that people run out and stop before their skin catches up. Your reset is sized so that never happens.

Here is what that figure means for you. The skin you are looking at today was built over the last month, under whatever you were doing last month. Nothing you apply this morning can change it. What it can change is the skin forming underneath — the layer you will be looking at in four weeks.

So the first month is not the result. The first month is the input. That is why this is a 90-day program and not a 30-day one: three renewal cycles is the first point at which what you see was made entirely under the new routine.

### The two clocks

There are two things happening at different speeds, and confusing them is what makes people quit.

- **Surface comfort** moves fast. Tightness after a shower, the itchy feeling in dry air, skin that feels like it needs something — this often eases within days. Real, but not the change you paid for.
- **Structural change** moves slowly. Rough patches softening and staying soft, hands that stop cracking in the same spot every winter, skin that still feels fine on day three of a cold snap — that runs on the renewal clock.

Almost everyone gets the first clock in week one, watches the second refuse to move in week two, and concludes it has stopped working. It has not. The two clocks were never running together.

:::callout title="The honest version"
This is a moisturizer. It is not doing anything clever or medical. What it is doing is putting fat and water on skin twice a day, every day, for long enough that the skin being built underneath is built in better conditions than the skin you have now. The only variable you control is the *every day* part.

## Your daily routine

Two steps. Consistency beats complexity every time.

:::image product=coconut-moisturizer image=AMZ-RealSkinCare-BodyCreamHeroImages_IB_3000x1497_01.jpg size=full caption="Body Cream, 4 oz. Denser than the lotion, and meant for the places that need more than the lotion gives."

### Every morning — Body Lotion

Apply to clean skin after your shower, or whenever skin feels tight. Massage in fully; it should absorb within minutes with no sticky film.

Cover more than you think you need to. Most people do their arms and forearms and stop. Shins, the outsides of the upper arms and the backs of the hands are where dryness actually lives, and they are the areas people habitually skip. You have three bottles; being generous is the intended use.

### Every night — Body Cream

Warm a small amount between your fingers and apply to dry patches before bed — elbows, knees, hands, feet. It is denser than the lotion and it is meant to sit where you put it. A little goes a long way.

Night is when this does its best work, because you are not washing your hands or rubbing against a desk for the next seven hours. If you only manage one thing in a day, make it this one.

:::callout title="The one rule that matters"
Apply within 3 minutes of getting out of the shower, while skin is still slightly damp. Damp skin holds moisture, and this single habit does more than any extra product ever could.

If you take nothing else from this guide, take this. It costs nothing, adds no time, and it is the difference between moisturizing skin and moisturizing a towel.
:::

### How much is enough

| Area | Roughly | You are aiming for |
|---|---|---|
| Full body, morning | A generous coin-sized pool in the palm, twice | A thin sheen that disappears in 2–3 minutes |
| Hands, after washing | A pea | Absorbed before you pick up your phone |
| Elbows, knees, heels, night | A fingertip's worth, per spot | Slightly tacky when you get into bed |

The overnight patches are meant to stay slightly tacky. That is not a failure to absorb — it is a heavier cream doing what a heavier cream is for, and it will be gone by morning.

## The three phases

Ninety days is too long to think about at once. Treat it as three stretches with different jobs.

| Weeks | Phase | The job | What you will notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Reset | Building the habit, stopping the loss | Fast comfort, then an apparent plateau |
| 3–6 | Rebuild | The first fully new skin arrives | Rough patches softening, less reapplying |
| 7–12 | Hold | Proving it holds under stress | Skin that stays fine on bad-weather days |

### Weeks 1–2 — Reset

The job in this phase is not results. It is attendance. Get to day 14 without missing more than two or three applications; everything else is secondary.

What normally happens: the first two or three days feel noticeably better. Skin stops feeling tight after showers, hands feel less papery. Then around day six or seven, that improvement stops advancing and simply sits there.

### What week 2 actually feels like

This is the point where most people quit, so it is worth describing precisely.

Week 2 feels like nothing is happening. Specifically:

- The quick early improvement has stopped improving. You got a jump in days 1–4 and now day 11 feels the same as day 8.
- Your rough patches — heels, elbows, the backs of the hands — look exactly as they did on day 1. Because they do. They have not been replaced yet.
- The novelty is gone. Applying it now feels like a chore rather than a change.
- If the weather turned cold in the meantime, you may even feel slightly worse than you did on day 3, and read that as the product losing effect.

None of this is a signal. It is exactly what the 28-day cycle predicts: the surface improvement is spent, and the structural improvement has not surfaced yet. You are in the gap between the two clocks.

:::callout title="If you are going to quit, you will do it this week"
The only thing that matters in week 2 is that you keep applying. Not enthusiastically — just reliably. Tick the boxes, write "nothing" in the notes column if that is honest, and carry on. Week 3 is where the tracker starts being interesting.
:::

### Weeks 3–6 — Rebuild

Around week 4 you cross the first full renewal cycle. The skin you are looking at was largely built while you were doing this.

What usually changes here, in rough order of how commonly people notice it:

- You reapply less. The clearest early signal is not softer skin, it is *needing it less often* — the mid-afternoon reach for hand cream stops happening.
- Rough patches get smaller before they get smooth. Heels and elbows tend to shrink at the edges rather than transform.
- Skin recovers faster from insults — a hot shower, a windy walk, a day of hand-washing — instead of taking two days to settle.

This is also the phase to re-read your own week 1 notes. People consistently under-remember how bad things were three weeks ago. That is what the notes column is for.

### Weeks 7–12 — Hold

The last phase asks a different question: does it hold?

Anything can look good for a two weeks. What weeks 7 to 12 test is whether the improvement survives normal life — cold weather, travel, a stretch where you were busy and sloppy about the night step.

Expect the tracker to get less dramatic and more flat here, in a good way. Weekly scores that sit at the same number for five weeks running are not stagnation at this stage; they are the result. The goal of a maintenance phase is boredom.

If you have a bad week here — most people do — what matters is how fast you come back. In week 2, three missed days cost you a two weeks; in week 10, a few days.

## Take your day-0 photo before you start

Do this today, before your first application.

You will not believe your own memory in twelve weeks. Skin change is gradual enough that you re-baseline continuously — a week from now, today's skin will feel like it was always that way, and by week 8 you will not be able to remember what your heels looked like in week 1. A photo is the only honest record you will have.

### How to take a photo you can actually compare

The value is entirely in the *comparable* part. A better-lit photo on day 90 proves nothing. Five rules:

1. **Same window, same light.** Stand near a window with daylight, facing it. Not a bathroom overhead light — it changes with the bulb and the time of year.
2. **Same time of day.** Morning, before you apply anything, is the most consistent. Skin looks different at 9 pm than 9 am.
3. **Same spot, framed the same way.** Pick the one or two areas that bother you most — the back of a hand, a heel, an elbow. Get close enough that the patch fills most of the frame, and include something for scale, like a knuckle or a wrist crease.
4. **No flash.** Flash flattens texture, which is the exact thing you are trying to see.
5. **No filter, no portrait mode, no edit.** A softened photo is a photo you cannot compare to anything.

Then take the same photo at day 30, day 60 and day 90. Four in total. Put them in one named album on your phone now — the day-60 photo does not get taken if you have to hunt for the day-0 one to remember the angle.

## Your 12-week tracker

This is the page to print and put where you get dressed. Not in a drawer — on the wall, on the mirror, inside a wardrobe door. Visibility does most of the work here.

How to use it:

- **The seven boxes** are the days of the week. Tick a box on a day you completed both steps. Half a day counts as half — put a slash. Do not tidy it up or fill it in retrospectively; a partly blank row is more useful than a neat lie.
- **Skin 1–5** is one number at the end of each week. 1 is tight, rough and flaky. 5 is comfortable all day without thinking about it. Score how the week felt overall, not how today feels.
- **Notes** is the most valuable column and the one everyone leaves empty. Two or three words is plenty: *heels still rough*, *no reapply at work*, *cold snap*, *forgot Thu/Fri*. In week 6 these notes are the only way to know whether week 2 was actually as bad as you now think it was.

One instruction matters more than the rest: **do not skip a week's score because it was a bad week.** A blank week is invisible in a pattern, and a visible bad week is what makes the good week after it legible.

Read the column, not the cell. Any single day is noise. What you are looking for is the 1–5 column drifting upward across weeks 3 to 8, and — the real prize — sitting still across weeks 8 to 12.

## Troubleshooting

Five things go wrong. All five are normal, and four of them are fixed by using less product, not more.

### Skin feels greasy

Almost always dosage rather than formula. This is an oil-based lotion: it covers the same area on less than a water-and-silicone drugstore lotion, and the first week of a switch is when people over-apply out of habit.

Halve the amount for three days, and apply to damp skin rather than dry — damp skin spreads it much further. If you are using the cream in the morning as well as at night, stop; it is too dense for a full-body morning application.

### It won't absorb

Two likely causes. The first is fully dry skin, which sits under the lotion rather than taking it in. Go back to the three-minute rule.

The second is a cold room. Coconut oil firms up below 76°F, so on a cold morning you are spreading a solid. Rub the amount between your palms for ten seconds to warm it first. That one habit fixes most of the winter complaints we get.

### The product went solid in the cold

It is meant to. Cold-pressed coconut oil sets below 76°F, so both the lotion and the cream will firm up, get grainy, or separate slightly in a cold house or a cold delivery van.

Nothing has spoiled. Stand the bottle in warm — not hot — water for a few minutes, or hold the amount you need in your hands until it softens. Cream in a jar can be stirred back together with a clean finger. A product that never changes texture in the cold is telling you something about what is in it.

:::callout title="Why we don't fix this"
We could add the stabilisers that would keep it identical at every temperature. That would mean a longer ingredient list to do a job that a warm palm does for free. The texture change is a side effect of the formula being what it says it is.
:::

### A patch got worse

First, be sure it is worse rather than newly noticed — people start looking closely at their skin in week 1 and find things that were always there.

If an area is genuinely more irritated, red or itchy after starting: stop applying there, keep going everywhere else, and give it a week. If it settles, reintroduce that area with a smaller amount every other day. If it does not settle, stop and talk to a doctor or a dermatologist. We are not going to diagnose anything from here, and a moisturizer should not be the reason you delay asking someone qualified.

Worth knowing: this is a coconut-oil-based line, and coconut oil does not agree with everyone. If your skin is one of the ones that reacts, more time will not change that — and we would rather you find out in week 1 than week 9.

### The scent is stronger or weaker than expected

Both directions are common, and both come from the same cause: the scent is essential oil, not fragrance.

If it seems weak, that is the design. Essential oils are volatile — noticeable on application and largely gone within the hour. There is no fixative in here to make them last, so this will never smell like a perfumed body lotion. If it seems strong, it will fade fast; apply less and give it fifteen minutes before deciding.

If it smells slightly different between bottles, that is a real-plant ingredient behaving like a crop. Batch, harvest and season all move the scent a little. Pure Unscented contains no essential oils at all.

## When you'll run out, and what to do about it

We would rather give you the real numbers than a comfortable answer.

| What you have | Measured use rate | How long it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Body Lotion — 3 × 8 oz | About 38 days per bottle | Roughly 114 days — past day 90 with room to spare |
| Body Cream — 3 × 4 oz jars | About 28 days per jar at full daily use | Roughly 84 days at that rate — most of the 90 |

These come from measured reorder gaps, not estimates. Read them as an upper bound: people who use more, or have more area to cover, will hit these numbers sooner.

### What that actually means

**Lotion: you are covered.** Three bottles at the measured rate is about 114 days against a 90-day program. You do not need to ration it, and rationing is the most common way people under-apply.

**Cream: close, and we would rather give you the real number.** At the rate someone uses a jar as their main daily moisturizer, 4 oz is about a month — so three jars is roughly 84 days against a 90-day program. That figure is an upper bound taken from reorder gaps, and it assumes full daily use.

The routine in this guide is built so that is fine. The cream is a night-and-patches product — elbows, knees, heels, hands — not a full-body one. Used that way, a fingertip per spot, a jar stretches well past its 28-day full-use rate, and three of them will see you past day 90. If you use it all over instead, expect the last stretch to be tight.

:::callout title="The one to reorder first"
If you only replace one thing, replace the cream. The lotion is the volume product and you have plenty; the cream is the one that runs closest to the line, and the one whose absence you notice at night.
:::

### Setting the reminder now

Put two reminders in your phone now:

- **Day 25** — check how much cream is left. Early enough that a reorder arrives before the jar is empty rather than a week after.
- **Day 80** — decide what happens after day 90.

The gap between running out and reordering is where 90-day routines quietly become 60-day routines. A calendar entry closes it.

## After day 90

Look at your four photos side by side before you look at anything else. Then read your tracker from week 1 forward — not the ticks, the notes column. Then answer one question: is this better than day 0?

If the answer is yes, maintenance is cheaper than the reset was. Most people settle into the morning lotion daily and the night cream two or three times a week on the patches that still want it — a considerably slower burn rate than the program you just finished.

If the answer is no, that is worth knowing too, and it took twelve weeks rather than twelve days to find out. Skin that has had a fair trial of oil-based moisturizing twice daily for three months and is unchanged is telling you something no further amount of the same will fix. We would rather you found that out properly than kept buying on hope.

Either way: you now have a record. Almost nobody does.
