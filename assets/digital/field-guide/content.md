## How to use this guide

You already own the products. This is the reference that goes with them — the part that will not fit on a bottle. It covers why the oil in your bottle is made the way it is, how to read any label fast enough to do it in a shop aisle, what to change when the weather does, and which product suits which problem. The ingredient lists at the back are generated from our product records, so they cannot drift out of date.

Nothing here is medical advice. These are moisturisers, soaps and a toothpaste. If a clinician has given you a routine, this guide is not a reason to change it.

:::callout title="The short version, if you read nothing else"
Buy oil that has not been refined. Read the first five ingredients and the last five; ignore the middle. Assume anything labelled unscented may contain something added to make it smell like nothing. Warm the product in your hands in winter, and apply within three minutes of a shower.
:::

## Why cold-pressed coconut oil

Not all coconut oil is equal. The difference comes down to one word: refining.

:::image product=coconut-moisturizer index=0 size=full caption="Cold-pressed and virgin — a real, whole ingredient, with the faint coconut scent to prove it."

Cold-pressed and virgin oil is pressed from fresh coconut without heat or solvents, keeping its lauric acid, vitamin E and antioxidants intact. Refined or fractionated oil is bleached, deodorised or chemically split for shelf life — cheaper and more stable, but stripped of most of what made it worth putting on skin.

The three grades sit on the same shelf and often carry the same words on the front. Here is what separates them.

| | Cold-pressed / virgin | Refined (RBD) | Fractionated (MCT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it is made | Pressed from fresh coconut meat, no heat, no solvents | Pressed from dried copra, then refined, bleached, deodorised | Heated and split to remove the lauric acid |
| Smell | Faintly of coconut | None — removed on purpose | None |
| Lauric acid | Intact | Largely intact, heat-processed | Removed by design |
| Vitamin E, antioxidants | Retained | Substantially reduced | Minimal |
| Solid below 76F | Yes | Yes | No — stays liquid |
| Shelf life | Shorter | Longer | Longest |
| Typical cost | Highest | Low | Moderate |

Fractionated oil is not a fraud. It is a useful carrier oil and never solidifies, which is why roll-on perfumes use it. It is simply not the same material: what makes it convenient is what removed the reason people buy coconut oil.

Refined oil is the one worth watching. Because refining removes the smell, a refined-oil product can be sold as fragrance-free with no effort, and it feels lighter on skin. Both read as advantages on a product page. Neither is a sign of quality.

:::callout title="A quick note on texture"
Cold-pressed coconut oil firms up below 76F. If your lotion or cream feels solid in winter, that is the real ingredient behaving normally. Warm a little between your palms first.
:::

### How to tell what you have

A jar of plain coconut oil can be checked at home with no equipment.

1. Smell it. Cold-pressed oil smells faintly of coconut. If it smells of nothing, it has been deodorised.
2. Put it somewhere cool. Below 76F, cold-pressed and refined oil both go solid and white. Fractionated oil stays clear and liquid at any temperature.
3. Read the label. Virgin, extra-virgin and cold-pressed are the words to look for. RBD stands for refined, bleached and deodorised.

None of this works on a finished lotion, where water changes the texture. There you are relying on the label — which is the next section.

## The irritant reference

This is the page worth keeping: the ingredients sensitive-skin buyers most often tell us they are avoiding, what they are actually called on the back of the pack, and where they turn up.

We make no safety claim about any of them. Most are common, legal and fine for most people. The useful part is the alias — an ingredient you are avoiding is easy to spot under its own name and nearly invisible under its INCI name.

| What people avoid | What it says on the label | Where it usually turns up |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic fragrance | Fragrance, Parfum, Aroma | Almost everything — lotion, soap, deodorant |
| Fragrance allergens | Limonene, Linalool, Geraniol, Citral, Eugenol | The end of any scented list, natural ones included |
| Sulfates | Sodium Lauryl Sulfate, SLS, Sodium Laureth Sulfate, SLES | Body wash, shampoo, hand soap, toothpaste |
| Isothiazolinone preservatives | Methylisothiazolinone, MI, Methylchloroisothiazolinone, MCI | Liquid soap, body wash, wipes, anything water-based |
| Formaldehyde releasers | DMDM Hydantoin, Imidazolidinyl Urea, Diazolidinyl Urea, Quaternium-15 | Lotion, liquid soap, shampoo |
| Parabens | Methylparaben, Propylparaben, Butylparaben | Lotion, cream, makeup |
| Drying alcohols | Alcohol Denat., SD Alcohol 40, Denatured Alcohol | Toner, spray deodorant, gel, quick-dry lotion |
| Propylene glycol | Propylene Glycol, PG | Deodorant, lotion, hair product |
| Lanolin | Lanolin, Lanolin Alcohol, Wool Wax | Lip balm, nipple cream, heavy ointment |
| Antiperspirant actives | Aluminum Zirconium Tetrachlorohydrex GLY, Aluminum Chlorohydrate | Antiperspirant — what makes it one |
| Foaming amphoterics | Cocamidopropyl Betaine, Coco-Betaine | Foaming hand soap, body wash, baby wash |
| Bicarbonate | Sodium Bicarbonate, Baking Soda | Natural deodorant, natural toothpaste |

### Two entries on that list are in our products

Being straight about this matters more than the rest of the page.

:::image product=coconut-oil-deodorant index=1 size=half caption="A deodorant, not an antiperspirant. It contains no aluminium salts, because that is the ingredient class that stops sweat."

**Sodium bicarbonate is in our deodorant.** Baking soda is what makes a natural deodorant work — it shifts underarm pH so odour-causing bacteria are less comfortable. It is also the most common reason people react to natural deodorants, usually as redness or stinging in the first fortnight, and more often on freshly shaved skin. If baking soda is on your avoid list, our deodorant is not for you, and we would rather you learned that here than from your armpit.

**Essential oils are fragrance.** Naturally derived fragrance is still fragrance, and the compounds that make people react to scented products — limonene, linalool, geraniol, citral — occur in citrus, lavender and rose oils as a matter of botany. If you are fragrance-reactive, buy Pure Unscented. That variant contains no essential oils at all, which is why it exists.

## How to read any label in 30 seconds

Not a list to memorise. A method, ordered so the most information comes for the least reading.

:::image product=coconut-lotion index=2 size=half caption="Everything a label can tell you is on the back. The front is advertising."

**1. Read the first five ingredients only.** Ingredients are listed in descending order of how much is in the product, so the first five are effectively the formula. If what the front of the pack is selling — shea, aloe, coconut, oat — is not in the first five, it is a garnish.

**2. Then read the last five.** Preservatives, fragrance and fragrance allergens live at the end — the isothiazolinones, the formaldehyde releasers, the parabens and the word Fragrance. Two glances, ten seconds, both ends of the formula.

**3. Ignore the middle** unless you are hunting one specific ingredient. The middle is emulsifiers, thickeners and pH adjusters, much the same in every lotion on the shelf.

**4. Know where the ordering rule stops.** Ingredients above one percent must be listed in descending order. Below one percent they may be listed in any order, so position tells you nothing down there.

**5. Find the fragrance.** In the US, Fragrance or Parfum can stand for a blend of many components that need not be disclosed individually, because the blend is treated as trade-secret information. One word hides a great deal, and if you react you cannot tell to what.

**6. Check what unscented actually means.** This is the one that catches people.

| Wording | What it is allowed to contain |
|---|---|
| Fragrance-free | Nothing added for the purpose of scent |
| Unscented | Not a regulated term. May contain a masking fragrance added to cover the smell of the base |
| No synthetic fragrance | Essential oils, absolutes and other natural aromatics |
| Naturally scented | Fragrance, from plants. Still fragrance, still contains allergens |
| Hypoallergenic | Not a regulated term in the US. No testing standard behind it |

Unscented is the trap. A product can be made to smell of nothing by adding something, and the front of the pack reads identically either way. The only way to tell is the list: a genuinely fragrance-free product has no fragrance entry at all.

:::callout title="Where our own labels sit on this"
Pure Unscented across our range means no essential oils and no masking fragrance. What you can still smell is the base material: cold-pressed coconut oil has a faint natural scent, and we do not deodorise it out, because deodorising is a refining step. That faint coconut is the ingredient, not something added.
:::

**7. Count the list.** Crude, but fast. A short list is easier to verify and leaves fewer suspects when something does not agree with you. Not a quality guarantee — just the one signal readable in a second.

:::pagebreak

## Winter, summer, and the 76F problem

Coconut oil melts around 76F, unusually close to room temperature. Almost every seasonal quirk comes from that one number.

### Below 76F

:::image product=coconut-moisturizer index=3 size=half caption="The jar in summer is soft and scoops easily. The same jar in January will want a moment between your palms first."

The oil in your lotion and cream firms up. In a jar this is obvious; the cream stiffens and you have to dig at it. In a bottle it shows up as the lotion pouring slowly and feeling denser going on. Nothing has gone wrong and nothing has separated. Work a small amount between your palms for five to ten seconds and it will loosen.

- Store it in the warmest room you reasonably can. A bathroom shelf beats a hall cupboard.
- Warm it in your hands, not in a microwave.
- Expect to want the cream more than the lotion. Cold indoor air is when the heavier format earns its place.
- Apply within three minutes of a shower. This matters more now than at any other time of year.

### Above 76F

:::image product=coconut-oil-lip-balm index=2 size=half caption="Lip balm is the one heat catches out. Stand a softened tube upright and let it set before twisting it up again."

The oil is fully liquid. The lotion pours faster and sinks in more quickly, and the cream softens noticeably. In a hot car it can go completely liquid — reversible, though the surface may set unevenly as it cools.

- Use less than you think. Warm skin absorbs more readily.
- Do not leave the jar in a car or on a sunny windowsill.
- If you sweat, wash and reapply rather than layering on top.

### What changes about the routine

| | Colder months | Warmer months |
|---|---|---|
| Main product | Cream, or lotion then cream on bad patches | Lotion alone is usually enough |
| Frequency | Twice daily, plus after every hand wash | Once daily for most people |
| Matters most on | Hands, shins, elbows, heels, knuckles | Feet, forearms, anything exposed |
| Shower habit | Cooler, shorter, apply within three minutes | Same rule, easier to keep |
| Amount | More, more often | Less, spread further |
| Usual failure | Waiting until skin has already cracked | Skipping days because nothing feels tight |

Both soaps are the same material: coconut oil turned into soap, with the essential oil, if any, added afterwards. They differ only in dilution. The bar is for the shower; the pump is for a sink where hands are washed constantly and a bar would sit in water all day.

Neither contains SLS, cocamidopropyl betaine or an isothiazolinone preservative — most of what people avoid in a hand wash. Both clean thoroughly rather than gently, so if your hands are splitting, follow with the cream rather than expecting soap to moisturise.

### The toothpaste

:::image product=coconut-oil-toothpaste index=1 size=half caption="Coconut oil, baking soda and myrrh. No SLS, and no fluoride."

The odd one out in the range, and the one most people do not know exists. Coconut oil, baking soda, xanthan gum, wildcrafted myrrh powder and stevia, flavoured with essential oils. It is fluoride-free.

Two things are worth knowing before the first brush. It does not foam, because the ingredient that makes toothpaste foam is SLS. And it is not sweet the way a supermarket tube is — the stevia takes the edge off the baking soda, nothing more. Both stop registering after about a week.

## Honest answers to the usual objections

The things people actually say to us, including the ones we would rather they did not.

### Coconut oil is comedogenic. Will it break me out?

For some people on the face, yes. Coconut oil sits high on comedogenic rating scales; those scales are old and were not built on human facial skin, and results vary by person and by body part. What is consistent is that reactions are far more common on the face than the body.

This is a body care range. If coconut oil has broken you out before, keep it off your face. If you react to it anywhere, this is not the range for you, and cold-pressing does not change that.

### Natural oils never absorb. Will I feel greasy?

The lotion is not oil. Water is the first ingredient and it is an emulsion, which is why it sinks in rather than sitting on top the way straight coconut oil does. Some people do report a slight sheen for a few minutes, usually after applying more than they needed. Use less, and apply to damp skin. The cream is deliberately heavier, which is what makes it work on heels and knuckles.

### The scent is lighter than I expected, or it faded

Accurate, and a trade-off we chose. Scent comes from essential oils at low inclusion with no synthetic fixatives to hold them, so it is light and fades within the hour. All-day scent is achievable, but it needs the fragrance chemistry this guide is about avoiding.

### The bar is small and the bottle runs out faster than I expected

Our most common complaint, and a fair one. There are no hardeners or fillers in a one-ingredient soap, and fillers are much of why a supermarket bar lasts longer for less. On the lotion, your reset ships three bottles precisely because one is not enough for ninety days of using it as generously as you should.

### My dermatologist recommends a drugstore brand

Then keep using it. A clinician who has looked at your skin has more information than a guide can. Many people use both.

### Is it safe for children?

Parents do use it across the household, and we will not make a claim about any particular child's skin. Do what you would with any new product: a small amount on one patch, wait a day, and choose Pure Unscented if in doubt.

### Why is there no fluoride in the toothpaste?

It is formulated without it. That is a statement about what is in the tube, not a position on fluoride, and your dentist is the right person to ask about your own teeth.
